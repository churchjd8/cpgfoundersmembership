Hope is not a strategy.

I've sat across from so many founders who say things like "we're hoping the Whole Foods buyer calls back" or "we're hoping this next quarter turns things around."

Hoping is what you do when you've stopped planning.

At Suja, Coca-Cola passed on acquiring us. I had a $100 million revenue company with less than $100K in the bank some weeks. $40 million in debt coming due. Burning over $10 million a year.

Hope wasn't going to fix that.

So I opened the spreadsheet. Meticulous weekly cash projections. Cut $4 million in marketing (sorry marketing team). Killed every low-margin SKU. Raw material cost reduction program. Uncomfortable conversations with every single department. Not fun. Necessary.

But here's what I don't think gets talked about enough. Even when you know what to do operationally, doing it alone is a different game. I had a board that held me accountable. Advisors who'd been through similar messes and pressure-tested my thinking. A team I trusted enough to have the hard conversations with. Without those people pushing back, challenging assumptions, telling me what I didn't want to hear, I would've moved slower and probably made worse calls.

We went from losing $10 million to generating $3 million in positive EBITDA. In one year. Not because of one person. Because the right people were in the room and we stopped hoping and started operating.

You don't have to do this alone. (I tried that in my 30s. Got the gray hair and the therapy bills to prove it.)

If something in your business is broken right now, stop hoping it resolves itself. Open the spreadsheet. Make the call. Do the math. And surround yourself with people who'll tell you the truth, not just what you want to hear.

Hope is a wonderful human quality. It's a terrible business plan.
