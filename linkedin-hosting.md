## 1. "Velocity" — Wed May 20
**Pillar: Distribution / Deep**

We paid nearly a million dollars in slotting fees to get Rowdy Energy into Albertsons.

A million dollars!! JUST to get on shelf. Over $100 per SKU per store, when the average is a free fill that's typically $15 to $20.

Eight months later, they discontinued us.

The slotting check? Gone. You don't get that back.

The company gross margin impact? Negative 10 points. Awful.

The painful part is we saw the velocity numbers creeping along for months. We told ourselves a story about the brand needing more time and more awareness. We hoped the numbers would turn.

I've said it before, but hope is not a strategy.

Here's the truth most founders never get told out loud: getting distribution is the easy part. Holding it is the whole game.

There's a chart in the Spins data they always show — dollar growth in your segment over different time frames. Founders celebrate things like "we're the third fastest growing brand in Spins." Anyone in the know understands that if you started with no or low distribution in those stores, that's pipeline fill. It means nothing about success on shelf.

If the growth is coming from rising weekly unit velocity per SKU, you're winning. The rest just makes uninformed angel investors feel like you're killing it... until you're not.

Buyers don't care about your door count. They care about velocity. Dollars per point of distribution. How fast your product moves off shelf compared to every other "challenger" brand fighting for the same space.

Benchmarks I use against category median (excluding the large legacy brands — you're really fighting the other challengers in your segment):

- Above 60%: push for expansion. If your % of segment sales is above your % of SKUs on the shelf, you've earned more SKUs. Go take them.
- 45-60%: solid. Stay the course. Above 50%, push for swaps to help grow the retailer's category.
- 30-45%: you need a real plan or you won't make the next reset.
- Below 20%: something is seriously wrong.

Most retailers will give you 12 months on shelf to prove yourself. But they're making decisions for the next year's set at around 7-9 months in, depending on the retailer.

"Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains."

Distribution gets you on shelf. Velocity earns your spot.

If you're staring at numbers that don't add up, don't go quiet. Walk into the buyer with a plan before they walk to you with bad news.

## 2. "Hire Slow, Fire Fast" — Fri May 22
**Pillar: People / Operational Wisdom**

Hire slow. Fire fast.

You've heard it before. Everyone nods. Almost nobody does it.

I know because I've made the wrong hire more times than I want to count. And every single time, I knew something was off in the first few weeks. I just didn't want to admit it.

A bad executive at a startup does more damage than a failed product launch. They mess with culture, they hire people who look like them, they make decisions that compound. By the time you act, you've lost months you didn't have.

Three mistakes I keep watching founders make (and I've made all of them too):

1. Chasing the star resume over culture fit. Big-company titles don't translate to startup pace.
2. Hiring in a hurry because the seat feels urgent. A rushed hire creates more work than an empty seat.
3. Waiting too long when someone isn't a fit. You know in 30 days...so trust it.

Show me your team, and I'll show you what your company is about.

## 3. "The Hero SKU" — Mon May 25
**Pillar: Product / Deep**

We were seated fourteen people around the table at a pre-board dinner in the Suja days. The energy was high. 

That's when a board member looked at me and said:

"Jeff, don't you see that what you think is disruptive innovation is just causing unnecessary churn?"

The room went quiet.

We'd launched 275 SKUs in seven years at Suja. One new product every nine days. Aggressively discontinued all but 55.

The real question wasn't "are we innovating?" It was: is this churn creating value or destroying it?

I responded back to him, "Innovation is not churn if it improves turns."

Here's what I've come to believe after watching a lot of brands win and lose:

Every lasting CPG brand has at least one hero SKU. Red Bull's original can. Coca-Cola's glass bottle. KIND's Dark Chocolate Nuts & Sea Salt. RXBar's Chocolate Sea Salt.

A hero isn't your best-seller. It's the product that carries the whole brand and creates gravitational pull for everything else. It earns its own velocity, generates word of mouth, and wins repurchase without trade spend.

I can see it at Proda 5 weeks in — two of our SKUs are running 3 USW above the other four.

The benchmark: if you don't have at least one SKU outperforming the rest of your portfolio by 30%+, you don't have a hero yet. You have a collection of products.

For Suja, the hero turned out to be the wellness shot. About $2,000 per week per club at Costco. The Costco buyer proposed a head-to-head test with a competitor. We almost treated it as a side show. The market told us what the hero was before we'd fully committed to believing it.

Once you find your hero:

1. Don't get bored with it. The hero is the engine, not the warm-up act.
2. Build around it. A strong hero single-serve will often generate 100%+ in additional revenue through family size, variety packs, complementary extensions.
3. Defend it in every retailer conversation. Don't let a shiny new idea steal its oxygen before it's earned the right.

"Innovation is not churn if it improves turns."

Find your hero. Nurture it. Let it lead.

## 4. "Gross Margin" — Wed May 27
**Pillar: Financial / Deep**

Gross margin determines destiny.

I mean that literally.

At Suja in 2018 we had two product lines on shelf. Kombucha and wellness shots. On the surface both were "working." Both were growing.

Under the surface, completely different businesses.

Kombucha: outsourced to a co-man, gross margin around 12%.

Wellness shots: produced internally, gross margin around 60%.

We killed the kombucha and poured resources into the shots.

Company-wide gross margin moved from roughly 32% to 40% with that ONE product-mix decision and ZERO revenue growth. The economics of the entire business transformed.

CPG is a "Penny Profit" business. The pennies matter. And the 48 pennies between those two products mattered enormously.

The math every founder should be running:

- End of Year 1: 40% gross margin floor. Below that, the math gets tight very fast.
- End of Year 3: 50% gross margin target. The best brands operate at 50-60%.
- Below 35%: danger zone. After trade spend and operating costs, you're burning cash whether you see it yet or not.

And calculate it on NET revenue. Net of trade, net of deductions. If you're using gross revenue, you're lying to yourself.

Revenue without margin is ego.

If your gross margin is below 40%, that's the most important problem in your business. Not your next round. Not your next retailer. Your margin structure.

Fix it before you scale. Scaling a bad margin just makes the hole bigger.

## 5. "Put Your Oxygen Mask on First" — Tue Jun 2
**Pillar: Personal / Vulnerability**

Entrepreneur years are like the equivalent to dog years. 1 year feels like 4.

Building Suja over 8 years to $100M and setting the foundation for it to get to IPO took a toll.

There was a long stretch after Coke "left us at the altar" where I was working 80-hour weeks. Sleeping 4 hours a night. Missing dinners, cutting vacations short, and missing moments with my family I'll never get back.

And I was drinking. Way too much.

My mother died in her early 60s from alcoholism. I knew the genes were there. I'd watched it happen to someone I loved deeply. And still... I told myself I was different. That I had it under control. That it was just how I decompressed after the pressure of payroll and board meetings and 3:00 AM panic attacks about whether the company would survive another quarter.

After I left Suja, I made the decision to spend a month in rehab. I knew that would give me the momentum of the fresh start I needed. My wife Linda and my family told me they believed that if I hadn't stepped away when I did, I might not still be here today.

I share this because nobody talks about it. In the founder community, we celebrate the grind. We wear exhaustion like a badge. We treat burnout as proof that you care enough.

It's not proof of anything except that you're breaking.

Here's what I know now at 64, looking back. Had I managed the balance better, I might still be the CEO today. I might have been the one ringing the bell at NASDAQ. That lost opportunity still sits somewhere in the back of my mind.

Put your oxygen mask on first. I don't care how strong you think you are. Your company needs you healthy. Your family needs you present. And the version of "tough" that means ignoring your own health... that's not tough. That's reckless.

Take care of yourself. There is no exit worth dying for.

## 6. "Distribution Sequencing" — Thu Jun 4
**Pillar: Strategy / Deep**

A senior Target buyer pulled me aside one night.

We were at our house in San Diego. Suja was scaling fast and we just announced we were going into Walmart.

She looked at me and said: "Jeff, if you use the same barcode and pack size at Walmart that you use here, we're going to price-match them. You'll kill your margin at Target and lose the account."

I had no idea.

We fixed it immediately. Launched a 10.5-ounce bottle at Walmart. Kept the 12-ounce at Target. The pricing held. Both accounts grew.

That conversation probably saved us several million dollars in margin erosion. But the deeper lesson wasn't about barcodes.

It was about something most founders never think about until it's too late:

The order you enter retail channels matters as much as which channels you enter.

Retail strategy isn't just about doors. It's about order.

Most founders think about "more" when they should be thinking about "next."

Whole Foods said yes? Great, now go get Kroger. Kroger picked you up? Go to Target. Target's onboard? Go to Walmart.

That logic is seductive. And it's wrong.

Every channel has a different velocity expectation. Different price architecture. Different consumer. Enter one before your brand is ready and you don't just underperform — you contaminate every channel that comes after.

I've watched brands get Walmart placement six months after launch, get discontinued in year one, then try to get back into Whole Foods. Door closed. The natural channel doesn't want a brand that already failed at mass.

The sequence that works for most better-for-you brands:

Stage 1 — Natural and Specialty (Whole Foods, Sprouts, Fresh Market). Earn the right to exist. Build velocity data. Hit 50-70%+ of category median before you go anywhere.

Stage 2 — Regional Conventional (Kroger, Publix, Albertsons, HEB). Prove the brand can travel. Get your trade spend discipline right or you'll bleed out slowly.

Stage 3 — Mass (Target, Walmart). Target before Walmart, nearly always. Target's consumer is closer to your natural buyer. Walmart's EDLP creates pricing tension across your entire portfolio. Protect with differentiated pack sizes and barcodes before you walk in.

Stage 4 — Club (Costco, Sam's Club). Its own world. Costco expects about $1,000 per club per week. Grocery sees $15 per store per week on a good brand. The expectation isn't 10x. It's 60x. And you get 13 weeks to prove it.

No universal sequence. If your category lives in conventional grocery, start there. If your consumer shops at Costco, test it. Use the framework as a map, not a straitjacket.

One possible exception — if you're in a large, mature category and you can cut a unique deal with Walmart to go in early and hard with their support. They're the largest retailer in the world and they're trying to do more of this. But you have to curate that specifically. Other than that, stick to the plan.

The principle never changes: earn the right to each channel before you enter it.

Build velocity first. Then buy doors.

Get the sequence right and every channel makes the next one easier.

Get it wrong and you'll spend more time defending decisions than building the brand.

Dream boldly. Plan soberly.

## 7. "Fundraising Is Not Proof You're Winning" — Tue Jun 9
**Pillar: Financial Wisdom**

Fundraising is not proof you're winning. It's table stakes to stay in the game.

I've run 44 rounds over 30+ years. Raised more than $200M. And I'll tell you — some of the most celebrated raises I've been part of came right before the hardest stretches those companies ever faced.

Capital amplifies whatever foundation you've built. Strong foundation? Capital accelerates the wins. Shaky? Capital just lets you fail at a bigger scale, faster.

I've watched founders pop champagne after a round like they just won the Super Bowl. Closing a round isn't an accomplishment. It's an obligation. You now owe people a return. The clock is running.

The real question isn't "did you raise?" It's "do you know exactly what every dollar is for, and does the math actually work?"

Enthusiasm attracts. Desperation repels. Discipline is what keeps the whole thing from falling apart after the wire hits.

## 8. "The Beet Juice Forklift Disaster" — Thu Jun 11
**Pillar: Suja War Story / Culture**

The most humbling moment of my CEO career.

In the early days at Suja I was making the first delivery to a Whole Foods distribution center. I'm driving a 28-foot truck into a yard full of 53-foot semi-trailers. Already out of my depth.

I need to unload pallets. The only forklift available is one I have no business operating. But it's a startup. There's nobody else. You figure it out.

I climbed on. Backed up to the truck. And immediately sliced into a pallet of beet juice!!

Beet juice...Everywhere! Bright red liquid puddle preading across the loading dock like a crime scene. Other drivers turned and stared. The warehouse guys shaking their heads.

I stood there, covered in it, thinking: this is what being a CEO actually looks like. 

Here's the thing though. That moment did more for our culture than any motivational speech I ever gave. The team saw the CEO make a complete fool of himself and laugh about it. Something shifted. People stopped being afraid to try things. Stopped worrying about looking stupid.

Because they'd already seen me — the guy who's supposed to have all the answers — standing in a puddle of beet juice wondering what just happened.

Culture isn't built in all-hands meetings. It's built in the moments when the leader shows that failure isn't fatal. That looking foolish is part of the deal. That willingness to try matters more than certainty of success.

Take the work seriously. Never take yourself too seriously.

## 9. "Kingmakers" — Tue Jun 16
**Pillar: Mentorship**

Some people will change your entire trajectory with one introduction. I call them "kingmakers." And one of the most important things a founder can learn is how to find them.

Bill Weiland was ours.

Bill founded Presence Marketing, one of the largest natural foods broker networks in the country. Very selective about the brands he worked with. But when he believed in something, he went all in.

Bill introduced us to the right buyers at Whole Foods. Connected us with distributors who actually understood cold-pressed. Opened doors that would've taken us years to find on our own — if we ever did.

Here's what most founders miss about kingmakers. Bill didn't help us because we asked. He helped because he believed in the product. He tried it, loved it, and saw something real. You can't pitch your way into a kingmaker's support. They have to feel it.

And Bill didn't just help the company. During one of our raises he created a program where his own employees could invest a portion of their retirement allocations in Suja. After we sold, a mid-level Presence employee came up to me at a trade show and thanked me. The money she made on her Suja investment put both her kids through college.

That still gets me.

Your network in this industry is one of the most powerful long-term assets you'll ever build. But it's not about collecting contacts. It's about building relationships with people who are genuinely connected, genuinely selective, and willing to put their reputation on the line for you.

I'm now one degree of separation from almost anyone in CPG. That didn't happen by accident. It happened because people like Bill took a chance on us. And we didn't let them down.

Who are the kingmakers in your category? Do you know them? Are you building something worth their reputation?

## 10. "Embrace Chaos" — Thu Jun 18
**Pillar: Mindset**

Embrace chaos. It's likely the reason you exist.

If your category were calm and orderly and perfectly served by the incumbents, there'd be no room for you. The chaos IS the opportunity.

At Suja we scaled across 7 manufacturing locations in 5 years. Processed 1.5 million pounds of fruits and vegetables every week. One night at 2 AM the power went out in our plant. There were too many orders left to fill. The transformer was on the roof and only the landlord had access.

Our production guys climbed a tree next to the building and jumped from a branch onto the roof to reconnect the circuit.

(I know, an OSHA nightmare, I did not tell them to do this!!)

But sometimes, that's a startup. That's what it ACTUALLY looks like. Not the pitch deck or the Forbes article or the fancy office snacks. But a guy climbing a tree at 2 AM because the orders have to go out.

Some founders are so focused on getting everything perfect before they move. Perfect process. Perfect team. Perfect timing. Meanwhile their competitors — messy, chaotic, imperfect — are already on shelves.

Speed compounds. So does indecision.

Start messy. Fix it as you go. Embrace the chaos!

## 11. "I Turned Down Wall Street" — Tue Jun 23
**Pillar: Personal / Mentorship**

I turned down Wall street at 28 years old. I had a permanent offer from Morgan Stanley's private equity group right out of Harvard Business School. I actually already signed the lease on an apartment in New York. I had everything set. The path I was supposed to take...all green lights ahead.

Then my roommate's boss at a company called ERICO asked me to come on, invest, and run operations. It was a 300 person, debt-ridden, unionized manufacturing company in New Jersey. Losing $3 million a year. Not a great setup. 

But I said yes. I saw the opporutnity and the experience I could gain. I turned down Wall Street. Pretty sure I was the first person from HBS that year to quit a job before it started.

I borrowed $100K from a bank in Cleveland using stock as collateral. Two months in, the president was fired. And suddenly, at 28 years old, I was running the whole thing.

I spent 10 years at ERICO. Built a state-of-the-art manufacturing plant. Transformed a commodity into a proprietary market leader. Ran 8 companies across North and South America. It was the single most formative decade of my career.

None of it would've happened on the safe path.

I think about this whenever I talk to founders who are on the fence. The ones with a good job and a startup idea. The ones who keep saying "next year" or "when the timing is right."

Here's what I know after 30+ years. There is never a convenient time. The day I decided to take the leap and buy my own company (Aztec) was the same month my youngest son Jacob was born. Life doesn't pause so you can chase your dreams.

But here's the other thing. What's the worst that can happen if you fail? You gain invaluable experience. You broaden your horizons. You go find a solid, well-paying position somewhere.

The worst outcome of trying is still better than 20 years of wondering what would have happened if you'd raised your hand.

## 12. "Show Me Your Team" — Thu Jun 25
**Pillar: People / Culture**

Show me your team and I'll show you what your company is about.

(My mom used to say: "Show me your friends and I'll show you who you are." Same principle but different stakes.)

I've built 8 companies. The ones that succeeded had teams with aligned values and complementary skills. The ones that failed — the people problems were always underneath it.

A misaligned co-founder will do more damage than a failed product launch.

A mediocre person in a position of influence creates toxicity that spreads.

A brilliant hire from a Fortune 500 who can't operate without a $10M budget and a team of 50 will DROWN in your startup (and they'll blame you for it!).

Hire for the stage you're in. Not the stage you hope to reach in the far off one-day future.

Anyone can copy your product. Very few can replicate your people.
