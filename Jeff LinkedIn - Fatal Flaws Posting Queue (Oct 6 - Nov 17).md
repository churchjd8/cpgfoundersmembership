## Tue Oct 6 — FF #3: THE $38,000 SIGNATURE

**CTA: Fatal Flaws assessment**

I want to tell you about one of the more expensive pieces of paper I never signed...

When I was selling Suja to Coke, they asked me for the confidentiality and invention assignment agreements for our team. It's a simple document that says anything you create while the company is paying you belongs to the company.

I didn't have any of them. Not one.

So Coke made me go back and get one signed by every single employee. And because we had our own manufacturing, that was 380 people.

Here's the part I didn't know. When you go back to someone down the road and ask them to sign a legal agreement, you have to give them something in return for it to hold up. So I ended up paying each employee about $100 to sign a document they would have happily signed for free on their first day.

Do the math on that (I did, painfully). It's around $38,000 for something I could have handled with a template.

People don't have a problem signing this at the start, because it only covers the work they do while you're paying them. It's when you come back years later that it gets awkward (and expensive).

So if you have employees, fractional people, consultants or independent contractors doing work for your brand right now, every one of them should have signed one. You can pull a template off ChatGPT or Babu this afternoon.

I'm pretty lax on this kind of stuff and I paid $38,000 for the reminder!

## Thu Nov 19 — FF #5: PARK CITY MISSED THE DATE BY 2 DAYS

**CTA: none**

This one sounds crazy until you read the fine print...

Most of you know Park City, the famous ski resort in Utah. Years ago the people running it had a renewal date on their lease, and somebody missed it. Not by a year, not by a month. They missed it by a couple of days.

It ended up in court, the court sided with the other side, and they ended up having to sell the resort to Vail.

An entire ski resort changed hands because of a date on a calendar!

Now flip that around, because in our world it usually works the other way. Auto-renew contracts (sometimes called "evergreen") are one of the banes of my existence. You sign in January, and buried in there is a line that says if you don't tell them by October 31st that you're NOT renewing, it automatically renews for another full year.

Unfortunately there are some bad apples out there who build their whole business this way. It's how they hold you in a contract long after you've stopped being happy with the work.

Most founders I work with are in at least one of these right now and don't know it. Usually it's a broker or a marketing agency.

So go pull your contracts this week, dump them into your AI tool, find every renewal date, and set calendar reminders at 90, 60 and 30 days out. Some people are very astute about this stuff, and they're counting on you not being.

And while you're in there... I won't sign anything longer than 3 months with an agency or a broker. If you can't show me some kind of improvement in 3 months, I don't want to be stuck with you for 12.

## Tue Oct 13 — FF #8: I KEPT MISSING MY NUMBERS AT THE BOARD MEETING

**CTA: Models in the kits**

I want to tell you about one of the more embarrassing stretches of my time running Suja...

I kept missing my revenue numbers. I'd stand up at the board meeting, explain why we missed, reset the forecast, and then come back the next quarter and do it all over again. It was a revolving door of me missing numbers.

And I'm a former CPA! I knew my way around a spreadsheet.

Finally my friend Nicky told me what the problem was. I was budgeting from the top down, by account. "We'll do this much with this retailer next year." It sounds reasonable and it's how most founders do it.

What I needed was a bottoms-up model, and it's only three things:

1. The number of doors, by retailer  
2. The number of SKUs you have on the shelf, by retailer  
3. The velocity per SKU, per retailer

Multiply it out and that's your forecast.

Here's the thing though. You're still going to be wrong. But now when you miss, you can go back and find out why. Were you wrong because you didn't get the doors you expected at Bristol Farms? Or because you got 3 SKUs on the shelf and not 5? Or because the velocity was off?

Each of those is a completely different problem with a completely different fix. A top-down number can't tell you which one you have, it can only tell you that you missed.

About half of the brands I look at don't have a bottoms-up model. And it has never been easier to build one, because Claude and the other AI tools have gotten pretty good at helping non-modeling people model.

You don't have to be right. You have to know why you were wrong.

## Thu Oct 15 — FF #6: THE DINNER TEST

**CTA: none**

I've done hundreds of hires over my 40 years of doing this.

When I really look honestly in the mirror, I have about a 60% success rate. That means I get it wrong 4 times out of 10, and most of those misses came through really great CPG recruiting firms. So it's not the recruiters. Usually I went too quick, or I didn't know exactly what I wanted.

A couple of things have helped me over the years.

The first is category experience. If you're in beverage, find somebody with beverage experience. If you're in snacks, find a snack person, or at least a food person. Once you start talking about RTDs and 12-ounce and snack pack sizes, the learning curve is steep for somebody who hasn't lived it, and you're the one paying for that curve.

The second is to triangulate. Find somebody who actually knows the person and has worked with them, not just the references they hand you.

And the third is my favorite. I like to take them out to dinner, ideally with my wife and their significant other, and just break bread. I want to understand what motivates them. But I'm also watching to see if they've got good manners, and most importantly, are they kind to restaurant staff that they're interacting with at the table.

Because the thing I hate more than anything is somebody who is great to you as the boss and treats everybody else like shit.

That person will interview beautifully. They'll also wreck the culture you're trying to build, and you won't hear about it until the damage is done.

You learn more over one dinner than you do in three interviews.

## Tue Oct 20 — FF #12: DO YOU OWN YOUR FORMULA? (ARE YOU SURE?)

**CTA: Fatal Flaws assessment**

Here's a painful one that I only figured out a couple of years ago...

Most of what I've developed over the years, I had my own in-house team creating it. So I owned everything and never thought about it.

Then I worked with a flavor house on a product and found out they owned the flavor. I went round and round with them on it, and then I vetted it with a bunch of other people in the industry, and it turns out that's actually the norm.

And it's fair when you think about it. If you go to an innovation house to build a flavor, you're paying $5,000 to $10,000 a SKU and you should have complete ownership of that. A flavor house however will do that work for little or nothing, because they make their money selling you the flavors. So they own the flavor. That's the trade, and it's okay. Ideally you negotiate a way to buy it over time, or once you've hit a certain volume.

Here's where it gets dangerous though.

In roughly a third of these agreements, the language gets broadened from the flavor to the FORMULA. The flavor is one component of your formula. The formula is your product.

If a flavor house or a co-packer owns your formula, you don't really own your company. I don't know a VC or a strategic who will buy a brand that doesn't own its own formulas.

The reputable flavor houses are super clear about exactly what you own and what they own (I had one on my Fatal Flaws webinar last month and he'd never even heard of a flavor house taking the formula). So this is about reading what you’re signing.

So go pull your agreement this week and find the ownership language. If you don't understand what it says, put it into Claude or ask somebody who does.

## Thu Oct 22 — FF #9: "IN A YEAR EVERYBODY'S WILL TASTE JUST AS GOOD"

**CTA: none**

Here's a conversation I keep having with one of my co-founders at Proda (our protein soda brand)...

Our product tastes really good. I'm proud of it, and people tell us this all the time.

And I keep telling him we can't rest on that, because in a year everybody else's product will taste just as good.

That's hard for founders to hear, because most of us fell in love with the product first. But most of what we're all creating in CPG is pretty easily copyable. If it works, somebody with more money than you is going to have a version of it on the shelf next to yours in 12 to 18 months. Take the protein soda category, when I got involved in this 15 months ago there were 2-3 protein sodas on the market and today there are over 20 brands fighting to get on retailer shelves.

What they can't easily copy is a brand.

It's also why I don't make a big deal about NDAs when I'm raising from angels. Some founders get really wound up about this. But most of your early angels are friends and family (they're not going to go compete with you), and the honest truth is there's usually not much in the deck that a good product developer couldn't figure out on their own. If you've got true technology or IP, that's a different conversation and you should protect it.

But for most of us, the recipe isn't the moat.

So the question I'd ask is where your time and money are going right now. If it's all going into making the product 5% better and none of it is going into making the brand mean something to somebody, I'd take a hard look at that.

A great product gets you in the game, the brand is what keeps you there.

## Tue Oct 27 — FF #11: THE CASH GAP, 5 DAYS AT A TIME

**CTA: Cash runway calculator**

Okay folks, let's talk about stretching your vendors for a moment.

I had a board member and partner at Goldman Sachs tell me once, "You can't do that, that's not legal." And I said well, if you want to stay in business you're going to have to start doing some of it. I've had to do it so many times over the years.

There's a number I want every founder to know, called your cash gap. It's your accounts receivable days, plus your inventory days, minus your accounts payable days. That's how many days of sales you are personally financing.

Here's how I work on each end of it.

On the receivables side, I offer 2% 10 net 30, and I WANT people to take the discount. I'll happily give up 2% to get paid in 10 days.

On the payable side, I stretch... gently. I'll push a vendor out by 5 days for a month. If I don't get any bitching, I'll go another 5 days. Pretty soon you're at 40, 45 days and nobody's upset.

But there's an art to who you can do it with.

You can't really do it with your freight companies, and you can't really do it with your ingredient suppliers. You need them too much and they'll cut you off.

You often can with law firms (unless it's part of a transaction, because then they just deduct it at closing) and a lot of times with marketing agencies.

Getting paid in 10 days while you're paying in 45 is a really favorable place to be.

And here's the part people don't really understand. If you start at 45 days of AR and 100 days of inventory and you bring those down, that's a one-time permanent cash savings. It's cash that comes out of the business and stays out. For a young brand that can be the difference between raising this quarter and raising next year on better terms.

Cash exhaustion is about 35% of the reason brands fail. This is one of the few levers that doesn't cost you any equity.

## Thu Oct 29 — FF #4: "THEY KIND OF HAD AMNESIA"

**CTA: none**

Here's something I tell every founder who is building with a partner...

Back at Suja, my two co-founders and I agreed that I would be paid a bonus as CEO if we hit a certain revenue level. We talked about it, we all agreed on it, and we moved on with building the business. We never put it in writing.

4 or 5 years down the road we hit that number... and they kind of had amnesia about it.

I don't think anybody was being dishonest. We forget. You have hundreds of these conversations in the early days, usually when you're exhausted, and five years later three people remember three different versions of the same conversation.

But it ends up in a lot of frustration and disagreement with people that you love, people you're really in this thing together with. And I think something like 80% of the businesses that fail had some kind of founder issue in there somewhere.

So now I do something simple. I write a Memorandum of Understanding “MOU” between the founders (call it whatever you want). It's not a legal agreement and it doesn't need an attorney. It's a statement between you and your co-founders about whatever isn't being captured in the legal documents. Who gets what if we hit which number, who's making which decisions, what happens if one of us wants out, etc.

Just put it down on paper while everybody still likes each other.

It takes about an hour and it could save your friendship.

It made the list in Chapter 21 of my book. Less than three weeks until it's out.

## Tue Nov 3 — FF #2: THE PHONE CALL TO COKE

**CTA: Fatal Flaws replay + assessment**

True story that I don't tell very often (talk about looking into the abyss)...

It was the night before Coca-Cola was voting on whether to invest in Suja. Years of work were riding on that vote going our way.

And that night I had to pick up the phone, call the CEO, and tell them we were having a product recall.

I can't really describe what that feels like. You dial the number knowing the sentence you're about to say could end the whole deal... and you say it anyway, because these people are about to be your partner and they need to hear it from you.

Here's the part that still gets me. It ended up that we didn't have to do the recall at all. It was an overreaction by one of our food safety attorneys.

But the call had already been made. So now Coke wanted to look at everything (rightfully so), and what should have been a vote the next morning turned into two more months of them going through the business before they finally closed the deal.

Two months of not knowing. I wouldn't wish that on anybody.

I share this because "has your brand experienced a recall?" is one of the 30 questions in my Fatal Flaws assessment, and it's one that founders tend to skip right past. None of us build a recall into our models. There is no line in the P&L for it. But a recall in your first 2 or 3 years, or in the middle of a fundraising round, is most of the time catastrophic.

Mine turned out to be a false alarm and it STILL almost cost me one of the biggest deals of my career.

So invest in your QA early, audit your suppliers, know how to trace your lots all the way back to the product being in the ground and know what a HACCP plan actually is. And if the bad news ever does come... make the call yourself.

## Thu Nov 5 — FF #10: THE $10 GREEN JUICE

**CTA: none**

When I started Suja, we were selling a 16-ounce bottle of green juice for $10.

And on the coasts it did great! People in LA and New York were happy to pay it.

But I knew we couldn't sell that in mainstream USA. Nobody in the middle of the country is paying $10 for a bottle of green juice, I don't care how good it is. And the goal was always to get to as many people as we could.

So we dropped the bottle from 16 ounces to 12 and got the price down to $3.99. Even that wasn't really enough, but it was enough that we could build a business and a market around it. And we took a real hit on margin to do it.

I was talking with a founder this week who has a terrific product (great technology, really well formulated) but due to his COGS he has to sell for $5 a can to make the margins work. And while retailers love it, they're not bringing it in.

Here's what I told him. Look at where your category is priced. You can be above it if you have certain features and benefits, but from most that I've seen you can't be more than about 50% above it and still get real volume. Some consumers will stretch. Most won't.

So if the math doesn't work at that price, you have a few options. Look at your dosage (if your ingredient is more bioavailable, you may not need as much of it). Look at a smaller size. Look at a different format altogether... the stick packs and little packets are doing really well right now because the cost per serving is so much lower when you're not shipping all that liquid.

I'm dealing with this myself right now. Proda is at $3.49 and the modern sodas are at $2.49. I'd love to be at $3.99 to cover the increasing cost of protein, however we feel like we’d lose the market.

If you're way out of whack on price, you'll sell some and the people who buy it will love it. You just won't sell it at scale.

## Tue Nov 10 — FF #7: MONTHS 10 TO 18

**CTA: Fundraising Kit**

Okay founders, let's talk about the stretch nobody warns you about...

Month one is all high-fives. We got this brand, we're going to make a billion dollars, everything's going great. You've got cash, you got a good valuation, you didn't take much dilution.

Then somewhere around month 10 to 12 it hits you. "This is a lot harder than I thought. How do people DO this?"

And that feeling runs until about month 18.

Part of the reason is that you're now a company with data. Before you had numbers, you were selling hope and dreams, and you could raise at a $5 to $10 million pre-money if you were on the shelf. Let's say you did your round at $7 million.

Now you're a year in with a $700,000 run rate, the cash is getting low, and an investor looks at that and says you're a $2.8 million business. And you're sitting there going "oh crap, do I have a down round?"

I see more brands get in trouble in this window than any other.

Two things I'd tell you if you're unfortunately there right now.

One, this is why I tell founders to raise 15 months of runway when they raise. If you can get through that first year and into the second on the cash you already have, you give the numbers time to catch up to the story.

Two, year two really is different. Think about how retail works. The buyer meetings often happen in the fall, but the resets happen 4 to 6 months later. So your first year is a partial year almost by definition, because you weren't on the shelf on January 1st. The next year you are, and all of a sudden you've got growth that was already baked in.

I'm living this right now with Proda. My partners keep asking why we can't go get more grocery accounts, and the answer is that there are no resets happening right now.

It's not you, it's the calendar... so manage your cash and hang in there.

## Tue Nov 17 — FF #1: THE $100M EXIT THAT PAID $5M

**CTA: Fundraising Kit**

I want to tell you about something that still bothers me every time I think about it...

I have 4 or 5 good friends who have sold their businesses for over $100 million.

Each of them walked away with less than $5 million.

Now, $5 million is not nothing (I would never say that). But think about what it took to get there. Ten years of grinding, the missed dinners, the 3am wake-ups worrying about payroll, pouring blood, sweat and tears into this thing... and when the wire finally hits, you own a sliver of all the value you created.

So how does that happen? It usually starts way back in the beginning.

When you don't have cash, it's really easy to say "okay, I'll give you a little slice of the company instead of paying you." A slice here, a slice there, and then a friends and family round where you give up way too much because the money was right in front of you and you needed it. None of it feels like a big deal at the time.

Then you do 2 or 3 more rounds on top of that and the math gets ugly fast.

Here's what I tell every founder I work with:

1. Don't give up more than 15 to 20% of the company in any single round.

2. After your friends and family round, you (or you and your founding team) want to be directionally above 80%. If you're sitting at 60% after that first round, you've got a problem, because there are more rounds coming. The typical CPG brand goes through 3 +/- rounds to get to profitability.

3. When you're all done fundraising, the founder or the founding team should still own at least 50% of the business.

I've been through over 40 fundraising rounds, and I can tell you that you can fix a bad hire and you can fix a bad co-man... but you can't get equity BACK once it's gone.
