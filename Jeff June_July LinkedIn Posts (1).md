## **1\. "My Gripe With Agencies" — Tue Jun 30**

**Pillar: Founder Wisdom / Operational**

I've watched the same scene play out maybe 30 times in 30 years.

A founder calls me, frustrated.

They signed with an agency three months ago. The agency founder who pitched them, the head of strategy who promised personal involvement, the senior creative director with all the war stories...gone.

In their place...a 25-year-old account coordinator who's never sold a unit of anything. Doesn't know the category. Doesn't know the buyer. Couldn't tell you the difference between an end cap and a side cap if you spotted them the first letter.

Meanwhile the A team is back out there pitching the next logo.

That's my gripe with agencies.

You get sold by the A team. You sign because the founder, or the head of strategy, or the senior creative director was in the room. They were sharp, they got your brand, you felt the alignment.

And then you sign the contract...

And they send in the C team.

I've watched this play out across more agencies than I can count. Mostly in the marketing space, i.e. creative shops. PR firms. Brokers. Even fractional CFO firms\!

Six ways to protect yourself...

1. Work with a SMALLER agency or even a fractional team member. The people who sold you are the people doing the work. Less prestige on the logo, way more accountability on the output.  
2. If you go with a bigger one, get the comms flow in writing BEFORE you sign. Who's on your account day to day? How often does the A team actually show up? What's the escalation path when something goes sideways? Get specific. Get names. Get it in the SOW.  
3. Triangulate with someone who has worked with this group before. Use things like my Founder's Club on WhatsApp to ask other founders whether anyone has worked with the group before.  
4. Most of the time that they don't go well for me it's because I didn't set the deliverables properly. At the start of a new relationship ask yourself, "what specifically do I want to see at the end of this commitment that would tell me I got great results and value from this contract?" Begin with the end in mind\!  
5. Never sign a long-term contract with an agency.  
6. Never sign more than a 3-month commitment. If you can't see some level of success in three months then this group isn't for you\!

The agency model is built to sell hours. Your job as the founder is to force the relationship into outcome terms before the contract starts. Not after.

You'll know in the first 30 days whether you got the A team or the C team...so trust it.

## **2\. "Nobody In Omaha Knows Your Brand" — Thu Jul 2**

**Pillar: Distribution / Deep**

I'll never forget the morning I sat down with our Rowdy distribution report and realized we were getting dropped.

Store by store. Drip... drip... drip... gone.

It was like Chinese water torture.

We had hit 20,000 doors in under 24 months. Faster than almost anyone in modern energy drink history. We had the capital. We had the story. We had the reputation to go big quickly. Investors were lit up. The team was celebrating. We were the brand "everyone" was talking about.

But we didn't have the marketing dollars to support those doors.

So we watched the doors fall off, store by store, week after week. And there was almost nothing we could do to stop it.

Because nobody in Omaha knew our brand.

(Nothing personal, Nebraska...let me explain.)

Here's something I tell every founder I work with about shelf space.

Getting on the shelf is only part of the game. It's actually the EASIER part of the game. STAYING on the shelf is the real game\!

Every door you open you have to DEFEND. Every door you defend costs trade spend, marketing, and management bandwidth. Spread that across 20,000 stores when you should be at 2,000 and you've signed up to lose slowly in every single one of them.

Take Target for example. I never recommend going into more than 250 stores in Target until you have the velocity to support it (even if they are pushing you to). After 250 stores in Target you start hitting the wall where reach outpaces awareness. Unless you've got the marketing dollars to drive trial in every one of those new markets at the same time...you're just renting shelf space.

James Richardson is excellent in how he explains this in his book, "Ramping Your Brand." You earn the next 250 doors with the velocity in the first 250\. Not the other way around.

Door count is the most seductive vanity metric in our industry. It looks GREAT on a deck, but dies hard on a shelf.

Earn the velocity first. THEN take the doors.

## **3\. "You Don't Have to Be First. You Just Don't Want to Be 61st." — Tue Jul 7**

**Category Strategy / Deep**

A few years after we launched Suja, I walked into a Whole Foods and counted the cold-pressed juice section.

60 SKUs.

In a single retailer.

When WE launched, there were maybe 3 of us fighting for that real estate. Blueprint was there. Evolution was there. We were the third major player on the shelf, not the first.

Three years later there were 60\.

Standing in that aisle staring at 60 versions of cold-pressed juice was the moment something clicked for me about category timing.

You don't have to be first. But you sure as hell don't want to be 61st.

Here's the rule I've learned watching brands win and lose for 30 years...

Being first is a fantasy founders chase because it sounds heroic. But being first means you carry the cost of educating the consumer. You pay to build the category. You pay to teach buyers what the category even is. And then by the time the category is real, three smarter competitors come in and take advantage of the fact that you are now out of cash\!

(One of the often missed keys to success here is a little bit of luck and timing too. Within two months of our launching, our top two competitors got acquired and as often happens, they went off the rails. We became the category leader almost by accident.)

Being THIRD into a real category and building a better mousetrap? That's a totally viable strategy.

In fact when we launched Suja we saw what Blueprint had done with putting their ingredients on the front of the label indicating transparency. But they didn't put them in the same order as on the nutrition panel and we thought that was an easy tweak to make. We didn't want to copy them, so we created a playful word cloud on the front with the ingredients in the proper order. Consumers loved it\!

But here's the other side...

In the 60-SKU section every week another founder was walking in pitching their juice idea.

That's the 61st problem.

If a consumer is going to walk past 60 options to find yours, your reason to exist better be undeniable. Not "we're organic." Not "we're cleaner." Sixty other brands are saying the same thing. The buyer has heard it sixty times already.

Before you launch into a saturated category, ask yourself the question every buyer is going to ask within 30 seconds of meeting you...

Why does the shelf need a 61st version of this?

If you can't answer that in one sentence, don't launch. Go back to find the answer. THEN come back.

You don't have to be first. You just can't be 61st with nothing disruptive to say.

## **4\. "Demo Your A Stores, Not Your D Stores" — Thu Jul 9**

**Sales / Operational**

Here's the math that should change how every founder runs their demo program.

A 'C' store doing 1 USW. You spend the demo dollars. Pump it to 3 USW. You gained 2 units a week.

An 'A' store doing 12 USW. SAME demo cost. Pump it to 18 USW. You gained 6 units a week.

Same demo spend. 3x the gain. AND political capital to walk into the buyer and ask for more SKUs, more facings, better shelf position.

But most founders run their demo program completely backwards.

They see a 'C' store doing 1 USW and they think...let's go save it\!\! Send the demo team. Pour lots of dollars and make it a rescue mission.

WRONG.

Every chain has A stores, B stores, C stores...and yes, a few D stores that probably shouldn't carry you in the first place.

A's: 10 USW. B's: 5 or 6\. C's: 1\. D's: decoration.

Now do the math on where your demo dollars actually move the needle.

This is the part most founders miss...

Demos shouldn't be a rescue mission. They're an AMPLIFIER. You amplify what's already working. The C's need a different conversation (or a swap), not a demo team.

Spend your money where the engine is already running.

That's how you get the A's to 20 USW. That's how you build the hero store list you walk in with.

## **5\. "Yes. Figure It Out Later." — Thu Jul 23**

**Pillar: Suja War Story / Mindset**

October 2012\. Two months after we launched Suja.

I'm sitting across from the Whole Foods Austin corporate team. We're in Southern California stores only. Tiny brand. Maybe 10 SKUs. We were running on adrenaline and cold-pressed juice.

And they made me an offer that should make any founder dizzy.

"Jeff, we think the cold-pressed category can handle a couple more brands. We don't want a private label. But if we let you in, we want our own dedicated SKU line. Exclusive to Whole Foods. And we want it in the Bake Off in February at Expo. Against Blueprint. Against Evolution. The managers will vote and choose one brand."

It was October. Expo was in February. We had 16 weeks.

Every smart voice in my head said no. We were a startup. We had 10 SKUs and no capital to launch a dedicated Whole Foods line. No bottles, no labels, no inventory, no cash flow.

I said YES anyway.

We scrambled. We built the line. We showed up at the Bake Off with a dedicated Whole Foods SKU line of cold pressed juices. The managers tasted ours next to Blueprint and Evolution and voted us in unanimously.

That moment changed the trajectory of Suja. Other retailers saw what Whole Foods did and they wanted in too. By the end of year one we had 3 product lines and 30 SKUs.

Was that smart? Probably not. Smart money says don't do that. Stay focused. Don't add complexity. Don't spread yourself too thin.

Sometimes smart money is wrong\!\!

Opportunity knocks once. You either grab it by the throat or you watch someone else carry it home.

Always have a Plan B. But when Plan A shows up at the door, say YES. Figure out the how later.

## **6\. "Trade Spend Is The Line Nobody Audits" — Mon Jul 27**

**Pillar: Financial / Deep**

I'll never forget the board meeting at Suja where our CFO walked us through every line of the P\&L.

When he hit trade spend, the actuals were materially higher than budget. Multiple points off.

What hurt worse was when I asked the team which specific promos were driving real lift and which ones were just giving away margin for free...nobody could give me a clear answer.

That was the moment I learned a hard truth about CPG and more specifically: Trade Spend\!

Trade spend is the biggest line item expense on your P\&L. And almost nobody manages it properly.

Look at a typical CPG P\&L...

Trade spend: 20-25% of gross revenue (sometimes more, depending on category).  
 Marketing: 10-15%.  
 Sales: \~5%.

Trade is your biggest controllable expense and most founders are not managing it.

Take a two-for-five vs a two-for-four promo. Sounds like a small decision. It's not\! A two-for-four is a much deeper discount, so you're giving away a lot more margin. And here's the kicker...you might not even get the lift to justify it.

Here's what most people don't realize about promo. A lot of consumers buy simply because they see the SALE tag. They're not doing the math on the price point, the tag itself is the trigger. So if you went deeper than you needed to, you didn't earn extra units. You just gave away margin for free.

Now, the other side of this is true a lot of the time too. Generally the deeper you discount, the more lift you should expect. On a BOGO you'd often expect to roughly double your volume, so if you normally do 5 USW you should be seeing 10 on the BOGO. A two-for-four might run around a 50% lift, a two-for-five maybe 30%.

So the discipline is simple. Know the lift you should expect from each promo, then measure what you actually get. If you're hitting those numbers, great, keep running it. If one is underperforming, adjust the next one and stop pouring hard-earned margin into the discounts that aren't working.

The discipline I work to build with every founder I work with...

1. Capture the data. Every promo, every retailer, every depth, every lift. ALL of it.  
2. Look at the competitive set. What is everyone else in your category running? Are you the cheapest TPR in the set? You're leaving money on the table. Most expensive? You're getting skipped.  
3. Audit every promo on a 90-day cycle. Kill what doesn't lift. Double down on what does.

CPG is a "Penny Profit" business. The pennies matter.

And the pennies you give away on a bad promo never come back.

## **7\. "We Accidentally Named Our Juice Brand 'Dirty Girl'" — Thu Jul 30**

**Pillar: Suja Origin Story / Comedic**

True story that most people don't know.

(But I got nothing to lose at this point, so here you go\!)

Eight weeks before we were set to launch Suja, we had everything lined up...besides what we were going to name the Company\!

For weeks we ran through names. Nothing stuck.

Then on the very last day when we had to make a decision my partner Eric had lunch at our local Whole Foods and happened to sit next to a beautiful Indian woman. When he asked her what her name was she cheerfully said “Suja”. When Eric asked what it meant she said that it meant "long, beautiful life" in ancient Hindu. When Eric came back after lunch he told me the story and i said, WERE DONE, lets go\!

I'll never forget the moment. It felt like a sign from the heavens. THIS was the name. We had been searching for weeks and the universe just dropped it in our lap at a dinner table.

So we named the brand Suja.

We never verified it.

A couple of months later, we were growing fast and getting a ton of positive PR from large publications and i said to our head of marketing at the time, Bella Tumini, would you mind getting a few team members and google as much as you can to find the long beautiful life definition in ancient Hindu and off they went. About two days later she came back and said in her normal very sarcastic way, well we have some good news and bad news and said further the good news is that we found the definition but the bad news was that it doesn't mean what we thought it was\! She couldn't help laughing as she said, well… 

"Suja" in Portuguese means "dirty girl."

Excuse me?\!

We dug. We searched. We could NOT find a single source backing up "long, beautiful life." Anywhere. Not Sanskrit. Not Hindi. Not anything.

What we DID find...was a lot of Portuguese speakers confirming that "suja" is, in fact, a slightly inappropriate phrase\!\!

So we accidentally named our organic wellness juice brand "Dirty Girl."

(Thankfully, we MADE it great\!)

So, thank you, Suja. Long, beautiful life. Or dirty girl.

We'll never really know\!

## **8\. "Find Your Hero. Then Stretch It." — Mon Aug 3**

**Pillar: Product / Innovation**

A few years into Suja I sat down with our SKU velocity data.

Lemon Love was our top selling sku in the product line. It was doing more than everything else combined.

The team's instinct was to add MORE flavors to scale the brand.

My instinct was the opposite.

I told them...don't add SKUs. Take the heroes and put them in different FORMATS.

Most founders innovate by adding SKUs.

The thinking goes...if 5 SKUs does X, then 8 SKUs must do 1.6X. If 8 is good, 15 must be great\!

It doesn't work like that.

Consumers don't buy three more units because you have three more SKUs. They buy ONE, at least in the early days of a brand. They pick the one they like. So now you've split the same velocity across more shelf space, and the buyer is staring at your weakest SKUs wondering why they took the bet.

The smarter move...don't add SKUs. STRETCH the heroes.

At Suja it was Lemon Love and the green juices. At Proda right now it's Strawberry Lemonade and the Shirley Temple. Those are the SKUs where the velocity is screaming louder than the rest of the lineup. The market is telling you what people love.

When you find one of those, don't go invent a new flavor. Take the hero and put it in a different FORMAT...

* 12oz hero...try an 48 ounce family style format.  
* Single...turn it into a 6-pack.  
* 12oz can...mini it down to 8.4oz, the way Coke did. They made a fortune on that format play.

A hero SKU doing $5M a year can become $15M just by extending into new formats. SAME product. SAME love. New use cases, new occasions, new dayparts.

Try this with a mediocre SKU and it won't work. Nobody loved it at 12oz, nobody loves it as a 6-pack either.

"Innovation is not churn if it improves turns."

Find your hero. Then stretch it.

## **9\. "The Psychic Medium Was Right About Suja" — Thu Aug 6**

**Pillar: Suja War Story / Faith**

A few years into building Suja, my wife Linda went to see a psychic medium.

(Linda's like that. Open to things I'm usually not.)

She came back and told me something the medium kept repeating during the session...

"I keep seeing these...baby Sujas. Tiny ones. They are the key."

Baby Sujas?\!

I had no idea what she was talking about. We were a 16oz cold-pressed juice company. There were no "baby Sujas."

But Linda kept pushing me on it. She said maybe we had to make mini shots of Suja. (This was before Vive or any of the other major wellness shots were on the shelf.)

Our head chef and developer Brian Riblett made the most incredible tasting shots and we peddled them into a bunch of retailers but because there was no category for it there wasnt an interest at the shelf. We decided to moth ball the idea and wait until at least two brands came out which was when we thought that with some help from other brands we could make a category out of it. About another two years later both Vive and KOR launched their shots and they were doing really well so we decided it was time and we jumped in with full force. Given our background in Cold pressed juice consumers gave us authority to launch shots and within 3 months we were the market leader in a fast growing, high gross margin category.  These 2oz bottles. TINY. Dedicated SKUs at Costco. In fact in some clubs they were doing $10K per club per week. Then everywhere.

The shots became the savior of the business.

Today nearly half of Suja's annual revenue comes through that line. Way better margins than the 16oz juice. They unlocked the entire Costco relationship. They carried us when the kombucha business stalled.

The baby Sujas.

Now look...I'm not telling you to go run your CPG strategy through a psychic.

I'm telling you something different.

Sometimes the move you need to make isn't in the data. It's in the conversation you almost dismissed. The off-hand comment from the person who isn't even in the meeting. The voice in your wife's head she kept hearing.

You can build a brand on spreadsheets. You CAN'T build a brand without also listening to the things you can't measure.

Stay open. The next $200M of your business might be hiding inside a sentence you almost ignored.

## **10\. "5 SKUs Is The Right Place to Start" — Mon Aug 10**

**Pillar: Product / Retail Strategy**

Every founder I talk to wants to launch with 12 SKUs.

"But we have 12 great flavors\!" "But we want a FULL lineup on day one\!"

I get it. You're excited. You've been working on those recipes for two years. You believe in all of them.

Here's the problem.

I've sat through hundreds of buyer meetings between Suja, Rowdy, and now Proda. I can tell you exactly what the buyer is doing in their head when you walk in with 12 SKUs versus 5...

With 12, they're trying to figure out which 7 you're going to fail on.

With 5, they're picturing 5 hero SKUs winning the set.

That's the math.

You only have so much velocity to spread across your launch. That velocity is the number the buyer is going to grade you on. Spread it across 12 SKUs and the average per-SKU looks WEAK. Spread it across 5 and the average looks STRONG.

It may be the same total business, but it tells a completely different story to the buyer.

The right number of SKUs to launch with is around 5\.

And here's the pro move I have the founders that I work with do once they're a year or two in...

Take the Nielsen data. Go look at the long tail in your category. In most categories the big brands have 15-20 SKUs and...5 are killing it, 10 are mediocre, 5 are dogs. Olipop and Poppi both have lineups that look like this.

Walk into the buyer with the data. Don't beg for a slot. Say...

"If you replace these 4 low-velocity SKUs with our 1 SKU that turns 5x faster, you grow the entire category."

Buyers don't grow their careers by accommodating new brands. They grow their careers by growing their CATEGORY.

Show them how you grow the category. That's how you get the slot.

Start with 5\. Earn the right to more.

## **11\. "People Were Upset When I Did The Coke Deal" — Thu Aug 13**

**Pillar: Suja War Story / Leadership**

Before we closed the Coke deal at Suja, I called Seth Goldman.

Seth had done it before us. Sold Honest Tea to Coca-Cola and absorbed every body shot the natural foods community could throw. If anyone could tell me what was coming, it was him.

His advice did three things at once.

It told me what to expect from the community.

It gave me the framework for how to structure the deal.

And it gave me the language I needed when the world I'd been part of for nearly a decade turned on me overnight.

He told me...

"Jeff, these smaller brands, Honest, Suja...we can be the catalyst for change in society. But we can't drive it. We're not big enough. We don't have the inertia or infrastructure. You NEED a Coke to drive it."

He was right.

When word got out that I was doing the Coca-Cola deal, the natural foods world took it personally. The blogs. The forums. The conferences. People I'd known for years suddenly looked at me differently. Even my kids questioned it (rightfully so).

I saw it as an advantage. Let me explain.

And yes, I am talking about doing the Coca Cola deal with Suja ;)

The thing Seth taught me that mattered more than the strategy was the GUARDRAILS. When he sold Honest to Coke he put it in the contract...they couldn't change the ingredients. They couldn't reformulate. They couldn't cut corners on what made Honest, Honest.

He paved the way for me.

When Coke came in at Suja we set the same guardrails. And honestly...they got it. They didn't push us to swap ingredients. They didn't push us to make it cheaper. They didn't try to make it unhealthier. They wanted what we'd built, not a watered down version of it.

And in return we got access to a distribution and capital engine no independent brand can match.

Here's the lesson I'd give any founder facing a strategic deal the purists in your community are going to hate...

Your job is not to be popular in the community. Your job is to SCALE the mission\!

If the mission gets bigger because of the deal, do the deal. If the mission gets compromised, walk.

Set the guardrails. Then go drive the change.
