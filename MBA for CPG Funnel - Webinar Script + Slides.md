## LESSON 1 — THE REAL PRICE OF ADMISSION

### Slide 8 — Lesson 1 title
**On slide:** 1. The real price of admission.

### Slide 9 — The two numbers
**On slide:** $150K to $350K. 9 to 12 months.

**Say (beats):**
- That's what it realistically takes to get a first food or beverage brand from idea to shelf. The average is about $250,000.
- Most founders I meet have budgeted half that and half the time.
- The Rule of Twos: everything takes twice as long and costs twice as much as you think. I have never once seen it fail.
- If you've already launched and you're short on cash, this is probably why. You're not bad at this. The plan was wrong.

### Slide 10 — The 8-phase Pre-Launch Roadmap
**On slide:** The table. Phase, focus, budget.
1 Market and white space, $10K · 2 Company foundation, $10K · 3 Formulation, $25K · 4 Brand and packaging, $40K · 5 Co-man and 3PL, $25K · 6 Go-to-market, $40K · 7 Investor model and raise, $20K · 8 Launch, $80K

**Say (beats):**
- Walk the phases quickly. The order matters as much as the dollars.
- Phase 1: retailers only want products that grow their category. If you replace something that already sells, they don't need you.
- Phase 3: taste. Consumers choose on taste 93% of the time. Most founders get it about 70% right and launch anyway.
- Phase 5: own your formula. Use a lawyer who has written a co-man contract before.
- Phase 7: have 25% of your raise soft-committed before you go out wide.

### Slide 11 — The takeaway
**On slide:** Dream boldly. Plan soberly.

**Say:**
"If you're pre-launch, build your budget on $250,000 and twelve months and you'll be one of the few who isn't surprised. If you've launched, go back and look at which phases you skipped. That's usually where the fire is."

## LESSON 2 — YOUR MARGIN IS DECIDED BEFORE YOU SELL A UNIT

### Slide 12 — Lesson 2 title
**On slide:** 2. Your margin is decided before you sell a unit.

### Slide 13 — The waterfall
**On slide:** A $5.99 shelf price stepping down. Shelf price $5.99 → after the retailer ~$3.50 (35 to 40% margin) → after the distributor ~$2.60 (25 to 30% on cost) → after trade and deductions $1.80 to $2.20 (trade 15 to 25%, deductions 3 to 5%) → after cost of goods ~$0.80 at a 40% gross margin.

**Say (beats):**
- A consumer pays $5.99. That is not $5.99 to you. Walk down the steps.
- The retailer needs a 35 to 40% margin, so they're buying from the distributor at about $3.50.
- The distributor takes 25 to 30% on cost, so they're buying from you at about $2.60. That's your invoice price.
- Then trade spend, 15 to 25% of gross revenue, plus the deductions and chargebacks you didn't know existed. Budget 3 to 5% for those.
- You're left with somewhere between $1.80 and $2.20. Your cost of goods, your marketing and your team all have to fit inside that number.
- Most new founders set their price by looking at the competitor on the shelf. Then they find out afterward what's left. That's backwards.
- (Numbers are the conventional grocery example from the gross-to-net blog post. The last step assumes about $2.00 net and the 40% target.)

### Slide 14 — The target
**On slide:** 40% gross margin by the end of year 2. 50% by year 3 to 4.

**Say (beats):**
- This is the plan I give every founder.
- CPG is a "penny profit" business. The pennies matter.
- Below that and you can't afford marketing, you can't afford a team, and investors can't make the math work.
- "Revenue without margin is ego."

### Slide 15 — Kombucha vs. shots
**On slide:** Kombucha: 12% gross margin. Wellness shots: 60%. Company margin: 32% → 40%.

**Say (beats):**
- At Suja we were selling kombucha that somebody else made for us at about a 12% margin. Great revenue. Terrible business.
- We launched a two-ounce wellness shot we made ourselves at around 60%.
- Swapping one for the other took the whole company from 32% to 40%. Not from growing. From choosing.
- That decision is a big part of why Suja is still here.

### Slide 16 — The takeaway
**On slide:** Gross margin determines destiny.

**Say:**
"I mean that literally. You can cut expenses all day long, but you can't cut your way to greatness. Do the waterfall on your hero product this week. If it doesn't get to 40%, you don't have a marketing problem or a sales problem. You have a math problem, and it's fixable, but only if you look at it."

## LESSON 3 — STORES ARE NOT THE GOAL. VELOCITY IS.

### Slide 17 — Lesson 3 title
**On slide:** 3. Stores are not the goal. Velocity is.

### Slide 18 — Rented space
**On slide:** "Shelf space is rented every week. Velocity is your rent payment."

**Say (beats):**
- New founders celebrate the purchase order. I did too.
- Getting on the shelf is the starting line. You have about nine months to prove you belong there.
- The retailer is measuring units per store per week against everything else in your set. Aim to be at least at the 50th percentile.
- Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains.

### Slide 19 — The $1M lesson
**On slide:** Rowdy Energy. ~$1,000,000 in slotting. Discontinued in 8 months.

**Say (beats):**
- One of my three strikeouts. We went wide and fast. We paid Albertsons about a million dollars in slotting.
- We spread our marketing too thin to support those stores. The velocity wasn't there. Eight months later we were out.
- And at Suja, we once ran billboards in Manhattan when we had four stores in the whole city. Awareness with nowhere to convert it.
- I've done this wrong at both ends. More stores is not more success.

### Slide 20 — Order matters
**On slide:** Natural and specialty → Target and regional → Club → Conventional grocery → Walmart last.

**Say (beats):**
- Retail strategy isn't about doors. It's about order.
- Each channel builds the proof you need for the next one.
- Win 100 stores before you say yes to 500.
- And watch repeat rate. If fewer than 20% of first-time buyers come back, you don't have proof of concept. You have churn. We aimed for 40 to 50%.

### Slide 21 — The takeaway
**On slide:** Your first sale belongs to your marketing. Your second sale belongs to your product.

**Say:**
"Never celebrate new distribution until it's producing velocity. If you're in stores now, find out your units per store per week and where that puts you in the set. If you don't know how to get that number, that's the first thing to fix."

## LESSON 4 — CASH IS OXYGEN. EQUITY IS EXPENSIVE.

### Slide 22 — Lesson 4 title
**On slide:** 4. Cash is oxygen. Equity is the most expensive money you'll ever spend.

### Slide 23 — Runway
**On slide:** 18 months of runway. Start your next raise with 4 months left, at the latest.

**Say (beats):**
- You can survive a flawed product, a bad hire, a strategic mistake. You can't survive running out of cash.
- Raise for 18 months. I'll be straight with you, my own career average has been more like 12 to 15, and I felt every one of those missing months.
- I have never successfully sold or financed a company from a position of weakness. Enthusiasm attracts. Desperation repels.

### Slide 24 — The 4% founder
**On slide:** Company sells for $200M. Founder owns 4%. $8M before taxes. Ten years of work.

**Say (beats):**
- Early on, equity feels free. A point for the advisor. Two for the designer. A big chunk for the first check.
- Then you raise four or five rounds.
- Rules I'd give you: 15 to 20% dilution a round, no more. Aim to still own half at exit. Never hand out equity without vesting. Equity without vesting is a time bomb.
- At Suja we raised 11 times in 7 years, and about $60 million of it went into building our own plants. If we'd outsourced, the founders would have kept roughly 20% more of the company. That's a decision you make in year one and pay for in year eight.

### Slide 25 — The takeaway
**On slide:** Capital amplifies whatever foundation you've built.

**Say:**
"Fundraising isn't proof you're winning. It's table stakes to stay in the game. If your margins and your velocity are right, money makes you bigger. If they're wrong, money makes the hole deeper."

## LESSON 5 — THE PAPER YOU SIGN CAN END YOU

### Slide 26 — Lesson 5 title
**On slide:** 5. The paper you sign can end you.

### Slide 27 — Three to never sign
**On slide:**
Personal guarantees.
Evergreen and multi-year contracts.
Anything that gives away your formula.

**Say (beats):**
- Personal guarantees. They survive the company. They can take your house. You will be tempted. Don't do it! I've skirted them for 40 years and you can too.
- Evergreen contracts renew themselves if you forget to cancel. The vendor is hoping you forget. Today I sign three months, max.
- Your formula. A flavor house may own the flavor. Nobody but you should ever own the formula.

### Slide 28 — The handshake
**On slide:** $1,000,000+ in spoilage deductions. 90 days.

**Say (beats):**
- Early at Suja, our shelf life was short and distributors were nervous. They said they'd lean in if we took the spoilage risk. We trusted them. We said yes.
- Ninety days later we had over a million dollars in deductions and had to go raise an emergency million.
- "Trust must be documented."
