## Post 1: THINGS MONEY CAN SOLVE AREN'T REAL PROBLEMS

**CTA: none.** Suggested slot: Thu Nov 26 (Thanksgiving).

People ask me how I handle the stress of this business, and my answer usually surprises them...

Things that money can solve aren't real problems to me.

Now don't get me wrong, they suck. A business going sideways can affect the house you live in, the car you drive and when you get to retire. (Linda and I always joke that she doesn't care about the money, she just cares about the stuff.)

But a real problem is a sick kid. A real problem is something that all the money in the world can't fix.

I've had 8 companies. 5 of them were wild successes and 3 of them were wild failures, and I felt every one of those failures in my stomach. I've stared into the abyss with $40 million of debt coming due and no idea how we were going to pay it.

Even then, when I held it up against the things that actually matter, it was a money problem. And money problems have solutions, even when you can't see them yet.

I think that's a big part of why I've been able to keep getting back in the batter's box. If you swing enough times you're going to strike out, and you have to be okay with that.

So if you're a founder heading into the holiday with a knot in your stomach about cash or a buyer or a raise, I've been there (more times than I'd like to admit).

Go sit at the table with the people you love this week. The business will still be there on Monday!

## Post 2: I USED TO THINK ENTREPRENEURS WERE BORN

**CTA: replay + book.** Suggested slot: Mon Nov 23.

Here's something I believed for the first half of my career that turned out to be completely wrong...

I thought entrepreneurs were born. I pictured these swashbuckling Steve Jobs types who came out of the womb ready to bet the house, and I knew I wasn't one of them.

At 38 I had a great job running a group of companies, a newborn at home, and my mom, my sister Barb and my wife Linda all telling me "you can do it, honey."

I felt like I was standing on a high diving board blindfolded, not knowing if there was any water in the pool, with the three of them yelling "jump!"

I couldn't pull the trigger for a long time. And when I finally did, I didn't suddenly become a different person. I just got more afraid of being mediocre than I was of failing.

All these years and 8 companies later, here's what I tell every founder I work with. Entrepreneurs are made, not born. We all start out neutral and the rest is learned.

You learn to be comfortable with ambiguity. You learn to look over the edge without flinching. You learn to run a real business with $3,000 in the bank and somehow be okay with that (I didn't say you learn to enjoy it).

And if you're waiting for everything in your life to be lined up perfectly before you start, you're going to wait far too long. There is never a good time.

I talked about this at the fireside chat for my book launch last week. The replay is here if you missed it: [replay link]

The book is The Cold-Pressed Truth, out now at cpgfoundersgroup.com/book

## Post 3: WE GAVE ONE RETAILER A FULL YEAR EXCLUSIVE

**CTA: none.** Suggested slot: Mon Nov 30.

Okay folks, I want to tell you about a decision we made at Proda that some people would say is stupid...

When we launched our protein soda this past April, we gave one retailer a full year exclusive. One retailer, for a year!

I did it because I've made the opposite mistake before. A few years back I ran a 100-store test with another brand and the velocities came back insane. The retailer said "we want to push you out everywhere," and I said great. We ended up in 20,000 doors (14,000 of them 7-Elevens).

Then the phone rang. "Jeff, what happened? You're turning 70% slower than you did in the test."

What happened is I didn't have anywhere near the marketing budget to pull product off 20,000 shelves. Almost no early-stage brand does.

So with Proda we went to Sprouts and said we want one partner who will lean in with us, and we'll lean in with you. They gave us both ambient and cold placement, and we put everything we had into making those stores work.

The goal was to be the fastest-turning protein soda in the country in the syndicated data, and we got there.

A buyer doesn't care how many doors you're in. They care how fast you turn in the doors you have.

(If you want the long version of this thinking, read Ramping Your Brand by James Richardson. It's the one book besides mine I tell every founder to read.)

## Post 4: I TELL HALF MY TEAM I LOVE THEM

**CTA: none.** Suggested slot: Fri Dec 4.

This one might sound a little soft coming from a CPA, but here it goes...

I say "love you" to about half the people I work with, and they say it back.

Somebody asked me at my book launch what the secret is to building a team that sticks with you through the ugly stretches, and my answer was that it's the EQ stuff.

I've worked with a lot of very smart people over 30 years, and I've come to believe most of us are within the same range when it comes to raw intellect. Intellect is not the side that's going to win. It's the empathy, the humility and the sense of humor.

For me a lot of it is being willing to laugh at the dumb decisions I've made (and there is no shortage of material). When you're honest about your own mistakes it breaks down the walls, and people start being honest right back.

I can't tell you how many founders have said to me, "that person never told ME that, but they told you." It's not magic. I listen, I don't judge, and I try to be approachable.

It's also why I push decisions down to the lowest level I can in a company. The person closest to the problem is usually just as smart as I am, and they know a lot more about it.

Early in my career a boss wrote in my annual review that I was too much of a "friend manager."

I guess I still am. I want to love the people I work with, and when things get hard (they always do) those are the people who will run through walls with you.

## Post 5: THE LAST CHECKS I WROTE AT ROWDY

**CTA: none.** Suggested slot: Fri Dec 11. Short one.

True story that I don't talk about very often...

A couple of years ago one of my businesses failed. Rowdy Energy was one of my 3 strikeouts, and by the end there was not a lot of cash left (mine included).

But there were people who stayed with me right to the last day. They could have jumped earlier and nobody would have blamed them.

So I paid out their severance for 6 months.

Plenty of people would tell you a failed company doesn't owe anybody anything. But these were people I loved and cared about, and they had my back when it would have been a lot easier not to.

I say this in the book and I'll keep saying it. There is never a wrong time to do the right thing.

How you close a business says as much about you as how you build one. Your people will remember it, and so will you when you look in the mirror.

## Post 6: YOUR BEST SELLER PROBABLY DOESN'T EXIST YET

**CTA: book.** Suggested slot: Mon Dec 7.

Here's something I've seen in just about every brand I've been around...

The top 10 SKUs in the business today weren't in the top 10 when the founder started. That was true at Suja and it's true for most of the brands I know.

At Suja the product that saved the company was a 2oz wellness shot. But I didn't get there in a straight line. To get to shots we went through probiotic waters, then vinegars, then kombucha.

Maybe if I were smarter I could have gone straight from A to Z. But you can't, because you just don't know what's going to hit.

So I always have a plan B and a plan C in the works. It drives my teams a little crazy because it's a lot of extra effort, and I get accused of churning (some of that is warranted, most of it isn't).

Here's how I think about it. Until you're turning at least at the mid-point for your retailer AND you have the gross margin to sustain the business, you're not safe. So you keep working on the next thing.

The hard part is knowing when to pivot. If you do it too often you wear everybody out and confuse your buyers. If you do it too late, well, had we not pivoted to shots there would be no Suja today.

I tell my teams the same thing every year. If we're successful, what you're doing today is not what you'll be doing a year from now. And for the people who came in early, that's the best part.

More on this in The Cold-Pressed Truth, out now at cpgfoundersgroup.com/book

## Post 7: MY BEST TASTE TESTER IS 4 YEARS OLD

**CTA: none.** Suggested slot: Mon Dec 14.

Somebody asked me at my book launch which company was my favorite, and I think they expected me to say Suja...

One of the ones I loved most was a little outdoor grill company called Lynx. It was doing about $10 million in revenue and teetering on bankruptcy when I bought it back in 2006.

It was the first consumer business I ever ran. Before that I was in construction products, where everything we made ended up in a ceiling. Nobody sees it, nobody touches it, and nobody at a dinner party wants to talk about it.

With grills, everybody had an opinion! So we listened and we got creative. We were the first to put a steakhouse-style sear zone in a backyard grill. We added little recessed lights over the knobs so you could cook at night, and a push-and-turn ignition like the range in your kitchen.

About 3 years later I sold it for around $35 million.

That's when I fell in love with consumer products, because people will tell you exactly what they think if you ask them.

These days my best taste tester is my 4-year-old grandson. He has no filter and he's not worried about hurting Babu's feelings. If it's bad, I know in about 2 seconds.

Most founders I work with are testing their product on people who love them, and those people are going to be nice about it.

Go find your 4-year-old!

## Post 8: I RAISED $125 MILLION. I NEEDED $50 MILLION.

**CTA: resources.** Suggested slot: Mon Dec 21 or first week of January. **Hold until Jeff confirms the numbers (section 4).**

I did a postmortem on Suja, and one number still bothers me...

We raised about $125 million to get that business to profitability. Roughly half of it went into manufacturing. We outgrew three plants before we landed in the fourth one (I call it our final resting place, and it's a beautiful 200,000 square foot facility).

Here's the number that bothers me. If I had gotten our gross margin to 40% by the end of year 2, I would have only needed to raise about $50 million.

That's $75 million of dilution, and I can't tell you how many months of my life on the road pitching investors, that I could have skipped.

And I should have known better, because I'm a CPA by background!

But when you're growing 200% a year, everybody is looking at the top line (including me). Then one day you look up and you've been spending half your time fundraising instead of running the business. It's exhausting and it's a distraction.

So I'm a little manic about this now with every brand I coach or start. Get your gross margin as high as you can, as fast as you can. Every point you add is money you don't have to go raise.

Revenue is exciting, but gross margin is transformative.

If you want to see where your margin is leaking, the free tools are here: cpgfoundersgroup.com/resources
