## 1. "The Target Buyers In The Bed" — Tue Aug 25

**Pillar: Suja War Story / Relationships**

I want to tell you about a dinner I'll never forget.

It's 2015 and the Target buying team is flying out to see our manufacturing facility in San Diego. Target was one of the most important relationships we had at Suja, so we decided not to do the usual thing. No plant tour, no conference room, no deck.

We invited them to stay overnight and have dinner at our house.

Linda cooked. My mother-in-law Rachel made this beautiful Challah bread, braided and golden, and somehow she had worked both the Coca-Cola and Suja logos right into the design. When it came out of the kitchen the whole table went quiet.

There were cocktails. There was real laughter, the kind you cannot schedule.

Later that night I was giving everybody a tour of the house, and one of the rooms has this oversized bed in it, and somebody said we should all pile in for a photo.

So we did. Buyers, founders, sales team, everyone. (Harmless fun, and yes, my wife was right there with us. 😉)

That photo did not close a single deal.

But here's something I tell every founder I work with about buyers.

They are not gatekeepers protecting shelf space out of spite. They are businesspeople under enormous pressure to grow their categories. A Whole Foods buyer might have 62 cold-pressed juice brands asking for a meeting in the same season. She cannot possibly see them all, so she is going to prioritize the brands where the relationship is already real.

That trust is the thing a deal can't buy. It means when your velocity dips, they call you before they cut you. It means when there's a shelf reset, somebody in that room fights for your facings.

Most founders send the samples, fill out the form, build the deck and hope.

Hope is not a strategy.

Feed people. Show them your operation. Let them meet the team that actually makes the stuff.

You'd be amazed what a loaf of bread and an oversized bed can do for a business.

## 2. "I Said Yes To A Smoothie I Didn't Want To Make" — Thu Aug 27

**Pillar: Retail Strategy / Deep**

Here's one of the not-obvious decisions I made at Suja that I would make again tomorrow.

Walmart called. They wanted us to produce a custom, Walmart-exclusive plant-based smoothie. Their formula, their specs, our production capacity, our team's time.

My operations people hated it from the first sentence. The economics were unattractive, the production run was inefficient, and it was not core to anything we were building.

I said yes anyway.

And Walmart is still one of Suja's largest customers today. Not because of the smoothie. Because the smoothie was the trade.

That distinction is everything in private label. Most founders get that call, feel the flattery of a giant retailer wanting to work with them, and then either panic and refuse or say yes enthusiastically without doing the math on either side. Both of those are expensive.

So when your call comes, get honest about what's actually on the table.

They're asking for your formula, your manufacturing infrastructure, your team's attention, and your working capital for tooling and minimum runs. They're offering revenue, usually at thinner margins than your branded business, often with less predictable volume, and always with their name on it instead of yours.

The volume math almost always looks fine on the surface. More cases, better absorption of fixed costs, and extra revenue is extra revenue.

What that math never includes is opportunity cost. What did that production slot and that month of your team cost you somewhere else?

Revenue without margin is ego. That's true across your whole P&L but it is most true right here.

I'm not telling you to say no. I said yes and I'd say yes again.

I'm telling you to know exactly what you're buying with it before you sign. If the answer is "the best relationship in the building at the biggest retailer on earth," that can be worth every point of margin you give up.

If the answer is just "more revenue," walk away.

## 3. "Our Kombucha Was Making 12%" — Tue Sep 1

**Pillar: Financial / Suja War Story**

Let me tell you about one of the quietest and most expensive mistakes I made at Suja.

We launched a kombucha.

Not because consumers were asking Suja for kombucha. Because everyone in the natural channel was launching kombucha. GT's was everywhere. Health-Ade was everywhere. The category was on fire and we did not want to miss it.

The product was genuinely good. We'd actually solved a hard technical problem that most brands in the category struggled with, which was keeping alcohol levels consistent.

Here's what I ignored.

Consumers did not see Suja as a kombucha brand. And we were outsourcing production to a co-manufacturer, which put the gross margin at roughly 12%.

Our wellness shots, which we produced internally, were running about 60%.

Both were on the shelf. Both were selling. One was quietly making us and one was quietly bleeding us, and the blended number hid the whole thing from me.

That's the product mix trap, and it's worth checking for this week.

Most founders look at blended gross margin. Blended margin can hide the fact that your fastest-growing SKU is also your most margin-destructive one. It can hide one outsourced product dragging the entire company's economics down. It can make you feel like you're building something healthy when you're really subsidizing a bad bet with the profits from a good one.

When our margins were sitting below 32% in late 2018, I was convinced we had a cost problem.

We had a mix problem.

So pull your P&L apart by SKU. Not by category, not by channel. By SKU. Then be honest with yourself about which products you're keeping because they're working, and which ones you're keeping because killing them would feel like admitting something.

We cut the kombucha. Margins went from 32% to 40%.

Gross margin determines destiny.

## 4. "What's Your Gross-To-Net?" — Thu Sep 3

**Pillar: Financial / Technical**

Okay folks, let's talk about the number that's hiding inside your P&L.

I sat down with a founder recently who was genuinely lit up. Eight million dollars in annual revenue, multiple retail accounts, growing fast. He wanted to talk about Series A timing.

I asked him one question. "What's your gross-to-net?"

He looked at me like I'd switched languages mid-sentence.

Here's the thing. That $8 million is not $8 million. After retailer margin, distributor markup, trade spend, promotional funding, deductions and chargebacks, he was looking at something closer to $4.2 million actually reaching his business.

Nearly half the revenue he thought he had was spoken for before his product ever left the warehouse.

Let me walk the waterfall for a conventional grocery item so you can run yours.

You set MSRP at $5.99. The retailer needs 35 to 40%, so they're buying from distribution around $3.50. Your distributor takes 25 to 30% on cost, so they're buying from you around $2.60. That's your invoice price before trade.

Then trade spend. A TPR, a BOGO, a feature ad. If you're spending 15 to 25% of gross revenue on trade, which is normal in most categories, there goes another 40 to 65 cents a unit.

Then deductions. Chargebacks, compliance fines, markdown money, late fees. Not every one of them is legitimate, but fighting them takes time and money you may not have. Budget 3 to 5% of revenue here and you won't get caught off guard.

Then spoilage and returns. We lived this one. We took on spoilage risk with a distributor early, trusted the relationship without enough documentation, and ninety days later over a million dollars in spoilage deductions hit us all at once. It required an unplanned emergency raise.

Add it all up and that $5.99 bottle might deliver you somewhere between $1.80 and $2.20 of net realized revenue. Everything from your COGS to your marketing budget to your payroll has to fit inside that number.

Gross revenue is the number you quote at a dinner party. Net revenue is what your business actually gets to work with.

Know both cold. Investors will ask, and the founders who can answer in one breath are the ones who get taken seriously.

## 5. "We Paid Almost $1M To Get Into Albertsons" — Tue Sep 8

**Pillar: Retail / Rowdy War Story**

I've paid for shelf space I couldn't keep, and I want to tell you what it cost.

At Rowdy Energy we wrote nearly a million dollars in one-time slotting fees to get into Albertsons. Conventional grocery, big opportunity, and we went in with real conviction and a real check. The team was pumped. The income statement looked great. We had distribution.

Eight months later they discontinued us. Velocity was below their hurdle. The slot was gone, the money was gone, and we had nothing to show for it.

I'm not telling you slotting fees are evil. Slotting is just the cost of doing business at certain retail levels and that is the reality of our industry.

What that experience burned into me is something I've now watched play out across eight companies.

You can pay your way onto a shelf. You cannot pay your way into staying on one.

And here's the part I'd genuinely do differently. We were measuring revenue. We should have been measuring contribution.

Almost nobody builds a Customer P&L, and it's one of the highest-leverage hours you can spend. It's a retailer-by-retailer income statement showing what each account actually contributes after every cost is stripped out. Not blended gross margin. By account.

I was with a founder recently who'd crossed $5 million across three retailers, two natural and one conventional. Big smile on his face. I asked which one was actually the most profitable.

Long pause. "We're growing in all three."

That's not an answer, and he knew it.

You can have an account doing $800,000 a year and be losing money on it. I've seen it more than once. The revenue shows up on the income statement and the margin quietly disappears into trade spend, deductions, demos, freight, broker fees, and slotting that nobody ever traced back to the account.

So before you write a slotting check, do the math on what velocity you'd need to earn it back. Then be honest about whether you have the marketing dollars to drive that velocity in those specific stores.

If you don't, that check isn't an investment. It's a deposit on a shelf you're going to lose.

## 6. "What's A Cat Man?" — Thu Sep 10

**Pillar: Retail / Suja War Story**

True story that my team still gives me grief about.

The year was 2014. Suja had just landed major distribution at Whole Foods, Costco, and Target. We'd gone from $600,000 in revenue in 2012 to $44 million in two years and we were not slowing down.

I walked into a team meeting feeling about as good as a founder can feel and said something like, "I can't wait until we achieve full distribution and have nothing left to worry about."

My head of sales Nicky gave me a look I came to know very well. The one that means *Jeff, you have no idea what you're talking about.*

"Two years from now," she said, "we'll be fighting tooth and nail to maintain our shelf space. We'll need the right cat man to win."

I stared at her and said, "What's a cat man?"

They have never let me forget it.

Here's what Nicky understood that I did not yet. Getting into retail is a transaction. Staying in retail is a relationship, and that relationship gets stress tested once or twice a year in something called the line review.

A line review is when the retailer resets the category. They pull the whole set apart, look at every brand's performance against the category, and decide who gets more facings, who gets fewer, and who's gone.

Most founders don't think about the line review until they're sitting in one. By then it's usually too late.

The brands that win line reviews are not the ones with the best pitch on the day. They're the ones who spent the previous eleven months building the case with the buyer. Velocity data. Category growth. What your brand brings to the shelf that isn't already there.

Category management, the cat man function, is the discipline that builds that case in the language the retailer actually speaks.

It sounds like a big-company thing you'll get to later.

It's actually the thing that decides whether you're still on the shelf next spring.

## 7. "I Almost Killed The Coca-Cola Deal" — Tue Sep 15

**Pillar: Fundraising / Suja War Story**

I want to tell you about the moment I nearly blew up the Coca-Cola deal.

July 2015. We were in the final stages of what became one of the most significant deals in the natural beverage space. Coca-Cola valued Suja at $300 million. They were writing a $90 million check. Goldman Sachs was writing another $60 million. From the outside it looked like the deal of a lifetime, and honestly it felt like it too.

Then our attorney walked me through the preference stack.

The way the terms were structured, nearly all of Coca-Cola's $90 million would flow straight to a private equity fund that had been in the deal for less than twelve months. Not to the angels who believed in us before we had a dollar of institutional money. Not to the founders who built the thing from nothing.

Some of those angels had written checks before we had a co-manufacturer, before we had consistent shelf life, before any of it felt real. And they were going to see almost nothing out of a $90 million investment from the most recognizable beverage company on the planet.

I sat with that information and decided we could not close the deal as structured.

We went back and restructured it. It was uncomfortable and it cost us time we didn't really have.

I have never regretted it for a second.

Here's why I'm telling you. Founders talk about their raise. They talk about their valuation. They post the round announcement. Almost nobody talks about the actual mechanics of the cap table, usually because they don't fully understand what they signed and the realization arrives at the worst possible moment.

I've watched founders sell for $200 million and walk away with 4% of it. Eight million dollars before taxes after ten years of 80-hour weeks. That's not failure, that's poor equity management, and it's far more common than you'd think.

Equity feels cheap when you're desperate for validation. An advisor wants 2%. A consultant wants 1%. Somebody who made an intro wants half a point. Each piece feels small, and then one day you're closing a Series B and you own 18% of your own company.

Two numbers worth holding onto.

Try not to dilute more than 10 to 15% in any single round. If you're raising $1 million and giving up 30%, the math is telling you something about either your valuation or your timing.

And try to hold 50% or more through your first two rounds, so you've got room to dilute later without landing in single digits at exit.

Go read your preference stack. Not the summary your lawyer emails you. The actual waterfall, with real exit numbers plugged in.

Do it before you need to know.

## 8. "3,000% In One Year" — Thu Sep 17

**Pillar: Operations / Technical**

In 2013 Suja went from $600,000 in annual revenue to $18 million in a single year.

That's not a typo. We grew 3,000% in twelve months.

Sounds like the best problem in the world. Let me tell you what it actually felt like inside the building.

We were making cold-pressed juice with a 24-day shelf life. Every bottle we produced was a live bet that a consumer would find it, want it, and buy it before it expired. We were processing 1.5 million pounds of fruits and vegetables every week. And our demand forecasting at the time was, let's call it, optimistic.

We over-produced and watched product expire. We under-produced and couldn't fill Whole Foods orders. We made promises we couldn't keep, and promises we kept but barely.

That year burned demand planning into my DNA, because when your product dies in 24 days and you're growing 30% month over month, a bad forecast isn't an inconvenience. It's an existential threat.

Most founders I talk to now have the same problem in a different costume, and it shows up two ways.

The first is the eternal stockout. You land the retailer, orders come in, you're thrilled, and then you can't fill them. Buyers do not care why you're out of stock. They care that the shelf is empty, because an empty shelf makes them look bad to their boss and makes you look unreliable to them. Delivery performance below 95% is a red flag. Below 90% and you're actively shortening your time on that shelf.

The second is the inventory graveyard. You get excited about a launch, go big on the production run to hit your cost-per-unit target, and figure you'll sell through in three months. Six months later you've got $400,000 in inventory and $90,000 in cash, and your CFO is trying to explain how that happened.

That inventory isn't an asset at that point. It's cash wearing a costume.

Neither of these is bad luck. Both are planning failures, and both get fixed by a forecast you update every week against real sell-through instead of the number you promised your board back in January.

## 9. "Costco Wants $1,000 Per Club Per Week" — Tue Sep 22

**Pillar: Retail / Technical**

Let's talk about Costco, because I don't think most founders understand what they're actually signing up for.

A Costco buyer called our San Diego office and wanted to run a head-to-head test. Suja's wellness shot against a competitor's, side by side, in clubs across the Pacific Northwest. And oh, by the way, could we also develop a plant-based milk? They wanted to test that too.

My production team's reaction was not enthusiasm. We had never made plant-based milk. It was outside everything we did well, operationally messy, and a genuine distraction.

We did it anyway.

The milk struggled. The wellness shot did about $2,000 per club per week.

That whole experience crystallized how I think about Costco. It is simultaneously the most rewarding and the most unforgiving channel in CPG. Rewarding because the volume and velocity, when you get it right, are unlike anything else in this industry. Unforgiving because Costco does not have time for products that aren't performing. You perform or you don't, and you find out fast.

Here's the number that should reframe things for you.

A mid-tier grocery account expects your product to move maybe $15 per store per week. At that velocity you keep the shelf and maybe you grow.

Costco expects $1,000 per club per week. Minimum.

That's roughly sixty-seven times the volume. Let that sit for a second before you go chasing a Costco meeting.

Because everything about your business has to be built for that number. Your co-man capacity. Your working capital. Your pack configuration. Your ability to fund demos across a whole region. If any one of those isn't ready, Costco does not wound you slowly the way grocery does.

Costco is not a grocery store with bigger boxes. It's a different business, and the brands that win there built for it on purpose.

## 10. "Dollar General Has 19,000 Doors" — Thu Sep 24

**Pillar: Retail / Technical**

A founder told me she was close to closing Dollar General and I didn't say congratulations.

I said, "walk me through the unit economics first."

She paused. She hadn't done them. She'd gotten a meeting, the buyer seemed interested, and she assumed that meant it was a good idea.

That's how a lot of good brands end up in trouble in the dollar channel.

Let me give you the scale first, because it is genuinely seductive. Dollar General has more than 19,000 stores. Dollar Tree and Family Dollar combined have another 17,000. That's more retail doors than Walmart and Target put together. For a brand doing a few million in revenue, that call feels like the big break.

Sometimes it is. More often it's the start of a slow-motion margin collapse and a positioning problem that takes years to undo.

Here's the math nobody runs before the meeting.

In natural grocery your 12-ounce beverage might retail at $4.99, and if you've built it right you're running 45 to 50% gross margin. You've got room for trade, for the distributor's cut, for a slotting hit, and you still have a real business.

Now put that same product in Dollar General. Their price architecture for food and beverage runs at $1.25, $2, $3, or $4. If your product retails at $4.99 elsewhere and DG prices it at $3, your landed economics just got repriced against a ceiling that was never designed for you.

Your COGS didn't change. Freight didn't get cheaper, and their DC compliance requirements are demanding. But you're now selling into a price point that compresses your gross margin by 15 to 20 points. A brand running 45% in natural might be running 25 to 28% in the dollar channel, if they're lucky.

Then there's the part that never shows up on a spreadsheet. Your consumer sees your product at $3 in Dollar General and $4.99 in Whole Foods, and you have just taught them which one is the real price.

None of this means never. Some brands are built for that channel from day one, with the pack size and cost structure to match.

It means don't let the door count do your thinking for you. Run the unit economics before you take the meeting, not after.

## 11. "The Green Juice Guy On A Skateboard" — Tue Sep 29

**Pillar: Suja Origin Story**

There's a guy in my world I call the green juice guy.

His name is Eric Ethans, and in his early twenties he was making cold-pressed juice at a raw food restaurant in Encinitas, California. He had about 10 recipes. Every day he'd load up those bottles and deliver them door to door in his neighborhood, on his skateboard.

That was his consumer research. Informal, sure, but it was real. He was watching people drink his product. Seeing their faces. Getting repeat orders, or not getting them.

When my partner James Brennan started bringing one of those bottles into my office, I refused it. Multiple times. I was busy, I didn't want kale, I had other things going on.

Eventually I tried it. Three ingredients. Apple, kale, lemon.

I called Linda that night and said, "honey, if I can love a product with kale in it, with my unrefined Midwestern palate, then anyone on the planet can."

That became Suja's Green Supreme.

That gut reaction was consumer research in its rawest, most honest form, and here's why I keep telling this story.

Most founders do the research backwards. They spend $250,000 on packaging, co-packing runs, slotting fees and broker agreements, and then they find out at retail that the price point is too high, the flavor profile is off, or the format doesn't work for how people actually shop.

By then you've written the checks and made the commitments. And the feedback you needed was available months earlier for almost nothing.

You do not need a research firm to start. You need people who are not your friends and not your family putting money down and coming back for more.

Farmers markets. A pop-up. One independent grocer who'll let you demo on a Saturday. Door to door on a skateboard if that's what you've got.

Customers don't reward passion. They reward value.

Go find out which one you're actually selling before you write the big check.

## 12. "I Called Seth Goldman" — Thu Oct 1

**Pillar: Mentorship / Suja War Story**

In 2015 Coca-Cola had just written us a $90 million check, and we were getting killed online.

Organic consumers do not love it when a juice brand they trust gets bought into by the largest beverage company in the world. The comments were brutal. The headlines were worse. Rightfully so, from where they were sitting. I needed to figure out how to respond and I needed to do it fast.

So I called Seth Goldman, co-founder of Honest Tea, which Coca-Cola had also acquired years earlier. He'd lived this exact moment, and his advice came immediately.

"Don't argue with customers. Invite them to watch. Tell them to give you a year. Ask them to judge you by your actions, not by their assumptions."

I wrote it down. Then I called John Foraker, who'd run General Mills' natural sector and had navigated corporate acquisitions at scale.

He said almost exactly the same thing.

Two different people. Zero coordination. Identical answer. That's when I knew what to do.

That is what a real advisory relationship feels like. Not a name on a slide. Not a logo in a deck. Someone who has already been in the foxhole you're about to walk into, who picks up the phone when everything is on fire.

Most founders build advisory boards backwards. They collect impressive names, put the logos on slide 14, schedule a kickoff call, and then the advisor doesn't hear from them for six months until a warm intro is needed.

Build it the other way around.

Start with the three or four moments you know are coming in the next 24 months. Your first conventional retailer. Your first real raise. A co-man transition. An acquisition conversation.

Then go find the person who has already done that specific thing, and ask them for one call about that one thing.

You'll learn very quickly who actually shows up.

And when you find the ones who do, keep them close and actually use them. An advisor you never call is just a logo, and a logo has never once saved a company.

## 13. "Do You Know What Your Brand Looks Like On Instacart?" — Tue Oct 6

**Pillar: Digital / Technical**

A Whole Foods buyer said something to me in 2019 that stopped me cold.

"Jeff, do you know what your brand looks like on Instacart?"

I thought I did. We were in every major Whole Foods in the country, our shelf sets were dialed, our velocity was strong. I assumed the digital version of our presence tracked with the physical one.

It did not.

She pulled up her phone right there in the meeting and showed me. No lifestyle image. A generic product description somebody had clearly written in thirty seconds. And we were buried on page four of the cold-pressed juice results, behind brands that were nowhere close to our velocity on the actual shelf.

She wasn't criticizing us. She was genuinely trying to help. But the thing that rattled me was this: the brands beating us on that screen weren't beating us on product. They were playing a different game and they knew the rules.

Here's the part most founders miss.

On a physical shelf, your placement gets negotiated once or twice a year and then it holds. On the digital shelf, your placement is recalculated constantly, based on content quality, search relevance, ratings, and availability.

Which makes it one of the only places in retail where you can improve your position this month without asking anyone's permission.

So go pull up your own brand on Instacart right now, the way a customer would. Search the category, not your brand name, and see where you land.

Then look at your images, your titles, your descriptions, your ingredient panels, and whether the items showing as out of stock are actually out of stock.

Most brands I look at are losing on that screen for reasons that take a week and almost no money to fix.

We were one of them, and we were doing nine figures at the time. 😊

## 14. "73% Of Revenue Came From Repeat Customers" — Thu Oct 8

**Pillar: DTC / Technical**

I put together a session for our founder group a while back and one slide has stuck with me ever since.

Fifteen or so CPG founders in the room, early and mid-stage, all trying to figure out digital. Ryan runs Amazon strategy for a beverage brand I co-invest in. Three years on the platform, roughly $500,000 a month. He pulls up a cohort analysis and there's one number on it.

73% of total revenue coming from repeat customers.

Not 30%. Not 50%. Seventy-three percent. That's about $4.3 million out of $6 million in annual sales, from people who had bought before. First-time buyers generated the other 27%, and they matter, but they were not the business. The business was the person who bought in March, came back in April, and came back again in May.

Here's the twist that breaks most retail-trained brains, mine included at first.

On that very first sale, they lose money. The unit economics do not go positive until the second or third purchase. By the time somebody has bought four or more times, they're worth $58 in cumulative profit above what it cost to acquire them, and the lifetime value of a 4x-plus customer is $293.

If you come from a retail-first background, everything in you resists this. In retail you think in velocity, units per store per week, and every unit should be margin-positive from day one because the sell-through has to work right now.

DTC does not work like that. And if you run it like retail, you will kill your own channel in month two by turning off the spend right when it was about to start paying you back.

So before you put another dollar into Meta, go find two numbers. What percentage of last quarter's revenue came from repeat buyers, and what your customer is worth on their fourth order.

If you don't know those two, you're not running a DTC business yet. You're running a series of expensive first dates.

## 15. "The Most Beautiful Brand I've Ever Seen Was Running 28%" — Tue Oct 13

**Pillar: Product / Technical**

A founder I was advising a couple years back had one of the most beautiful brands I've ever seen. Stop-you-in-the-aisle beautiful.

Custom glass with a proprietary shape. Embossed labels. Matte finish with a spot gloss logo on the front panel. Every detail dialed in.

First impression: this person really gets it.

Second impression, once I looked at the financials: they really don't.

They were running 28% gross margin with packaging that belonged on a 55% gross margin brand. And the math was broken because every packaging decision had been made on aesthetics, without anyone ever asking what it cost per unit.

That's one of the most expensive traps I see early-stage founders fall into, and it's expensive because it's so hard to undo. Changing your package after launch means new tooling, new artwork, new co-man qualification, and a conversation with a buyer about why your item doesn't look like the one they approved.

Your packaging has to do two completely different jobs at the same time.

It has to stop a shopper walking past a 48-foot gondola and earn the trial. Three seconds, maybe less. Color, shape, a clear benefit, your brand name readable from four feet away.

And it has to fit inside a cost structure that leaves you enough margin to actually run a business.

Most founders design for the first job and find out about the second one when they're already committed.

Here's the practical fix. Before you brief a designer, decide what your packaging is allowed to cost as a percentage of your COGS. Then hand the designer that constraint on day one, right alongside the brand brief.

Great designers do their best work inside a real constraint. They just need somebody to give them one.

Your package is your best salesperson, and it's also one of the most leverageable lines in your entire cost structure. You get to decide which of those two facts you find out about first.

## 16. "The PO You Can't Fill" — Thu Oct 15

**Pillar: Fundraising / Technical**

Here's a moment almost every CPG founder hits, and almost nobody talks about it.

There's a purchase order sitting on your desk. Could be Whole Foods, could be Target, could be Costco. More revenue than you've ever seen in a single order. Your hands are shaking a little as you read the line items.

And you cannot fill it.

Not because the product isn't ready and not because the relationship isn't there. Because you don't have the cash to buy raw materials, pay the co-man, and bridge the 60 to 90 days before the retailer actually pays you.

So you do what most founders do. You start thinking about your next equity round. You call investors, build a deck, take the meetings. And quietly, without quite noticing it happen, you trade away another 15 or 20% of your company to solve what was fundamentally an inventory financing problem.

I've watched this play out over and over, and I want to give you a different way to think about it.

Equity is forever. A purchase order loan is not.

Run the math on what that "cheap" equity actually costs. If your company eventually exits at $50 million, every single point you gave up early to bridge a cash gap is worth $500,000 at exit. Twenty points is $10 million. To cover an inventory build.

There's a whole set of tools built for exactly this problem that most CPG founders never look at. Purchase order financing. Inventory lines. Receivables factoring. Revenue-based financing. Equipment leasing instead of buying. State and USDA programs depending on what you make and where you make it. Even negotiated terms with your co-man, which costs you nothing except a hard conversation.

None of it is free, and some of it is expensive. That's fine. Expensive money you pay back beats cheap money you never get back.

Match the financing to the problem. Working capital problems deserve working capital solutions.

Save your equity for what equity is actually for, which is building the team and the brand that make the whole thing worth owning.

## 17. "Getting Picked Up Isn't Getting Distributed" — Tue Oct 20

**Pillar: Distribution / Technical**

There's a call every emerging CPG founder gets at some point, and I want to prepare you for what comes after it.

Your broker calls, slightly breathless. "Good news, UNFI is picking us up."

You hang up. You text your co-founder. Maybe you crack a beer. It feels like you won something.

Six months later you're staring at a deduction report that looks designed to confuse you, your fill rate has slipped below 90%, and three Whole Foods regions are showing zero inventory on your top SKU.

We worked with both KeHE and UNFI at Suja, and honestly we made every mistake in the book before we figured out how to run those relationships well. Nobody tells you the rules, so let me try.

First, a distributor is not a broker. Founders conflate these constantly.

Your broker represents your brand in front of buyers. They're your sales arm, they carry your story into the room and fight for your shelf space. Presence Marketing and Bill Weiland were essential to Suja's early Whole Foods launch, and Bill was extraordinarily selective about which brands he'd take on. That selectivity was the signal.

A distributor is a warehouse operation. They receive your product from your co-man, hold it in regional DCs, and truck it to retailers on their route. They are the physical link between your production floor and the cooler.

Here's what founders miss.

Getting picked up by UNFI is not the same thing as getting distributed by UNFI.

Getting picked up means they'll carry your SKU in their system. Getting distributed means retailers are actually ordering it, UNFI is actually shipping it, and product is actually turning. The space between those two things is where a lot of brands die quietly, still telling investors they have national distribution.

So the week after you get picked up, the work starts, it doesn't end. Know your fill rate. Know your turns by DC. Know which regions are ordering and which ones have never placed a second order. And have somebody whose actual job is chasing deductions.

Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains. They are completely different scoreboards, and only one of them keeps you alive.

## 18. "International Isn't A Reward. It's A Stress Test." — Thu Oct 22

**Pillar: Growth Strategy / Deep**

Every founder has had this moment.

You're sitting across from somebody at a trade show, or an inbound email lands from a distributor in Toronto or a chain in London, and they tell you they've been watching your brand, they love what you're doing, and they want to bring it to their market.

Your pulse picks up. Global brand. Flags on the map.

I've been there. I've also watched a dozen founders walk through that door and come out five years later with burned cash, wrecked operations, and a domestic business that quietly fell apart while they were busy chasing it.

Here's what most of them missed on the way in.

International expansion is not a reward for domestic success. It's a stress test. If your domestic flywheel isn't airtight before you try it, it will fail that test loudly and expensively.

I say "don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains" constantly about domestic strategy. Internationally it's an even bigger trap, because getting in is easy and winning is hard.

A brand can get picked up by a Canadian distributor in 90 days and be on shelf at Loblaws or Sobeys within six months. The founder watches a map light up and feels like they're winning.

Then the reorders don't come. The distributor loses interest. Shelves go empty, product sits, expires, gets pulled. The international launch quietly disappears, and the damage comes home in cash and attention you didn't have to spare.

The right question isn't "can we get into that market." It's "can we win in that market."

Winning means somebody is running demos in Manchester. Somebody is managing that distributor every single week. Your label complies with their regs. Your pricing survives the freight and the duty. Your shelf life survives the boat.

If you can't name the person who owns that market on Monday morning, you're not expanding internationally. You're exporting inventory and hoping.

Get your domestic house genuinely airtight first. The opportunity will still be there, and you'll actually be able to hold onto it.

## 19. "Your Broker Can't Name Your Top 3 SKUs" — Tue Oct 27

**Pillar: Sales / Operational**

You hired a broker. Signed the contract, paid the retainer, shook hands, and waited for the magic.

Six months later velocity is flat, your buyer meetings keep getting rescheduled, and your broker rep can't name your top three SKUs from memory.

I've had this conversation with a lot of founders, and I want to reset the expectation, because brokers are genuinely useful and most founders use them wrong.

A broker is a manufacturer's rep who sells your product to retail buyers on your behalf. They know the buyers, the category, and the promotional calendar. They typically take 5% of net revenue to start, with a goal of negotiating toward 3% once you're at real scale.

What they are not is a brand builder, a silver bullet, or a substitute for your own relationship with your retail partners.

And here's the one that bites hardest. Your broker is also repping 80 other brands. When a buyer says no to your product, the broker shrugs, moves to the next SKU on the list, and keeps that relationship warm.

You would push back. You would ask why. You would come in with data and conviction.

They won't, and it isn't because they're bad at their job. It's because they are not going to spend their buyer relationship capital on your one SKU.

I tell every founder I work with the same thing. They'll push the point, but they'll cave much quicker than you would.

That asymmetry isn't a reason to avoid brokers. It's the reason to stay in the room yourself.

So before you sign, get specific. Which buyers, at which chains, by when. What the first 90 days actually looks like. Who the human being on your account is and how often you talk to them.

And don't sign a long commitment out of the gate. If you can't see real movement in three months, that group isn't for you.

Hire the broker for the door. Keep the relationship yourself.

## 20. "The Monday Morning Dashboard" — Thu Oct 29

**Pillar: Financial / Framework**

The week Coca-Cola told us they weren't completing the acquisition, I sat in my office and tried to remember the last time I'd actually looked at our gross margin by channel.

Not a quarterly report. Not a board slide. Our actual weekly number.

I couldn't remember.

We'd gone from zero to $100 million in six years. Named one of the fastest growing companies in America. Over 350 employees, relationships at Whole Foods, Target, Costco, and Walmart.

And somewhere in all that velocity, I had traded the discipline of looking at the business every week for the performance of presenting the business every quarter.

That was a big mistake, and it's the one I see most often in founders who are doing well.

By the time Todd Fisher, our CFO, and I were living inside weekly cash projections trying to figure out whether Suja would survive, I finally understood something I should have understood years earlier.

The data that saves a company is never the data you review after a crisis. It's the data you review every Monday morning before one starts.

Seven numbers. Every week. No exceptions.

1. Cash balance and runway in DAYS, not months. "We have eighteen months" is board meeting language. How much is in the bank today, and how many days does that last at your current burn?
2. Gross margin by channel. Not blended. By channel, so you can see which one is quietly subsidizing the others.
3. Velocity, units per store per week, by retailer. This is the number that decides whether you keep your shelf.
4. Fill rate. Below 95% you're on notice, below 90% you're on the clock.
5. Trade spend as a percentage of gross revenue, actuals against plan.
6. Inventory on hand in weeks of supply, by SKU.
7. Your deduction total for the week, and how much of it you're actually disputing.

None of this requires an analytics team or a BI platform. It requires discipline, which in my experience is exactly the thing founders resist most when things are going well and want most when they aren't.

Put an hour on your calendar every Monday morning, before anybody else needs you.

It's the cheapest insurance policy in this business.
