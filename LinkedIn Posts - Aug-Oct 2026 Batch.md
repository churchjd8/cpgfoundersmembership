## 2. "40% Is The Floor, Not The Ceiling" — Tue Sep 1

**ADVICE — previews webinar topic 8**

Okay folks, let's talk about the one number that decides whether you survive in this wild jungle that is CPG industry.

It's not revenue, not door count, and not TikTok followers. It's...Gross margin!

And I see this all the time especially with smart business people taking their first spin at a CPG brand...because the way you list your chart of accounts and calculate your gross margin in CPG is different than any other industry. Let me break it down for you.

For starters, gross margin is what's left after you pay for the product itself. Ingredients, packaging, co-man fees, and inbound freight to your warehouse. It's the money that funds everything else, your team, your marketing, your trade spend, your growth.

If it's too thin, nothing else works. You can't afford to promote. You can't afford to hire. You can't afford to raise at a valuation that doesn't wreck your equity.

First, calculate it correctly, because I see founders get this wrong constantly.

Gross margin equals net revenue minus COGS, divided by NET revenue. The key word is net, meaning after trade spend and retailer deductions. If you're calculating margin on gross revenue you're overstating it and making decisions off a number that isn't real.

What goes into COGS: ingredients, packaging, co-manufacturing fees, and inbound freight from your co-man to your warehouse.

What does not go into COGS: outbound freight to retailers, trade spend (that's contra-revenue), slotting fees, and broker commissions. Those are all operating expenses.

Get your chart of accounts right on day one. When investors look at your financials they expect a CPG-standard income statement, and if freight is sitting in your COGS your margin looks artificially low and you're not comparable to anything.

Now the benchmarks.

Year one, 40% is the minimum. At 40% you've got room to fund basic trade spend at 15 to 20% of net revenue, cover your operating expenses, and still have a path to profitability.

Year three, 50% is the target. As you scale, COGS should come down through volume-based pricing from co-mans, better ingredient sourcing, and packaging optimization. The best brands in this business run 50 to 60%.

Below 35% is the danger zone. After trade spend and opex you're almost certainly burning cash faster than you should be. Fix the margin structure before you scale, not after.

I know 40% sounds impossible when you're sitting at 31% staring at a co-man invoice. I've been there. I've also been on the other side of it, running a company doing $100 million in revenue at sub-32% margins, and I can tell you that revenue does not save you.

Gross margin determines destiny.

Go calculate yours correctly this week. ## 3. "What's A Cat Man?" — Thu Sep 3

**STORY — supports webinar topic 11**

My head of sales Nicky gave me a look I came to know very well. The one that means *Jeff, you have no idea what you're talking about.*

The year was 2014. Suja had just landed major distribution at Whole Foods, Costco, and Target. We'd gone from $600,000 in revenue in 2012 to $44 million in two years and we were not slowing down.

I walked into a team meeting feeling about as good as a founder can feel and said something like, "I can't wait until we achieve full distribution and have nothing left to worry about."

That's when the look from Nicky came flying in. 

"Two years from now," she said, "we'll be fighting tooth and nail to maintain our shelf space. We'll need the right cat man to win."

I stared at her and said, "What's a cat man?"

She has never let me forget it.

Here's what Nicky understood that I did not yet. Getting into retail is a transaction. Staying in retail is a relationship, and that relationship gets stress tested once or twice a year in something called the line review.

A line review is when the retailer resets the category. They pull the whole set apart, look at every brand's performance against the category, and decide who gets more facings, who gets fewer, and who's gone.

Most founders don't think about the line review until they're sitting in one. By then it's usually too late.

The brands that win line reviews are not the ones with the best pitch on the day. They're the ones who spent the previous eleven months building the case with the buyer across velocity data, category growth, and what your brand brings to the shelf that isn't already there.

Category management...the "cat man function"...is the discipline that builds that case in the language the retailer actually speaks.

It sounds like a big-company thing you'll get to later.

It's actually the thing that decides whether you're still on the shelf next spring.

## 4. "What's Your Gross-To-Net?" — Tue Sep 8

**ADVICE — previews webinar topic 3**

There's a metric hiding in your P&L that is a big deal in the CPG world.

I sat down with a founder recently who was lit up. She had two million dollars in annual revenue, multiple retail accounts, growing fast. She brought me in as an advisor to help her with the strategy and planning of her Series A.

I asked her one question. "What's your gross-to-net?"

She gave me the look I've come accostomed to seeing from first time CPG founders.

Let me walk the waterfall for a conventional grocery item so you can run yours.

You set MSRP at $5.99. The retailer needs 35 to 40%, so they're buying from distribution around $3.50. Your distributor takes 25 to 30% on cost, so they're buying from you around $2.60. That's your invoice price before trade.

Then trade spend. A TPR, a BOGO, a feature ad. If you're spending 15 to 25% of gross revenue on trade, which is normal in most categories, there goes another 40 to 65 cents a unit.

Then deductions. Chargebacks, compliance fines, markdown money, late fees. Not every one of them is legitimate, but fighting them takes time and money you may not have. Budget 3 to 5% of revenue here and you won't get caught off guard.

Then spoilage and returns. We lived this one. We took on spoilage risk with a distributor early, trusted the relationship without enough documentation, and ninety days later over a million dollars in spoilage deductions hit us all at once. It required an unplanned emergency raise.

Add it all up and that $5.99 bottle might deliver you somewhere between $1.80 and $2.20 of net realized revenue. Everything from your COGS to your marketing budget to your payroll has to fit inside that number.

Gross revenue is the number you quote at a networking event. Net revenue is what your business actually gets to work with.

Know both cold. Investors will ask, and the founders who can answer in one breath are the ones who get taken seriously.

## 5. "I Almost Killed The Coca-Cola Deal" — Thu Sep 10

**STORY — supports webinar topic 2**

I want to tell you about the moment I nearly blew up the Coca-Cola deal.

July 2015. We were in the final stages of what became one of the most significant deals in the natural beverage space. Coca-Cola valued Suja at $300 million. They were writing a $90 million check. Goldman Sachs was writing another $60 million. From the outside it looked like the deal of a lifetime, and honestly it felt like it too.

Then our attorney walked me through the preference stack.

The way the terms were structured, nearly all of Coca-Cola's $90 million would flow straight to a private equity fund that had been in the deal for less than twelve months. Not to the angels who believed in us before we had a dollar of institutional money. Not to us founders or founding team either.

Some of those angels had written checks before we had a co-manufacturer, before we had consistent shelf life, before any of it felt real. And they were going to see almost nothing out of a $90 million investment from the most recognizable beverage company on the planet.

I sat with that information and decided we could not close the deal as structured.

We went back and restructured it. It was uncomfortable and it cost us time we didn't really have.

I've never regretted it for a second.

Here's why I'm telling you. Founders talk about their raise. They talk about their valuation. They post the round announcement. Almost nobody talks about the actual mechanics of the cap table, usually because they don't fully understand what they signed and the realization arrives at the worst possible moment.

I've watched founders sell for $200 million and walk away with 4% of it. Eight million dollars before taxes after ten years of 80-hour weeks. That's not a failure by any stretch of the imagination, but it is poor equity management, and it's far more common than you'd think.

Equity feels cheap when you're desperate for validation. An advisor wants 2%. A consultant wants 1%. Somebody who made an intro wants half a point. Each piece feels small, and then one day you're closing a Series B and you own 18% of your own company.

Here's two numbers worth holding onto.

Try not to dilute more than 10 to 15% in any single round. If you're raising $1 million and giving up 30%, the math is telling you something about either your valuation or your timing.

And try to hold 50% or more through your first two rounds, so you've got room to dilute later without landing in single digits at exit.

Go read your preference stack. And I recommend reading the actual waterfall with real exit numbers plugged in, not just the summary your lawyer emails you.

## 6. "Know Your Velocity Before Your Buyer Tells You" — Tue Sep 15

**ADVICE — previews webinar topic 11**

I've watched founders come back from a buyer meeting absolutely lit up. "We got into 800 Kroger stores!" "We're in Sprouts nationwide!"

I get it, I really do. Distribution feels like winning. It's tangible, you can point to it on a map, you can post about it.

Here's the part nobody says out loud. Getting distribution is the easiest part.

Distribution tells you how many doors you're in. Velocity tells you whether those doors are doing anything. And the retailer does not care about your door count. They care about dollars per point of distribution, and they are measuring you against every other brand competing for that same shelf space.

Most founders don't know where they stand until a buyer tells them they're out, and by then it's too late to fix.

So here's how to benchmark yourself against your category median. These aren't perfect for every category but they're directionally right across most of food and beverage retail.

At 80% of category median or above, you're in a strong position. You can push for expansion, off-cycle resets, secondary placement.

At 60 to 80%, you'll likely be able to increase SKU count at the next reset. Stay the course and don't get cute.

Around 50%, you're okay. Not thriving, not dying. Don't take your foot off the gas.

At 30 to 50%, you need a real plan, and without one you probably won't make the next reset.

Below 20%, something is seriously wrong. Either the product isn't resonating, the placement is bad, the price point is off, or your marketing spend behind that channel is zero.

Most retailers build planograms for about 12 months with a 6-month refresh. Once you're on shelf you have roughly 9 months to prove you belong, and in a competitive category sometimes less. That is not a long runway.

When velocity struggles, founders blame marketing, demos, or awareness. Sometimes that's right. More often low velocity is a symptom and not the diagnosis, so ask these first.

Is your product in the right section? At Suja one of our early breakthroughs was getting cold-pressed juice moved into the refrigerated produce section at Whole Foods instead of the back refrigerated wall where nobody went. That one placement change transformed velocity in those stores.

Is the price working for that channel? A $9.99 product can fly at Whole Foods and stall at a conventional chain where the competitive set sits at $3.99. We lived that. We went from a 16-ounce bottle at $9.99 to a 12-ounce at $3.99 for mass adoption. Same brand, completely different velocity trajectory.

Is your best SKU actually on the shelf? Sounds obvious. I've walked into countless stores where the top performer was out of stock and the slowest mover was front and center. In-store execution is somebody's job. Make sure it's somebody's.

Is the category itself growing or shrinking? If the whole category is contracting, this might have less to do with your brand than with the macro trend. Know the difference.

To sum it up once again...Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains. Only one of those keeps you on the shelf!!

## 7. "The Green Juice Guy On A Skateboard" — Thu Sep 17

**STORY — supports webinar topic 12**

There's a guy in my world I call the green juice guy.

His name is Eric Ethans, and in his early twenties he was making cold-pressed juice at a raw food restaurant in Encinitas, California. He had about 10 recipes. Every day he'd load up those bottles and deliver them door to door in his neighborhood, on his skateboard.

This was his consumer research. Watching people's faces as they tried the juice and got addicted to how they felt from drinking it.

When my partner James Brennan started bringing one of those bottles into my office, I refused it. I wouldn't touch any green juice with a ten foot pole.After all, you can take the man out of the Midwest but you can't take the Midwest out of the man!!

Eventually I tried it. It was three ingredients: Apple, kale, lemon.

I called Linda that night and said, "honey, if I can love a product with kale in it, with my unrefined Cleveland palate, then anyone on the planet can."

That became Suja's Green Supreme.

That gut reaction was consumer research in its rawest, most honest form, and here's why I keep telling this story.

Most founders do the research backwards. They spend $250,000 on packaging, co-packing runs, slotting fees and broker agreements, and then they find out at retail that the price point is too high, the flavor profile is off, or the format doesn't work for how people actually shop.

By then you've written the checks and made the commitments. And the feedback you needed was available months earlier for almost nothing.

You do not need a research firm to start. You need people who are not your friends and not your family putting money down and coming back for more.

Farmers markets. A pop-up. One independent grocer who'll let you demo on a Saturday. Door to door on a skateboard if that's what you've got.

Customers don't reward passion. They reward value.

Go find out which one you're actually selling before you write the big check.

## 8. "Why Does The Shelf Need You?" — Tue Sep 22

**ADVICE — previews webinar topics 10 & 12**

Here's a pattern I see more than almost any other.

A founder has a product they absolutely love and are passionate about it. They get into a few stores. And then they stall.

Not because the product is bad. Because nobody knows what to think about it.

I'll ask them what they make and I get something like, "we make clean, organic snacks for health-conscious families."

Okay. So does everyone else on that shelf. Why should a consumer reach for you instead of the brand six inches to your left?

Positioning is not your tagline and it's not your packaging copy. It's the answer to one question. 

--> Why you, for who, and why now?

And the reason it matters isn't philosophical, it's financial. Consistent clarity directly impacts velocity, and velocity earns you shelf space. If a buyer and a consumer can't immediately understand why you exist, your turns will show it, and low turns get you pulled from the planogram. That's how good brands die quietly.

Here's the other piece worth sitting with. Products are copyable. Brands are not. Somebody can copy your formula and they can copy your packaging. They cannot copy what you mean to someone.

I've used the same framework to evaluate every opportunity for 30 years. I call it MAP. Market, Audience, Positioning.

Start with Market, and define it in four nested layers instead of one vague one. Industry, category, segment, niche. Not "we're in the health food space," because that's an aspiration, not a market. Beverages, then non-alcoholic beverages, then juice, then organic cold-pressed juice shots. For each layer you want the real market size, the five year CAGR, who the actual competitors are, and how that niche sits as a percentage of the broader market.

That work is what surfaces white space. And white space is rarely "nobody makes this." It's usually a gap in what the category MEANS to people, not a gap in what's on the shelf.

For Suja the white space was premium organic juice that made healthy living feel accessible instead of punishing. Health without the punishment. A Whole Foods juice bar in a bottle.

That's the whole thing in one sentence, and it's the sentence we built the company on.

What you're looking for is a category that's new but not too new. Consumers already understand what they're buying. Retailers are actively expanding shelf space. And the early leaders have made enough mistakes that there's room to improve on something that genuinely matters to somebody.

So here's the exercise. Write your positioning as one sentence in this shape.

We're the only [X] that [Y] for [Z].

If you can't fill in those blanks without hedging, you don't have a marketing problem. You've got work to do before you spend another dollar on packaging.

## 9. "I Said Yes To A Smoothie I Didn't Want To Make" — Thu Sep 24

**STORY — supports webinar topic 6**

Here's one of the not-obvious decisions I made at Suja that I would make again tomorrow.

Walmart called. They wanted us to produce a custom, Walmart-exclusive plant-based smoothie. Their formula, their specs, our production capacity, our team's time.

My operations people hated it from the first sentence. The economics were unattractive, the production run was inefficient, and it was not core to anything we were building.

I said yes anyway.

And Walmart is still one of Suja's largest customers today. Not because of the smoothie. Because the smoothie was the trade.

That distinction is everything in private label. Most founders get that call, feel the flattery of a giant retailer wanting to work with them, and then either panic and refuse or say yes enthusiastically without doing the math on either side. Both of those are expensive.

So when your call comes, get honest about what's actually on the table.

They're asking for your formula, your manufacturing infrastructure, your team's attention, and your working capital for tooling and minimum runs. They're offering revenue, usually at thinner margins than your branded business, often with less predictable volume, and always with their name on it instead of yours.

The volume math almost always looks fine on the surface. More cases, better absorption of fixed costs, and extra revenue is extra revenue.

What that math never includes is opportunity cost. What did that production slot and that month of your team cost you somewhere else?

Revenue without margin is ego. That's true across your whole P&L but it is most true right here.

I'm not telling you to say no. I said yes and I'd say yes again.

I'm telling you to know exactly what you're buying with it before you sign. If the answer is "the best relationship in the building at the biggest retailer on earth," that can be worth every point of margin you give up.

If the answer is just "more revenue," walk away.

## 10. "The Four That Keep Killing Good Brands" — Tue Sep 29

**ADVICE — previews webinar topic 1**

Let's talk about what actually kills early CPG brands, because it's almost never the product.

I've built 8 companies and invested in dozens more over 40 years, and the thing that still gets me is founders getting tripped up by stuff that was completely avoidable. Not obscure regulatory landmines. Basic contract terms nobody bothered to read.

Here are four I'd put on a wall.

**Long-term contracts.** Never sign anything longer than a year when you're early stage, and I don't care how good the terms look. Your business is going to change dramatically in the next twelve months. Your co-man might not be the right fit. Your distributor might not perform. You might pivot your entire product line, and if you're honest with yourself you probably will.

I've watched founders stuck paying a co-manufacturer they'd completely outgrown because they signed a three year deal for a slightly better per-unit price. That savings cost them hundreds of thousands in opportunity cost. If a vendor won't do 12 months, that tells you something about how much they actually value the relationship.

**Evergreen clauses.** This one is sneaky and it catches smart people constantly. An evergreen contract auto-renews unless you give formal written notice, usually 60 to 90 days before the renewal date. Miss that window by a single day and you're locked in for another full term.

One day. I've seen founders accidentally renew broker agreements, warehouse leases, and co-man deals because nobody put the cancellation deadline on a calendar. The day you sign anything with an evergreen clause, put that notice deadline in your calendar with multiple reminders and treat it like a fundraising deadline. Because it is one.

**Trademark conflicts.** You spend two years building awareness, getting into retail, building a following, and then a cease and desist shows up because another food brand in a completely unrelated category has a similar name.

## 12. "What Retail Actually Costs You" — Tue Oct 6

**ADVICE — previews webinar topic 7**

Okay folks, let's talk about what it actually costs to get on a shelf, because the number is almost never the number you were quoted.

Slotting fees are upfront payments you make to a retailer in exchange for shelf space. Some call them new item fees, some call them stocking allowances. The name changes, the math doesn't. You're writing a check before you've sold a single unit.

From the retailer's side it makes complete sense. They have finite shelf space and hundreds of brands knocking, so a slotting fee is a financial filter. It signals you're serious, and it partially offsets their cost if you don't perform.

From your side it's the first real test of whether your unit economics survive contact with retail.

The spectrum is much wider than most founders expect.

In natural, Whole Foods, Sprouts, and most natural independents typically charge little to no formal slotting. That channel rewards distribution-readiness and brand story over financial power. When Suja launched in 50 Southern California Whole Foods stores in 2012, we didn't write a slotting check. We got in on the product, the relationships, and the velocity data.

In conventional it gets real. Kroger, Albertsons, Safeway, Publix can run anywhere from a few thousand per SKU per store cluster to hundreds of thousands across a division. A regional rollout at a mid-size chain might be $50,000 to $200,000 before you see your first sale.

In mass, Walmart is largely slotting-free, but don't mistake that for free. Their everyday low price model requires you to deliver margin, and the cost of meeting their volume expectations is its own kind of commitment. Target has historically charged modest new item fees. Mass usually gets you on trade spend, not slotting.

At club, Costco doesn't charge traditional slotting either. They want a killer product at a killer price with a margin structure that works for both sides. But they'll ask you to fund samples, in-store demos, and promotional discounts, and those add up fast.

Then there are MCBs, manufacturer chargebacks, and this is the line item nobody warns you about.

MCBs are deductions the retailer takes against your invoice. Damaged goods, short shipments, compliance issues, and a dozen other things. New founders see them show up and assume somebody made a mistake.

It's not a mistake. Budget 2 to 5% of gross revenue in MCBs from day one in conventional.

And audit them monthly. Some are legitimate and plenty are not. I've seen brands recover six figures just by disputing deductions they never should have accepted. Most retailers and distributors have a dispute window, and once you miss it that money is gone for good.

Two negotiating moves most founders never make.

Offer scan-down trade instead of upfront cash. Some retailers will accept a per-unit discount off invoice for a defined period, say 12 to 16 weeks, in place of a lump sum. That aligns your cost with your actual sales, so you only pay if the product moves.

And propose a pilot. Instead of paying for 300 stores out of the gate, ask for a 50-store test in your highest-potential markets, show 90 days of velocity, then expand. More buyers take that deal than you'd expect.

Slotting gets you the chance. Everything that comes after it determines whether you keep it.

## 16. "A Wish List With A Spreadsheet Attached" — Tue Oct 20

**ADVICE — previews webinar topics 2 & 3**

Let's talk about the financial model, because I see the same one over and over and it isn't a model.

It goes something like this. "The market is $5 billion. We'll capture 1% of that by year three. That's $50 million." And then there's a revenue line that goes from $2 million to $50 million with no real explanation of how.

Top-down projections feel confident. They feel big. Investors don't believe them because they can't interrogate them. There's no velocity assumption, no door count, no trade spend. It's aspiration formatted as math.

Enthusiasm meets math, and math always wins.

Build from the shelf up instead. Start with what you can actually control and observe, then work outward. Revenue is a result, not a starting point.

Here's the entire revenue engine.

Number of SKUs, times average door count per SKU, times average weekly velocity, times average net selling price, times 52.

That's your revenue line. Not "we think we'll do $8 million next year."

Two SKUs in 400 stores each, turning 6 units per store per week, at a $4.50 net selling price? That's $1.12 million. Write it down, then stress test it. What if velocity drops to 4?
