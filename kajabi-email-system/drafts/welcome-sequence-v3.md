## Email 4 (Day 7) — Problem
**Subject:** the first number I'd look at
**Preview:** it isn't revenue

*(v2 Email 6, MBA pitch moved to PS, ends in a reply)*

Hey {{first_name}},

If you handed me your business and let me look at one number before I said anything, I wouldn't ask about revenue. I'd ask about gross margin.

At Suja we went from zero to $100 million in six years. Faster than KIND, Honest Tea and ZICO. From the outside it looked like a rocket ship. On the inside our gross margins sat under 32%, we were burning $10 million a year, and some weeks we had less than $100,000 in the bank against $40 million of secured debt.

Growth and survival are not the same thing. I learned that one the expensive way.

So the rule I run now is build to 50%. Below about 45%, every dollar of growth costs you money and you're structurally dependent on the next raise. Above 50%, you can fund trade, absorb a co-man mistake, eat a slotting hit, and still be standing.

Three levers, in the order I'd pull them:

- **Formulation and pack.** The cheapest margin you'll ever find is the margin you design in before launch.
- **Co-man terms and MOQs.** Most founders sign the first agreement they're handed because they're relieved somebody said yes. That relief costs points for years.
- **Trade spend.** MCBs, slotting, promos nobody modeled. If you can't tell me your real net price per unit by channel, you're not managing margin...you're hoping.

Eighteen months before Coke walked away, we launched two-ounce wellness shots at roughly 60% gross margin. It felt like a side experiment. It's what saved the company.

So, honest question. What's your gross margin today, and what do you think it is at scale? Reply with the two numbers and I'll tell you whether I'd be worried.

Talk soon,
Jeff

PS The full margin playbook (models, trade spend calculator, all of it) lives in the [MBA for CPG](https://cpgfoundersgroup.com/mba-for-cpg).

## Email 5 (Day 9) — Philosophy
**Subject:** the mistake that quietly kills most raises
**Preview:** fundraising isn't a project

*(adapted from the old Fundraising Masterclass Email 4)*

Hey {{first_name}},

Most founders think about fundraising as a project. Finish the product, then the deck, then go raise.

That's a mistake, because by the time they're raising they're raising scared. Into a headwind, back against the wall, out of time. And scared rounds are expensive rounds.

Here's how I think about it instead. Always be fundraising. I spent half my career on it, and it's never a project you start and finish. The founders who raise well are building investor relationships when they don't need money.

And they protect the cap table like it's oxygen. My ladder:

- Friends and family at no more than 10% dilution
- Angel rounds at 15 to 20% each
- No single round more than 20% dilutive, ever
- By your Series A, you and your founding team still own at least half the company

There's a timing piece too. The best time to raise is off your second-half numbers, not your first. I'm bridging a company right now for nine months so they can raise off a $25M run rate instead of $10M. Same company, same team, same product. Roughly double the valuation.

If you're raising in the next twelve months, this is the conversation to have before you send the first deck, not after. That's exactly what a [Strategy Session](https://cpgfoundersgroup.com/work-with-jeff) is for. We'll go through your model and your timing, and I'll tell you what I'd do.

Talk soon,
Jeff
