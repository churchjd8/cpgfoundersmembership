I had a conversation not too long ago with a founder who was genuinely excited. Eight million dollars in annual revenue. Multiple retail accounts. Growing fast. He wanted to talk about Series A timing.

I asked him one question: "What's your gross-to-net?"

He looked at me like I'd switched languages mid-sentence.

Here's the thing. That $8 million number... it's not really $8 million. By the time you subtract retailer margin, distributor markup, trade spend, promotional funding, deductions, and chargebacks, that founder was looking at something closer to $4.2 million actually reaching his business. Nearly half the revenue he thought he had was already spoken for before his product ever left the warehouse.

This is one of the most dangerous gaps in CPG. And most founders don't even know it exists.

## What Gross-to-Net Actually Means

"Gross revenue" is the number you quote at a dinner party. Net revenue is what your business actually has to work with.

The difference between them is the waterfall. The cascade of concessions, fees, discounts, and deductions that erode your top-line number before a single dollar hits your bank account.

Here's a simplified version of how the waterfall works in conventional grocery.

You set your MSRP at $5.99 for a 12-oz bottle. The retailer requires a 35-40% margin, so they're buying from distribution at roughly $3.50. Your distributor takes 25-30% on cost, so they're buying from you at around $2.60 per unit. That's your "invoice price" before trade.

But then it gets complicated.

**Trade spend.** You're running a promotional event... a temporary price reduction, a BOGO, a feature ad. The retailer pulls that off your invoice. If you're spending 15-25% of gross revenue on trade (which is normal in most retail categories), there goes another $0.40-$0.65 per unit.

**Deductions.** The chargebacks, compliance fines, markdown money, late fees that show up on your remittance. Not every deduction is legitimate. But fighting them takes time and money you might not have. Budget 3-5% of revenue for this category, minimum, and you won't be caught off guard.

**Spoilage and returns.** In the cold-pressed juice world we lived this acutely at Suja. We took on spoilage risk with a distributor early on, trusted the relationship without enough documentation, and ninety days later we had over a million dollars in spoilage deductions hitting us at once. That required an unplanned emergency raise. That kind of hit can break a young company.

Add it all up. That $5.99 MSRP might deliver you somewhere between $1.80 and $2.20 of net realized revenue per unit in conventional grocery. Everything from your cost of goods to your marketing budget to your team has to fit inside that number.

That's the waterfall. That's gross-to-net.

## The Lies by Channel

Different channels have very different gross-to-net profiles. This is where a lot of founders make a critical mistake: they think more doors is always better.

"Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains" is only half the problem. The more dangerous confusion is treating all distribution as equally valuable to your P&L.

**Natural and Specialty (Whole Foods, Sprouts, natural co-ops):** Distributor margins tend to run on the higher end (UNFI and KeHE typically take 25-30% on cost). But trade spend requirements are often lower, and quality-conscious consumers tend to have better repeat rates. Net revenue realization in natural channel often runs 30-40% of MSRP after the full waterfall.

**Conventional Grocery (Kroger, Albertsons, Publix):** Higher volume potential but more demanding promotional requirements. Expect trade spend of 20-30% of gross revenue if you want to stay competitive and keep your velocities respectable. The deduction environment is also more aggressive at scale. Net realization might be 25-35% of MSRP.

**Costco:** We did meaningful business with Costco at Suja, and they're actually among the most transparent buyers out there. You know exactly what margin they take (roughly 14-15%). There's no distributor layer, which helps your net considerably. But the volume minimums are substantial, and the rotational nature of Costco means you need to model sell-through carefully. If a rotation ends with inventory still on hand, you're absorbing that.

**DTC (your own website):** This looks the best on paper. No retailer margin, no distributor. But then come customer acquisition costs, shipping, fulfillment, and returns. CAC in consumer packaged goods can run anywhere from $18 to $65 per new customer depending on your category and competition. The channel with the best gross margin often has the worst payback period. And you cannot market your way into loyalty -- so the repeat rate question matters enormously in DTC.

**Amazon:** Referral fees typically run 15% of revenue plus FBA fulfillment costs. Add 8-15% in sponsored products just to show up in search. Net revenue realization on Amazon often runs 35-45% of what founders initially model. It's a real channel with real scale potential, but go in with eyes open.

The point isn't to avoid any of these channels. The point is to know your gross-to-net by channel before you scale. Because scaling a low-net-revenue channel feels like progress until you run the real numbers.

## Why Investors Care About This More Than You Think

When we were building toward the Coca-Cola investment in 2015, one of the first things their diligence team did was pull apart our gross-to-net by channel, by SKU, by period. They wanted to see our promotional cadence. The shape of our deduction history. Whether our reported revenue had integrity behind it.

Because what Coke was really asking was: "Do you understand your own business?"

A brand doing $10M in reported revenue with 22% gross margins after trade looks very different from a brand doing $8M in reported revenue with 38% gross margins after trade. The second company is worth considerably more. It has more resilience. More leverage. More options.

"Gross margin determines destiny" isn't just a line I repeat because it sounds good. It's the operating principle behind every valuation conversation I've ever been in. And gross-to-net is the mechanism by which gross margin is either protected or destroyed.

When investors or acquirers look at your business, they're going to normalize your revenue. They're going to adjust for what's real. The question is whether you understand it first, or whether they're going to discover a gap you didn't know was there.

Hope is not a strategy. Know your numbers.

## Getting Your Gross-to-Net Right

Here's where I'd start:

**Build the waterfall by channel.** Take one channel. Map every deduction from MSRP to your net realized revenue per unit. Retailer margin, distributor markup, trade spend, deductions, freight. Do this for every meaningful channel you're in. Compare them. You'll likely find that one or two channels are genuinely value-destructive at your current scale.

**Audit your deductions quarterly.** The legitimate ones you accept and budget for. The illegitimate ones you dispute with documentation. Most founders I talk to have never done a formal deduction audit. Most of them discover somewhere between 15-30% of their deductions can be recovered or disputed successfully. CPG is a "penny profit" business. The pennies matter.

**Understand your trade ROI, not just your trade rate.** Spending 20% of revenue on trade isn't inherently wrong. Spending 20% of revenue on promotions with a 0.4x velocity lift... that's a problem. Measure your velocity lift from every promotional event. Know what you're buying with your trade dollars before you commit to the next one.

**Model net revenue, not gross revenue.** Every financial model I've ever seen from a first-time founder starts with gross revenue projections. The model that actually matters starts with net revenue. What's actually funding your operations? What's the true denominator for your margin calculations? Build your P&L from the net number up, not from MSRP down.

**Set a net revenue per case floor.** This is the minimum your business can function at. If a deal, a channel, or a customer is pushing you below that floor, the answer is no. This is especially important in the early days when every buyer is asking for money off and every distributor is asking for more margin.

## The Number That Tells the Truth

We were deep in the growth years at Suja when I had a moment of clarity about this. We were reporting impressive topline numbers. Growing 20% year over year. Getting press. Getting doors. Feeling like a real company.

And then I sat down with our true gross-to-net picture. After trade, after distributor, after deductions. What I saw was a company running on a much thinner margin than the headline numbers suggested. Gross margins hovering around 28%. The gap between what we looked like from the outside and what we needed to look like to survive was real.

We fixed it. We worked the gross margins up to nearly 50% by the time I left Suja. But it required making some genuinely hard decisions. Taking on the $1M spoilage write-off and learning the lesson. Shifting to the 12-oz democratized product. Building the internal discipline to say no to distribution that looked like growth but was actually diluting our financial engine.

It also required owning the mistake. There was a period where we were celebrating topline growth while our gross-to-net was quietly deteriorating. We were excited about the wrong number.

Revenue without margin is ego. The gross-to-net is where that equation gets settled.

If you're not tracking it by channel, by SKU, by period... you're flying blind. And in this business, flying blind is a plan for failure.

Dream boldly. Plan soberly. And know your net.
