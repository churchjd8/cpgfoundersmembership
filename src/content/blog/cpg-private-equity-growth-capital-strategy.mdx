The year was 2014. We had a PE firm in the Suja capital structure, and Goldman Sachs had just put in $60 million. Coca-Cola was writing a $90 million check for a minority stake. And one of our existing investors, a PE firm we'd brought in less than twelve months earlier, had negotiated contractual rights that would have meant virtually all of that Coke money went straight into their pocket.

Not to the angels who'd been with us from the beginning. Not to the founders. To the PE firm that had been with us for less than a year.

Legally? They were entitled to it. Structurally? They had every right.

That's how private equity works.

I'm not telling that story to vilify anyone. I'm telling it because that moment taught me more about how PE investors actually think than anything I'd learned in business school. And if you're a CPG founder approaching the stage where PE becomes a conversation, you need to understand this before you're sitting across the table negotiating.

**Private equity is not a check. It's a transaction.** And the terms of that transaction will define your company and your personal outcome for the next five to seven years.

---

## PE vs. Everyone Else

Angels write checks because they believe in you. Family offices write checks because they think you're going to build something enduring and they want along for the ride. Strategics write checks because they want to understand your category, leverage your relationships, or eventually acquire you.

Private equity firms write checks because they're running a fund, and they need that fund to generate returns for their LPs. They need your company to be worth significantly more in five to seven years than what they paid for it.

That's not cynical. That's just the structure of the business. Understanding it protects you.

The PE return model is straightforward: they buy in at a certain multiple of EBITDA, they help you grow EBITDA, and they sell at the same or a higher multiple. If they paid 8x EBITDA on $5M (a $40M check for a minority stake, say), they need you to exit at 10x EBITDA on $12M two years later to generate a decent return. The math drives every conversation.

When PE tells you they want to "partner" with you to grow the business, they're telling the truth. They do want to grow the business. They just want to grow it in ways that maximize the multiple at exit, which may or may not be aligned with your vision of the brand.

That gap in objectives is where most of the friction happens.

## The Three Ways PE Money Changes Your Company

**First: the reporting burden becomes real.**

Angels are patient. Family offices check in quarterly. PE firms want weekly dashboards, monthly board packages, and deep-dive operating reviews every quarter. If your financial infrastructure isn't built before they invest, it'll be built on their timeline, under their pressure. I've seen PE investors spend the first eighteen months of an investment basically retrofitting a company's finance function to meet their needs.

This isn't entirely bad. The discipline forced by institutional investors builds better companies. But if you don't have a real CFO and real systems before the check clears, expect to spend a large portion of your management bandwidth on reporting rather than growth.

**Second: the talent dynamics shift.**

PE investors have portfolio companies and operating partners. They will introduce you to executives, advisors, and consultants who they believe can accelerate the business. Some of these introductions will be genuinely useful. Others will be placeholders designed to give them more visibility into operations.

More importantly: when PE comes in, your existing team will feel it. Suddenly there's more scrutiny, more process, more "governance." The entrepreneurial culture you built can get squeezed out if you're not intentional about protecting it. I've watched that happen to brands I genuinely believed in. The PE money arrives, the culture leaves, and two years later the brand has all the right systems and none of the soul.

Your job as founder is to hold the line on culture while simultaneously meeting the operational expectations of a disciplined financial sponsor. That tension is real, and nobody tells you about it in the pitch meeting.

**Third: your options narrow at exit.**

This is the one that surprises founders most. When PE buys a minority stake, they typically negotiate rights that give them significant influence over your eventual sale process. Preferred return structures, information rights, board seats, co-sale rights, drag-along provisions... by the time you read all the documents (and you need to read all the documents), you'll realize that your exit options have been meaningfully shaped by the terms you agreed to on the way in.

The story I opened with, ACG and the Goldman Sachs round, that was a preferred return structure working exactly as designed. The fact that it felt unfair to the angels didn't make it illegal. It made it a lesson about negotiating properly when you first bring a PE firm into the structure.

## What PE Firms Actually Look For

If you're going to take PE money, understand what they're evaluating.

**Gross margins.** PE investors look for businesses that can sustain and expand EBITDA over time. Thin margins make that nearly impossible. If your gross margins are below 40 percent when you approach institutional capital, you're going to have a hard conversation. They may still invest, but they'll underwrite it differently and your valuation will reflect the risk.

Gross margin determines destiny. It really does. And PE investors know this better than almost anyone.

**Velocity and repeat rate.** Not just distribution breadth... velocity per point of distribution and repeat purchase. A brand in 10,000 stores with weak velocity is a liability. A brand in 1,000 stores with strong velocity is an asset. PE firms have seen enough blown-up CPG businesses to know the difference.

**Clean cap table and legal structure.** You'd be amazed how many companies get to the institutional capital stage with messy cap tables, unresolved equity promises, missing IP assignments, or employment agreements that weren't properly structured. PE firms do serious diligence. If your house isn't clean, they'll either pass or reprice the deal to reflect the cleanup cost.

**A management team that scales.** PE investors are not investing in a person. They're investing in a team. If the entire business depends on the founder being present for every decision, that's a risk factor they'll flag. Building a management layer underneath you, before the institutional conversation, is not just operational maturity. It's a valuation driver.

**A credible path to exit.** The PE firm is thinking about their exit on day one of their investment. They want to understand who the eventual strategic buyers are, what category dynamics look like in five years, and whether your brand will be compelling to a large acquirer or a public market at the end of their hold period. Have that answer ready before they ask. If you can't articulate it, they'll underwrite it themselves, usually more conservatively than you'd like.

## What to Negotiate Before You Sign

**Understand the waterfall before you agree to any terms.** How does money flow in an exit? In a recap? In a down round? PE investors have seen a thousand ways that founders end up with less than they expected because they didn't fully model the distribution scenarios when the deal was inked.

Don't just review the headline valuation. Model the scenario where the company exits at 1x, 1.5x, and 3x the investment. What do the founders actually receive? What do the angels receive? How does the preferred return structure interact with a suboptimal exit?

**Negotiate board composition carefully.** PE investors will typically want board representation. This is fair. What you want to avoid is a situation where you're outvoted on decisions about your own company's direction. The composition of the board, and the specific decisions that require board approval, matter enormously.

**Understand the preferred return structure.** Not all preferred is the same. Participating preferred is very different from non-participating preferred. The difference can be worth millions at exit. Understand exactly what you're agreeing to.

**Get clarity on operational governance.** Before you sign, ask what decisions require board approval. What spending thresholds trigger a vote? Can you make key hires without PE sign-off? Can you open new channels, sign major contracts, pivot the brand? The answers to these questions will tell you more about how the relationship will actually work than anything in the pitch meeting.

## When PE Money Makes Sense (and When It Doesn't)

PE makes sense when you've proven the business model and need capital to accelerate distribution, build out infrastructure, or make strategic acquisitions. When the business is working and the question is speed, PE can be a powerful accelerant.

PE doesn't make sense when the business is still searching for product-market fit. When gross margins are still below 40 percent. When the management team isn't built. When the founder isn't ready to operate under institutional governance. When you don't have a clear exit thesis that aligns with what the PE firm needs.

There's no shame in not being ready for PE. I've known plenty of founders who would have been much better served taking another angel round or a strategic minority investment rather than a PE firm, simply because the stage wasn't right.

The worst outcomes I've seen in CPG involve PE firms investing in businesses that looked attractive on the surface but weren't operationally ready for the governance expectations that came with the check. The PE firm ends up frustrated. The founder ends up feeling micromanaged. The business underperforms. Everyone loses.

Do the honest work of assessing your readiness before you start the conversations.

## What Happened to Suja

You know the rest of the story at Suja, at least broadly. Coca-Cola passed. We restructured. We stabilized. I stepped back. In 2020, Suja sold to Paine Schwartz for approximately $300 million.

Paine Schwartz is a PE firm. And under their ownership, the company grew to more than $350 million in revenue with meaningful EBITDA margins. They did exactly what PE firms are supposed to do: they took a turnaround and turned it into an exit.

That story ended well.

The story with ACG in the middle of the Coca-Cola transaction was harder. We ended up doing the right thing by fighting to get our angels their fair share. We brought in Goldman Sachs at $60 million to give the angels an earlier liquidity option, and they eventually returned roughly 10x. The PE investor got their return. The angels got theirs.

It required fighting. It required creativity. And it required understanding, clearly, that the PE firm was operating within their contractual rights... and that our job was to build a structure where everyone who'd helped build the company could share in the outcome.

**Deals are not just about valuations. They are about fairness and morale.**

---

## The One Question to Ask Yourself

Before you take any institutional capital, ask yourself one question: *Do I fully understand, in every scenario, what this money will cost me?*

Not just in dilution. In governance. In operational autonomy. In exit options. In the culture of my company.

If you can answer yes, clearly and confidently, the check might be the right next step.

If you're not sure... that uncertainty is expensive. Do the work first.
