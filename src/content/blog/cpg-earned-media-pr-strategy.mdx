I was standing in a Whole Foods in San Diego sometime in late 2013 when a woman grabbed a Suja bottle off the shelf, looked at it, and said to her friend: "Oh, I read about this in Shape magazine."

She hadn't seen an ad. Hadn't clicked a Facebook post. Hadn't watched a demo. She'd read a story somebody else wrote about us... and that was enough.

That moment stuck with me. Because we had spent exactly zero dollars to make it happen.

---

The CPG world loves to debate marketing channels. Meta versus TikTok. Trade spend versus digital. Demos versus influencers. Everyone's got an opinion on where to put the dollar.

But there's one category that gets almost no systematic attention from early-stage founders: earned media. Press coverage. Editorial features. Journalists writing about your product because they found it interesting, not because you paid them to.

In my experience building Suja to over $100 million and scaling eight companies across three decades... earned media is consistently the highest-ROI awareness channel available to an emerging brand. It's also the one most founders approach completely backwards.

Hope is not a strategy. And most founders are hoping for press coverage rather than building a system to earn it.

---

## The Suja Story That Shaped How I Think About This

When Suja launched at Whole Foods in Southern California in early 2013, we weren't the first cold-pressed juice brand. Evolution Fresh had just been acquired by Starbucks. Blueprint Cleanse had just been scooped up by Hain Celestial. The category was happening and journalists were looking for the next chapter.

We weren't the story because we paid for it. We were the story because we were the independent guys still standing. Three San Diego founders, an HPP cold-press technology most consumers had never heard of, and a genuinely differentiated product in a juice aisle that hadn't changed in decades.

Within 18 months, Suja had been featured in The New York Times, Shape, Women's Health, People, and Forbes. Inc. Magazine named us the fastest-growing consumer business in America. We landed on Forbes' list of most promising brands.

Not one of those features was a paid placement.

And here's the thing... those stories did more for our velocity than almost anything we ran in paid media. A consumer reading a Shape magazine article doesn't skim past it the way they skip an ad. They read it. They remember it. They tell their friends. Then their friend tells the Whole Foods stranger standing in the juice aisle.

That's compounding. Paid media doesn't compound. Earned media does.

---

## The Principle

Earned media comes to brands with a real point of view. Not brands with a polished press kit. Not brands with the most expensive agency. Brands that stand for something specific, that have a story worth telling, that give a journalist something genuinely interesting to write about.

You can market your way into trial. But you cannot market your way into loyalty. And you absolutely cannot write a check for real media credibility.

The brands that consistently earn great coverage aren't the best at public relations. They're the best at being interesting.

---

## What I've Learned About Making This Work

**1. Define your editorial angle before you need it.**

What's the story a journalist would want to write about your brand? Not the story you want told. The story their readers would actually find interesting.

At Suja, the story wasn't "new juice company launches at Whole Foods." Nobody cares. The story was "HPP cold-press technology is about to disrupt a category that's been stagnant for twenty years, and three guys from San Diego figured it out." That's a story.

When you hand a journalist a specific, human, interesting angle... they don't have to manufacture the narrative. Most of them are working five stories simultaneously and grateful when a brand does some of the work for them. Make it easy to say yes.

**2. Think in tiers, and work them in order.**

Not all press is created equal, and not all press serves the same audience.

A feature in Food Business News moves buyers. A feature in Shape or Women's Health moves consumers. A feature in Forbes moves investors. A local news segment moves velocity in a specific market.

Founders make the mistake of swinging for the biggest outlets first and then wondering why nobody picks up the phone. Build your credibility in the trade press first. NOSH, BevNET, Food Dive. These journalists cover your category full-time. They know the buyers who read their columns. When you have a legitimate story worth telling, call them before you call anyone else.

The consumer press will come when the trade press has already established that you're real.

**3. Your story has to be true and verifiable.**

Journalists are skeptical. They're supposed to be. When you make a claim about your product... about efficacy, origin, uniqueness, or scale... it needs to hold up to scrutiny.

I've watched brands lose their best press opportunities because they got caught in a puffed-up claim. One bad interaction with a fact-checking journalist and you're not just losing that story. You're losing that journalist's trust forever. And journalists talk to each other.

The Suja story held up because it was true. The HPP technology was real. The ingredient sourcing was real. The founders were real. When reporters dug in, they found exactly what we told them they'd find.

Dream boldly. Plan soberly. Same thing applies to how you present your story to press.

**4. PR has to be a system, not a one-time push.**

This is where most founders fall down. They hire an agency for three months around a product launch, get a couple of placements, and then let the relationship go quiet.

Earned media compounds when you give journalists a reason to cover you at every stage of the journey. Pre-launch (category trend story). At launch (new product feature). When you raise money (founder story). When you hit a distribution milestone (the market is responding piece). When you win an award. When you have data that tells an interesting category story.

Build a rhythm. Send quarterly updates to the five or ten journalists covering your space. Not pitches. Updates. "Here's what we're seeing in the category. Here's what we're doing. Here's a number that surprised us." Relationships built between stories survive the noise.

## The 2019 Lesson That Changed Everything for Me

In 2019, after Coca-Cola walked away from completing their acquisition of Suja, we had to get brutal about costs. We cut marketing spend by more than $4 million. I was genuinely terrified. That's a significant reduction for a brand at our scale.

Revenue grew about 10% that year.

What I realized: we had built a brand that people genuinely loved and talked about without us. The earned media from the prior three years... the editorial features, the word of mouth, the brand story consumers had internalized... that was doing work that paid media never fully could replicate.

CPG is a "Penny Profit" business. The pennies matter. When every marketing dollar has to justify its existence, earned media starts to look like the most capital-efficient channel you have. Because once you've earned credibility with a journalist, they remember you. That coverage lives on the internet indefinitely. Consumers find it. Investors find it. Buyers find it.

Gross margin determines destiny. So does the quality of attention you earn versus the attention you rent.

---

## The Practical Audit

A few things worth looking at right now:

Do you have a clear editorial angle for each stage of your brand? Pre-retail, post-retail, and post-fundraise are three different stories. Make sure you know which one you're telling.

Are you building relationships with trade press before you need them? NOSH, BevNET, Food Dive. These people remember who reached out early with a legitimate story versus who cold-pitched during a fundraise.

Is your product story airtight and genuinely differentiated? Not in a marketing-speak way. In a way that survives a skeptical journalist who spends three days checking your claims.

When you get coverage, are you amplifying it everywhere? On your website, in your pitch deck, in your sell sheets, in your email sequences? Earned media has a serious multiplier effect when you use it strategically. Don't let it sit on the journalist's website. Make it part of your brand narrative everywhere a buyer or investor encounters you.

Are you treating PR like a campaign or like a relationship? Campaigns end. Relationships compound.

---

## The Closer

The best CPG brands don't just make great products. They make themselves interesting.

A great product is table stakes. A great story is a competitive advantage. And when that story starts getting told by other people... by journalists, by bloggers, by consumers recommending you to strangers in a grocery aisle... that's when you know you've built something that lasts.

The woman in the Whole Foods who grabbed that Suja bottle because she'd read about it in Shape? She didn't know our marketing budget. She didn't know our trade spend rate. She just knew someone she trusted had found us worth writing about.

That's what you're really building toward. Not awareness you bought. Awareness you earned.
