At Rowdy Energy, we paid nearly a million dollars in one-time slotting fees to get into Albertsons.

The income statement looked great. We had distribution. We were celebrating. Eight months later, Albertsons discontinued us. Velocity was below their hurdle. The economics never worked.

We'd been measuring revenue. What we should have been measuring was contribution.

That experience cost us. Not just the slotting fees... the reputational cost, the shelf resets, the morale hit when the team finds out a major account just cut you. All of it traceable back to one failure: we didn't have a Customer P&L.

---

## What Is a Customer P&L?

Simple concept. Almost nobody builds one.

A Customer P&L is a retailer-by-retailer income statement that tells you what an account is actually contributing to your business... after every cost is stripped out. Not just your company P&L, not just your gross margin. A bottom-up view by account.

I was sitting with a founder recently. He'd crossed $5 million in annual sales. Three retailers. Two natural, one conventional. Big smile on his face.

I asked: "Which one is actually the most profitable?"

Pause.

"We're growing in all three..."

That's not an answer. And he knew it.

Here's the hard truth: you can have a retailer doing $800,000 in annual revenue and actually be losing money on that account. I've seen it. More than once. The revenue shows up on the income statement. The margin disappears into trade spend, deductions, demos, freight, broker fees, and slotting that nobody tracked to the account level.

Revenue without margin is ego. That's true at the company level. It's even more true at the customer level.

---

## The Anatomy of a Customer P&L

Let me walk you through what it actually looks like. This is not complicated. You can build this in a spreadsheet this week.

**Start with Gross Sales to that retailer.**

Then subtract:
- **Trade allowances and off-invoice deductions** (the deals you're funding: OI, scan-downs, ad fees, temporary price reductions)
- **Invoice deductions and chargebacks** (short pays, compliance violations, EDI errors, damaged goods claims — these pile up quietly)
- **Slotting and new item fees** (amortize these over the commitment period, typically 12 months)
- **Coop advertising and ad fees** (what you agreed to pay for their circular, their digital placements)
- **Demo and sampling costs** (in-store demos are expensive — $400 to $600 per event in many markets — and usually tied to specific accounts)
- **Broker commissions** (typically 4-6% on retail revenue, but make sure you're attributing them to the right accounts)
- **Direct freight and logistics costs** (if you're paying to get product to a DC or shipping direct to stores, track it by customer)

What's left is your **Customer Contribution Margin.** That's the real number.

Most founders would be shocked at what they find.

## The Math That Changes Everything

Let me show you a simplified example.

Say you're doing $1 million in annual net sales to a major conventional grocery chain. You feel good. It's a big account.

Strip it out:

- Trade spend (15% of net sales for promos, scans, ad fees): -$150,000
- Deductions and chargebacks (3-4% is common in conventional): -$35,000
- Slotting (amortized over the year): -$80,000
- Demo costs: -$25,000
- Broker fees (5%): -$50,000
- Incremental freight (maybe this account requires pallet delivery, extra trucking): -$20,000
- **Total cost below gross margin: -$360,000**

Now take your gross margin on that account. Let's say you're at 40%.

Gross profit on $1M net sales: $400,000.

Subtract that $360,000 in account-specific costs below the gross margin line.

Customer Contribution: $40,000.

On $1 million in revenue. That's a 4% return before you've paid a single dollar of overhead.

If your slotting was higher or your velocity was weak enough to require heavier promos to stay on shelf... you're negative. You're paying to be in that store.

CPG is a "Penny Profit" business. The pennies matter. And nowhere do the pennies hide better than inside a customer P&L you haven't built.

## What the Data Actually Tells You

When I finally got serious about building Customer P&Ls at Suja — we were north of $40 million in revenue by then — what we found was uncomfortable and clarifying at the same time.

Some of our fastest-growing accounts were the most margin-destructive. A couple of accounts we'd been "protecting" because of their prestige... were actually cash drains.

There's a line from the book I keep coming back to: "If the consumer is the holy grail, retailers hold the keys to the kingdom." True. But holding keys doesn't mean every door they open is profitable for you.

The Walmart Smoothie story is worth telling here. Walmart asked us to produce a plant-based smoothie exclusively for them. Our production team hated the idea. The economics were bad, the run was operationally inefficient. But we agreed to it. Not because that SKU was going to be a margin winner... but because trading that short-term pain opened the door to expand distribution of our higher-performing, higher-margin SKUs across Walmart's network.

That's a strategic trade. Knowingly entering a bad-margin play to unlock something bigger.

The difference between strategy and stupidity is knowing which one you're doing.

Without a Customer P&L, you can't tell the difference.

## When to "Fire" a Customer

This is the conversation nobody wants to have. But sometimes the right move is to walk away from an account.

Not common. But it happens.

Signs you might need to have the conversation:
- The customer contribution margin is negative for two consecutive years, with no credible path to improvement
- Deductions and chargebacks consistently exceed 8-10% of that account's net revenue
- You're on heavy perpetual promotion just to maintain velocity at that chain (meaning their consumers are your least loyal customers)
- The retailer's demands for exclusives, custom sizes, or packaging are fragmenting your supply chain to the point it's costing you on all other accounts

Sometimes a difficult conversation with a buyer ("we need better economics to stay in your stores") leads somewhere good. Buyers who want you want to find a path. But you have to bring the data. That's the Customer P&L doing its job.

The accounts worth fighting for are the ones where you can see a clear line to a positive contribution over the next 12 months. Everything else is a conversation, not a given.

## Build It Now. Not When You Hit $10M.

Most founders think this is a problem for later. When they have a finance team. When they have better systems. When they hit some threshold.

Wrong.

The time to build a Customer P&L is before you sign your next slotting agreement. Before you accept those ad fee requirements from a new retailer. Before you agree to a demo program that your category manager says you "have to do."

You don't have to be a finance person to build this. A spreadsheet. A column per major account. Walk through the math once. It'll change how you negotiate, how you prioritize, and how you think about expansion.

Here's where to start:

1. Pull the last 12 months of invoices to your top 5 accounts
2. Pull all trade spend, deductions, and chargebacks by account from your deductions management system (or your broker reports)
3. Estimate broker fees and demo costs by account
4. Build one row per account, bottom-line to contribution margin
5. Rank them

The ranking will surprise you.

---

## The Principle

"Gross margin determines destiny." That's true. But inside gross margin, the real story is in the customer-level contribution.

You can have a company generating 42% gross margin and still have two accounts that are actively destroying value. The company average masks the truth. The Customer P&L reveals it.

Don't confuse revenue milestones with business health. The $5 million founder I mentioned... once we built his Customer P&L, we discovered his most profitable account was his smallest one. His largest account was his worst-performing. We restructured his trade spend on the big account, tightened the deductions management, and pulled back on demos that weren't driving velocity.

Twelve months later his gross margin improved four points without adding a single new door.

The opportunity was in the data he already had. He just hadn't organized it right.
