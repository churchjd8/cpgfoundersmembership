It was 5:00 p.m. on July 3, 2018.

I was standing in my kitchen. The house was full of family, getting ready for the Fourth of July weekend. Kids running around. Linda cooking. The phone rang.

It was Scott Uzzell from Coca-Cola's Venturing and Emerging Brands group.

He told me Coke wasn't going to acquire the rest of Suja.

I hung up. Walked downstairs. And wept in front of my sons.

What most people didn't know — what I'd barely admitted to myself — was that behind all the press coverage and the "fastest-growing CPG brand in America" headlines, we had weeks with less than $100,000 in the bank. We had $40 million in secured debt due in October. Our gross margins were sitting below 32%.

We had built something real. And we had nearly run ourselves into the ground financially.

That day taught me something I now share with every founder I work with: the brands that fail in CPG rarely fail because the product wasn't good enough. They fail because the financial foundation underneath the product quietly cracks, flaw by flaw, until one bad quarter or one lost deal brings the whole thing down.

I've been involved in forty-four fundraising rounds across thirty-plus years and eight companies. I've seen the same financial mistakes show up again and again. Not the complicated stuff. The avoidable stuff.

The other fatal flaw posts in this series covered people, legal, operations, and sales and marketing. But the financial flaws are the ones I see kill companies that had every other thing going for them.

Here are the five I watch for first.

---

## Flaw 1: Gross Margin Below 40% When You Hit Scale

I'll say this plainly: "Gross margin determines destiny."

If your gross margin is below 40% at scale, you're building a treadmill, not a business. Every dollar of growth requires another dollar of outside capital. Every setback threatens your survival. And when things get hard... you have no financial cushion to absorb the blow.

At Suja, we had a defining moment around this. We were running kombucha as a core SKU. The gross margin on that product? About 12%. We were outsourcing production, the category was getting crowded, and no matter how hard we pushed, the math never worked. Meanwhile, Bryan Riblett had been experimenting with two-ounce wellness shots — concentrated, produced internally, gross margins north of 60%.

We made the call. We walked away from kombucha. We leaned into shots.

That single product mix decision helped move Suja's overall gross margin from roughly 32% toward 50% by the time we sold the company. The company went from -$10 million EBITDA in 2018 to +$3 million EBITDA the following year. Not because we got smarter. Because we got more disciplined about where our margin actually lived.

Revenue is exciting. Gross margin is transformational.

Here's the benchmark I give founders: aim for 40% gross margin by the end of year two. Close to 50% by years three or four. If you're not on that trajectory, it's not a revenue problem. It's a business model problem. And no amount of distribution wins will fix it.

## Flaw 2: Running Out of Runway Before You Start the Next Raise

Cash flow kills more CPG startups than competition.

Not slowly, dramatically. Quietly. Gradually. And then all at once.

The rule I live by: maintain 12 to 18 months of runway at all times. Begin your next fundraise with at least 4 months of cash still in the bank. Because by the time you draft the deck, run the process, receive a term sheet, negotiate terms, complete diligence, and get cash in the account... you've used all of it.

Founders who wait until they're desperate to start a raise put themselves in an impossible position. The investors you want to work with can feel desperation in the room. They'll use it. "Enthusiasm attracts. Desperation repels." That's not just a phrase. It's leverage mathematics.

What saved Suja after Coca-Cola walked away wasn't some heroic pivot. It was that we still had time... not much, but enough. We hosted management presentations with more than 15 potential buyers over the next eighteen months. We had something to offer. If we'd waited another quarter, we might not have had that option.

Monitor your burn weekly. Not quarterly. Weekly. And begin the next raise when you feel comfortable, not when you feel scared. Comfortable is the only moment when you can walk away from a bad deal. Scared is the moment when you can't.

## Flaw 3: Inventory Management That Treats Cash Like It's Free

This one hurts founders who are growing fast, because growth feels like permission to buy more.

It's not.

Every pallet of product sitting in a warehouse is cash that isn't paying your team, isn't funding your next marketing push, and isn't available for the emergency you haven't predicted yet. CPG is a "Penny Profit" business. The pennies matter. So does the working capital you've got locked up in a SKU nobody ordered this week.

One of the most expensive lessons in Suja's early days: we agreed to take on spoilage risk with a distributor. We trusted them. We believed in the velocity. Ninety days later, we had over $1 million in spoilage deductions and had to do an unplanned emergency raise.

Trust must be documented. Assumptions must be stress-tested. Inventory decisions must be driven by data, not optimism.

Build your demand plan from the bottom up — by SKU, by customer, by week. Know your lead times. Calculate how much inventory you actually need before you order what you hope you can sell. Overstocking is a silent tax. Most founders don't notice it until they're wondering why they're out of cash despite strong top-line revenue.

## Flaw 4: Casual Equity Distribution in the Early Days

Equity feels cheap when you have nothing.

That's exactly the trap.

A well-meaning advisor gets 2%. A friend who made one introduction gets 1%. An early hire accepts below-market salary in exchange for 3%. A co-founder who isn't pulling their weight still owns 20%. Months pass and suddenly the cap table has fifteen owners and a founder who's sitting at 35% of their own company... before they've raised a single institutional dollar.

Here's the math founders don't run early enough: if you exit at $200 million and you own 4%, you take home $8 million before taxes. After ten years of work. That's not a catastrophic outcome by some standards. But if you gave away equity too casually before you had leverage, you paid dearly for every piece you let go.

Raise at increasing valuations. Target no more than 10 to 15% dilution per round. Try to maintain meaningful founder ownership through the Series A. And never hand out equity without vesting — equity without vesting is a time bomb that detonates during acquisitions, restructurings, and moments when you can least afford it.

The cap table is a financial document. Treat it like one from the first conversation.

---

## Flaw 5: Building Revenue Projections From the Top Down

This one shows up in almost every first-time founder's pitch deck.

The slide says something like: "The functional beverage market is $20 billion. If we capture just 1% of that, we're a $200 million company."

That's not a projection. That's a hope.

Top-down revenue modeling is seductive because it makes big numbers easy to justify. But investors who've seen hundreds of decks know the difference immediately. And more importantly, your business will not care about your top-down model when it's time to make payroll.

Build your projections from the bottom up. Start with the accounts you have or can realistically close in the next 90 days. Model your velocity per store based on demo data, early scan results, comparable brands. Add distribution conservatively, one retailer at a time, one region at a time. Build the number honestly, then be ready to defend every line of it.

We ran billboards in Manhattan during Suja's early days when we had four Whole Foods stores open in the entire city. We created awareness with nowhere to convert it. That's what top-down thinking produces at scale — spend that runs ahead of the foundation that makes it pay off.

The founders who close fundraises and build financially durable businesses are the ones who can walk a room through the specific accounts, the specific velocities, and the specific cost structure behind every revenue dollar they're projecting. That's a bottom-up mindset.

"Hope is not a strategy." Never was. Never will be.

---

## The Pattern Underneath All Five

Look at those flaws again.

None of them are exotic. None require an MBA to understand. And every single one is avoidable — with awareness, discipline, and the willingness to look at your own numbers honestly.

What connects them is this: they all happen when founders prioritize the feeling of growth over the foundation of growth. When the press is good and the momentum is real, it's easy to tell yourself the finances will sort themselves out.

They won't.

The lesson from July 3rd wasn't that Coca-Cola was wrong. The lesson was that we had let the financial fundamentals drift while the top-line headlines kept us feeling safe. When the safety net disappeared, the flaws were all still there.

We fixed them. The company survived. Eventually thrived. But it didn't have to be that close.

Build the foundation first. The velocity will follow.
