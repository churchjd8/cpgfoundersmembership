We made a technically excellent kombucha.

I mean that. Our team at Suja cracked one of the hardest challenges in the category: keeping alcohol levels consistent throughout shelf life. That's a real problem most brands struggle with. We solved it.

The product still failed.

Not because of quality. Not because of taste. Because the consumer didn't believe Suja had any business making kombucha.

That's the lesson nobody warns you about when you start extending your brand. The failure isn't usually in the product. It's in the permission.

**The permission problem**

Suja was a cold-pressed juice brand. That was our identity from day one. "Health without the punishment" — a concentrated shot of whole fruits and vegetables in a bottle, HPP-processed to preserve every nutrient. When we showed up in the kombucha section with a Suja logo on it, people didn't say "great, Suja made a kombucha." They said... nothing. They kept walking.

Meanwhile, that kombucha was outsourced at roughly 12% gross margin. We were pulling resources from our core business, confusing our category story, and generating margins that made no sense for the risk we were taking. We eventually discontinued it and didn't look back.

Around the same time, we launched wellness shots. Two-ounce bottles packed with ginger, turmeric, apple cider vinegar. Cold-pressed, organic, functional. The consumer picked up a Suja shot and immediately understood it. "Of course Suja makes this." Produced internally. Gross margin near 60%.

The difference? One extension built on what consumers already believed about us. The other asked them to believe something completely new. In CPG, you almost never have enough runway to change perception AND sell product at the same time. "Gross margin determines destiny" — and the destination of our kombucha was written in that 12% from the start.

**The math will lie to you**

Here's where a lot of founders get into trouble. The P&L says the new product is profitable. Distribution is lined up. Retailers are interested. All the green lights...

But the math doesn't capture consumer permission. It doesn't tell you whether the person who trusts your brand will follow you somewhere new.

At Suja, we launched 275 SKUs in our first seven years. One new product roughly every nine days. We were aggressive about innovation... maybe too aggressive. We eventually discontinued all but 55. Only one of our original top-ten SKUs at launch was still in the top ten a decade later.

I'm not saying don't innovate. We had to move fast — the cold-pressed juice category was exploding and standing still wasn't an option. But if I could go back, I'd filter every launch decision through one question before anything else: *Does this extend what the consumer already believes about us, or does it ask them to believe something different?*

If it's the latter, you need at least twice the time and twice the money to make it work. That's the Rule of Twos applied to brand extension. Plan for it from the start, or you'll discover it the hard way.

**A three-part extension test**

Before you launch anything new, run it through this:

**The Core Belief Test.** Does this new product share the same fundamental belief system as your flagship? Not just the same ingredient profile or demographic... the same belief. Suja's was: "Your body deserves clean, whole-food nutrition without compromise." Wellness shots passed. Kombucha failed. The test isn't complicated — but you have to be honest when you run it.

**The Shelf Recognition Test.** If a shopper who loves your core product sees this new item on shelf with no context, will they immediately understand why you made it? You want their reaction to be "of course" — not "interesting..." The pause is dangerous. That pause means you've got an education job ahead of you, and education is expensive.

**The Margin Gate.** Extensions should improve or hold your company-wide gross margin, not just be profitable on their own. Kombucha was technically profitable compared to zero. But it pulled our resources, capped our margin, and created narrative confusion. The wellness shots pushed our company-wide gross margin from roughly 32% to 40%. That's what a successful extension looks like. Revenue without margin is ego — and extending your line just to show more products is a form of ego too.

**Speed is a weapon... when you're pointing the right direction**

One thing we learned from the Trader Joe's relationship: when a buyer spots a trend and comes to you, you have a real window. In 2019, Trader Joe's saw the celery juice movement taking off — Anthony William, the Medical Medium, had basically built a health category from scratch. They called us.

Trader Joe's typically preferred private label. But their private label process would have taken too long to capture the trend. From our first call to product on Trader Joe's shelves nationwide: less than six weeks.

That speed was possible because celery juice was a completely natural extension of cold-pressed. It wasn't a stretch. We had the manufacturing capability, the cold-pressed credibility, the organic certifications. The consumer already believed we could make it. Execution was the only question.

Speed of execution is a genuine competitive weapon in CPG. But speed in the wrong direction just gets you lost faster. Don't confuse innovation speed with innovation wisdom. And whatever you do, don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains — same principle applies here. Putting a misaligned extension in more doors doesn't fix the underlying consumer permission problem. It just scales it.

**Always develop Plan B**

Here's one discipline I'd build into every product development process: develop an alternative in parallel.

Not because you lack confidence in Plan A. Because in CPG, at least half the time Plan A doesn't materialize — a retailer passes, a co-manufacturer can't hit the cost target, a formula doesn't survive shelf life testing. If all your energy is in one direction and it falls apart, you lose months.

At Suja, when we were developing a new innovation offer for a major retailer, we'd routinely have a backup running simultaneously. This kept the conversation alive when something went sideways. It also made us look prepared to buyers — showing up with two thoughtful options instead of one is a different posture than scrambling to fill a gap.

The retailers who trusted us most were the ones who saw us bring honest assessments of underperforming SKUs... and arrive with solutions already prepared. That's what separates brands buyers love from brands buyers tolerate.

**Build your hero first**

One more thing before you extend anything. Go back to your core SKU.

If you don't have at least one product outperforming the rest of your line by 30% or more, you don't have a hero SKU yet. And without a hero SKU, you don't have a clear enough signal of what your brand actually stands for to extend it safely. You're extending a hypothesis. That's a very different risk profile than extending from a position of proven consumer trust.

Build the hero first. Get velocity. Learn exactly who is buying it, why they're buying it, and what they tell their friends about it. That feedback is the map for where you can go next.

At Suja, the Green Supreme — apple, kale, lemon — was the original heartbeat. Three ingredients, and I'm telling you, the first time I tasted it, I told my wife Linda: "If I, with my unrefined Midwestern palate, can love a product with kale in it, then anyone on the planet can." Everything we built after that was either tethered to what Green Supreme stood for or it eventually struggled.

The best brand extensions don't feel like extensions at all. They feel like the brand finally made the thing you always wanted from them.

The worst ones feel like a company trying to grow. That's the difference.
