July 3, 2018. My phone rings. The house is full of family getting ready for the Fourth of July.

Scott Uzell from Coca-Cola. He tells me they're not moving forward with the full acquisition of Suja.

Six weeks earlier, their management team had flown in to start integration planning. We thought we were done. We thought we'd crossed the finish line.

I put the phone down, walked downstairs, and wept in front of my boys.

The next morning -- Fourth of July in Mayville, New York. Blue skies, 82 degrees, fireworks. I woke up feeling sick to my stomach. We had $40 million in secured debt coming due in October. We were burning more than $10 million a year. And at certain points over the next several months, we were operating with less than $100,000 in the bank.

On a $100 million revenue company.

What kept us alive? Not my marketing instincts. Not our brand equity. Not our relationships with Whole Foods or Target or Costco.

Todd Fisher.

Our CFO, Todd, and I spent the next year living inside weekly cash projections. Not monthly. Not quarterly. Weekly. Every dollar in, every dollar out. That level of financial visibility is what separated us from becoming another cautionary tale. And there were moments where the margin between survival and failure was measured in days, not months.

Most CPG founders wait way too long to build their finance function. I see it constantly. And by the time they realize it, they're already in trouble they didn't see coming.

---

Here's what typically happens. In Stage 1, you've got QuickBooks and a part-time bookkeeper. Maybe a CPA you call once a quarter. You're watching your bank account like a hawk and that's essentially your finance function. It works because the business is simple enough that you can hold most of it in your head.

Then the business gets complicated. You're managing retail deductions from eight different accounts. Your gross margin by SKU looks completely different than your blended margin. You have trade spend committed six months in advance. You've got debt covenants with reporting requirements. Investors asking for a waterfall analysis. Co-man contracts with minimum volume commitments that show up as liabilities if you miss them.

And you're still running finance on QuickBooks and gut instinct.

That's when you're in real danger. Not the visible kind of danger. The slow-motion kind. Where the plane is descending and nobody has checked the altimeter.

"Hope is not a strategy." That applies to finance just as much as it applies to your retail expansion plan or your next fundraise.

---

**The Finance Maturity Ladder**

Let me give you a simple framework. Four stages. Know which one you're on.

**Stage 1: Pre-Revenue to $3M**

Finance function: A part-time bookkeeper, accounting software, and the founder doing FP&A.

What matters at this stage: unit economics, COGS by SKU, gross margin at the product level, weekly bank balance, burn rate. You don't need a controller yet. You don't need a CFO. You need to understand the difference between gross margin and net margin, and you need to know those numbers cold.

Founders who can't answer "what's your gross margin by SKU?" without digging for 30 minutes... they're already behind.

**Stage 2: $3M to $10M**

This is the danger zone. You're growing. You have real retail accounts. Trade spend is real. Deductions are starting to pile up. Your working capital needs are getting complicated because growth in CPG consumes cash before it creates it.

Finance function at this stage: a part-time or fractional CFO ($5,000-$10,000 per month) and a controller or senior accountant.

What the fractional CFO does: closes your books faster, builds the rolling 13-week cash flow forecast, manages investor reporting, and starts thinking about your next capital raise before you need it desperately. Because "Enthusiasm attracts. Desperation repels." That applies just as much to fundraising as it does to anything else in your business.

This is also when you should start tracking velocity and gross margin together at the account level. The best finance people I've worked with in CPG don't think like traditional accountants. They think like operators.

**Stage 3: $10M to $30M**

Finance has to become a real function now. Not a part-time gig. Not the thing you think about when you have time.

Finance function: A full-time CFO, controller, and one FP&A analyst.

The CFO at this stage is a true strategic partner. They're managing your banking relationships, your covenant compliance, your cap table. They're modeling the impact of a price increase before you walk into the buyer negotiation. They're telling you which SKUs to discontinue before the buyer tells you.

Having Todd as our CFO at Suja wasn't a luxury. It was the reason we survived. He understood CPG specifically. He understood trade spend and deductions and co-man agreements and all the operational complexity that makes the CPG P&L look nothing like what someone from a traditional finance background expects.

That context matters enormously. A CFO who's never reconciled a deduction report in their life is a liability, not an asset.

**Stage 4: $30M+**

Finance function: Full team. CFO, VP Finance or Controller, FP&A, dedicated treasury function, and eventually a data analyst embedded with the finance team.

At this level, your finance team is a command center. They're pulling in syndicated data, tracking velocity by account, modeling gross margin improvement scenarios quarter by quarter, and giving you the numbers you need to walk into a board meeting and tell a clear, defensible story.

"Gross margin determines destiny." I mean that literally. But the finance team is the infrastructure that actually tracks it, protects it, and helps you improve it.

**Five Signs You're Behind**

Here are the signals that you need to move to the next rung on the ladder.

One: You can't answer basic cash questions without digging. How much cash do you have right now? What's your monthly burn rate? How many weeks of runway? If these aren't live numbers you know off the top of your head, you're running blind.

Two: Your gross margin by SKU is a mystery. Blended gross margin tells you almost nothing. You need to know which products are making money and which are burning it. SKU-level economics is where the real decisions live.

Three: You don't have a rolling 13-week cash flow forecast. This is non-negotiable once you cross $5M in revenue. Weekly cash visibility. Every CPG founder who's survived a real crisis will tell you the same thing. I promise you.

Four: Your deductions are a black hole. Retail deductions, slotting fees, chargebacks, damaged goods... they can quietly eat 5-10% of your revenue if nobody's managing them systematically. That's not a finance problem. That's a business problem that your finance function has to own.

Five: You're raising money while running on fumes. If investors sense desperation in your financial data -- inconsistent reporting, no clear burn rate, reactive projections -- the raise gets 10x harder. Clean books and tight forecasting signal that you run a real company. "CPG is a 'Penny Profit' business, the pennies matter" -- and so does the discipline that shows investors you're tracking them.

---

**What a Great CPG CFO Actually Does**

People think a CFO does your taxes and closes your books. That's a CPA. A great CPG CFO is doing something completely different.

They're your co-pilot on the financial model. They challenge your top-down revenue projections with bottom-up math. When you say "I think we can hit $20M next year," they say "Show me by account. Show me by SKU. Show me the velocity data to support it." Then they either validate your number or show you the gap you need to close.

They manage your banking relationships. Revolving credit lines, term loans, SBA facilities -- those relationships need cultivation year-round. You want your banker calling you with opportunities, not you calling them in a panic.

They protect your equity. Every unnecessary bridge note you take at aggressive terms, every preference provision you accept without fully understanding its consequences... those decisions compound in ways that become painful at exit. A great CFO runs the cap table modeling before you sign anything and shows you exactly what that decision costs you in dilution or liquidation preference.

And maybe most importantly -- they help you tell a clear financial story. To investors, to your board, to strategic buyers. "Revenue without margin is ego." But if you can't present your margin trajectory compellingly and clearly, you leave value on the table in every single conversation.

---

**Common Mistakes**

Three mistakes I see over and over.

First: hiring a bookkeeper when you need a controller. A bookkeeper records what happened. A controller understands why it happened and can project forward. Different skill set. Different compensation ($60K-$90K vs. $90K-$130K fully loaded). Different impact.

Second: hiring a controller when you need a CFO. Controllers are excellent at historical accuracy. CFOs think strategically. If you're about to raise a Series A or navigate a covenant breach or explore a strategic partnership, you need someone who's been in those rooms before.

Third: waiting until there's a crisis. I've seen this movie dozens of times across 30+ years and eight companies. Founders keep playing CFO until something breaks. A covenant violation. A cash crunch they didn't model. A botched investor report that delays a raise. Then they're hiring in a panic, with reduced leverage and compressed time.

"Hire slow, fire fast" is the principle. But you can't hire slow if you wait until you're desperate.

---

I think about Todd Fisher often when I talk about what it actually takes to build a lasting CPG brand. We had great products. A great brand. Great retailer relationships. But without the financial infrastructure to see around corners, none of that would have been enough in 2018.

We survived because we had a real CFO who could tell us exactly how many weeks of cash we had, exactly what levers we could pull, and exactly which decisions would preserve our options versus which ones would close doors permanently.

Don't wait for your crisis to find yours.
