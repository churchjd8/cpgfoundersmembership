The call came from Walmart.

They wanted Suja to produce a custom, Walmart-exclusive plant-based smoothie. Specific formula. Their specs. Our production capacity and our team's time. My operations people hated the idea from the first sentence. The economics were unattractive. The production run was inefficient. It wasn't core to what we were building.

I said yes anyway.

And Walmart is still one of Suja's largest customers today... not because of the smoothie, but because the smoothie was the trade. The economics on that product were bad. The strategic value of what I got in exchange was not.

That distinction -- between saying yes to something and knowing *why* you're saying yes -- is everything in private label. Most founders get the call, feel the flattery of a major retailer wanting to work with them, and either panic-refuse or enthusiastically agree without doing the math on either side. Both mistakes are expensive.

Here's what eight companies and product in every major U.S. retailer have taught me: private label isn't automatically good or bad. It's a negotiation. And if you don't know what you're trading, you're just giving stuff away.

---

## What They're Actually Asking For

When a retailer calls about a private label arrangement or an exclusive product, they're asking for several things at once.

Your formula (or your capacity to create one). Your manufacturing infrastructure. Your team's time and attention. Your working capital for tooling, minimum runs, and inventory.

In exchange, they're offering... revenue. Usually at thinner margins than your branded business. Often with less predictable volume. And always with their name on it, not yours.

The unit economics sound fine on the surface. You have fixed costs. More volume means better cost absorption. Extra revenue is extra revenue, right?

"Revenue without margin is ego."

That applies to private label more than almost anything else in CPG. Because here's what the volume calculation always misses: opportunity cost. The invisible thing you're trading away when you say yes.

---

## The Math That Looks Good But Isn't

Let's run a scenario. Your branded product runs at 40% gross margin -- reasonable for a scaled beverage or food brand. A retailer comes to you with a private label offer at 25% gross margin. They'll take 50,000 cases. That sounds like a win.

But ask yourself: what does it actually take to produce those 50,000 cases?

Co-packer time. Production planning cycles. Ingredient procurement and lead times. Quality control and compliance. Customer service calls. Deduction management. Buyer meetings to troubleshoot issues that will absolutely come up.

All the same infrastructure. All the same team attention. Fifteen margin points lower.

And while your team is managing that private label program -- what aren't they doing? They're not optimizing your hero SKU at Whole Foods. They're not running down the Costco rotation you've been pitching. They're not chasing velocity on your branded product, which is the only product that builds actual equity.

The 50,000 cases are not really 25% gross margin. They're 25% gross margin minus the attention tax. And the attention tax, if you're at Stage 1 or Stage 2 of building a brand, is higher than most founders realize until it's too late.

---

## When to Walk Away

If you're still proving product-market fit -- still building velocity, still establishing your branded identity in a handful of key retailers -- private label is almost always a distraction. Full stop.

Here's why. You haven't built enough branded momentum to know what you're actually trading. You may think you have excess capacity, but excess capacity in CPG is usually just capacity that hasn't been deployed yet toward your best opportunities. And the operational drag of a private label program during a critical brand-building window is real.

Walk away if:

- The margin is within 10 points of your branded gross margin (you need a significant premium for the equity you're not building)
- It would divert more than 15-20% of your production capacity
- Your velocity on existing SKUs is still below hurdle levels (fix that first -- a distraction won't fix a velocity problem)
- You're considering it because it "feels like validation," not because you have a clear strategic logic for it

"Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains." The same principle applies here: don't confuse private label revenue with brand revenue. They're not the same thing, and one builds a business while the other often just generates cash you don't keep.

## When to Say Yes -- and What to Trade For

Here's the real playbook. Private label arrangements aren't business models. They're negotiation chips.

At Suja, I agreed to produce that Walmart smoothie at unattractive economics because I was trading one thing for another. Unattractive margin on a custom product, in exchange for expanded distribution of our highest-performing, best-margin branded SKUs. The production team saw a bad deal on the smoothie. I saw a door into Walmart's planogram for the products that actually mattered.

That trade worked. Walmart became one of our largest customers -- not because of the smoothie, but because the smoothie created the relationship that let our branded business scale.

Before you say yes to anything like this, run through five questions:

**1. What am I getting in exchange that I couldn't negotiate any other way?**
If the answer is "just the revenue," renegotiate or walk.

**2. Is the gross margin at least 10-15 points above my fully loaded costs?**
You're not building brand equity here. The margin has to compensate for that.

**3. Can I produce this without compromising velocity on my core SKUs?**
Your branded business is the thing that builds long-term value. Never sacrifice it.

**4. Is the minimum volume large enough to justify the disruption -- and is it guaranteed?**
"Forecast" is not a guarantee. Get volume minimums in writing or don't take on the operational complexity.

**5. Do I have an exit clause if the economics stop working?**
Multi-year private label agreements with no out are one of the quietest traps in CPG. Short initial terms. Easy exit language. Never lock yourself in.

If you can't answer all five clearly, go back to the table. Don't say yes until you can.

## The Negotiation Reality Nobody Tells You

Retailers asking for private label aren't doing you a favor. They're asking you to absorb operational complexity and capital commitment so they can expand their margin. They buy from you at cost-plus and sell at whatever price they want. The branded business you've spent years building gives them a reason to stock your product at eye level. The private label program... is for them.

That doesn't mean you should refuse. It means you should extract value.

Here's what to put on the table whenever a major retailer requests exclusive or private label work:

- **Expanded distribution of your branded line.** More doors. Better shelf placement. A commitment to new SKU ranging. The smoothie deal got us into categories and placements we wouldn't have earned otherwise.
- **Volume guarantees.** Minimums, not forecasts. If they want your capacity, they're committing to volume.
- **Quick payment terms.** Net 30 or better. Thin-margin programs need tight cash cycle discipline. "CPG is a penny profit business -- the pennies matter."
- **Short initial commitment.** Three to six months. Not multi-year. Prove the economics work before you're locked in.

One more thing on pricing: a senior Target buyer once gave me advice that stuck. Don't use identical pack sizes or barcodes across all your retail partners. If you do, Target will price-match Walmart's everyday low price and your premium retail positioning collapses. We launched a 10.5-ounce bottle at Walmart and a 12-ounce bottle at Target to preserve pricing integrity on our branded line.

Same logic applies to exclusive products. The exclusivity has value. Make sure you're pricing it accordingly -- either in dollars or in distribution terms.

---

## The Deeper Trap

Here's what trips up founders the most in this conversation.

They say yes to private label because they need the revenue. Cash is tight. Fundraising is slow. Branded sales are softer than projected. And a big retailer with a big PO looks like oxygen.

But running a private label program at 5-10 margin points below your branded business, at any meaningful scale, doesn't solve your cash problem. It compounds it. You're moving volume at economics that don't give you room to breathe, and you're doing it at the cost of the brand equity you've been building -- which is the thing that eventually gets you acquired or allows you to raise at a premium.

"Dream boldly. Plan soberly." The private label decision is one of the clearest applications of that I know. Don't let a big purchase order from a big retailer substitute for the strategic clarity you need before you say yes.

Ask yourself: what would have to be true for private label to make strategic sense for us right now?

If the answer requires a lot of "ifs," walk away. If the answer is clear -- here is the trade, here is the value, here is exactly how this unlocks our branded business -- then negotiate hard and take the deal.

Private label done wrong is a distraction. Private label done right is leverage.

Know the difference before you pick up the phone.
