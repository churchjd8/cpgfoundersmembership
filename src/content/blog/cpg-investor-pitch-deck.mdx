I was watching football with my oldest son Josh a few years back when he asked me an offhand question. How many fundraising rounds had I actually done over my career?

I thought it would take me five minutes to answer. It took 45.

When we were done, the number was 44. Forty-four fundraising rounds across 30-plus years, 8 companies, $200 million raised from friends and family, angels, PE firms, and VCs. At Suja alone, we ran 11 rounds in 7 years. Some years we were raising twice.

I tell you that not to brag. I tell you that because I have sat across the table from investors more times than almost anyone alive, watched what works and what doesn't, and learned the hard way that most founders waste 80% of their prep time on the wrong things.

They obsess over the deck. They perfect every slide. They agonize over fonts and colors and whether to use bullet points or icons.

And they walk in with no clarity on their economics, no traction data, and no real understanding of what the investor is actually trying to decide.

Here's the truth: **the deck doesn't get you funded. You do.** The deck is just how you organize the evidence.

So let me tell you what that evidence actually needs to look like.

## First, the structure

Keep it under 25 slides. Not 40. Not 52. Under 25.

I've reviewed thousands of pitch decks. The ones that are 50 slides don't get funded because the founder is more organized. They get skimmed, then shelved. Clarity is respect. If you can't make your case in 25 slides, you haven't made your case yet.

The deck should tell one story: here is a real market opportunity, here is how we're capturing it, here is proof that it's working, here is the team that can finish the job, and here is what we need from you.

That's it. Every slide should serve that story or get cut.

## The slides that matter most

**1. The cover.** Who you are in one sentence. Not a paragraph. Not a mission statement. One sentence that tells me your niche and why it's interesting. "The first organic wellness shot in the club channel, growing at 40% year-over-year." Done.

**2. The problem and the opportunity.** Don't tell me the global beverage industry is $161 billion. I know. Tell me about the specific gap your brand fills. Make me feel it. Who's not being served? Why now? What's changed in the market that makes this the right moment?

**3. Your product and your positioning.** Show the product. Show the package. Show it in context on a shelf or in someone's hands. Then give me the one sentence that defines your positioning: "We're the only [X] that [Y] for [Z]."

At Suja, that sentence was essentially: "A Whole Foods juice bar in a bottle." Simple. Sticky. Differentiated.

**4. The market.** Here's where most founders go too broad. Don't pitch me "the functional food market." Pitch me the specific niche you own and the adjacent segments you can expand into. Use nested layers: Industry, Category, Segment, Niche. Give me a five-year CAGR at the niche level. Anything growing over 20% annually is worth attention. Anything below 5% probably isn't your path to $100M.

**5. Traction.** This is the most important slide in the deck for any brand that's been in market. Show me velocity, repeat rate, distribution, and revenue trajectory. Don't cherry-pick your best stores. Show me the full picture and tell me what you're doing about the weak spots.

I'll be direct: most institutional investors today want to see $3-5M in trailing 12-month net revenue and gross margins above 40% before they'll write a meaningful check. If you're below that threshold, know it and frame your ask accordingly.

**6. Business model and unit economics.** This is where most founders lose me. Not because they don't have the data. Because they don't lead with gross margin.

"Gross margin determines destiny." I mean that literally.

Show me your gross margin today. Show me your path to 50%. Show me what's driving the improvement (co-man renegotiation, volume leverage, formulation refinement). Investors know this is a "Penny Profit" business. The pennies matter. A 35% gross margin brand and a 55% gross margin brand look completely different to a sophisticated investor, even at the same revenue.

**7. The financial model.** Five years, built from the bottom up. Not top-down aspirations.

Top-down says: "The market is $1.5 billion. We'll capture 3% in five years. That's $45 million."

Bottom-up says: "We're currently in 1,200 doors at $8 per store per week. With planned distribution expansion to 4,500 doors by year two and velocity improvements to $12 per store per week, that's..." and so on from there.

Bottom-up models force honesty. They also signal to investors that you actually understand your business. The Rule of Twos applies everywhere: everything takes twice as long and costs twice as much. A bottom-up model bakes in that humility. A top-down model usually doesn't.

**8. Distribution and channel strategy.** Where are you now and where are you going, and why in that order? The sequencing matters. A brand that tries to be everywhere immediately is almost always nowhere effectively. Show me the channel logic. Show me why Whole Foods before Target before Walmart, or whatever the right sequence is for your brand.

"Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains." Every investor I respect has seen brands bloat their door count and watch velocity crater. Show me you understand the difference.

**9. Your team.** One page. Relevant experience only. If you built something before, show me what you built. If you have advisors with strong CPG networks, name them. The hiring slide isn't about credentials. It's about conviction that this team can execute at the next stage of the business.

"Hire slow, fire fast" is my personal mantra, and investors know it. They want to see a team that's honest about gaps and has a plan to fill them, not a team that pretends it's already complete.

**10. The ask.** Be specific. How much are you raising? What structure (SAFE, convertible note, equity)? What valuation? What does the runway look like? What milestones does this round fund?

Target 18 months of runway minimum. Raise for that. Don't raise for 6 months hoping the next round closes faster. It won't. "Hope is not a strategy" applies to financial planning more than almost anywhere else.

## The mistakes that kill raises before they start

**Asking for too much too soon.** Pre-revenue? Your pre-money valuation should probably be $1-5M. Early retail brand with some proof? Maybe $4-10M. If you're doing $3-20M in revenue, investors are typically paying 3-4x trailing twelve-month net sales. Push that multiple too hard and you'll scare away good investors or set yourself up for a down round.

**Not soft-circling first.** Before you launch a formal raise, you should have verbal interest on at least 25% of the round. That lead investor creates momentum and credibility for everyone who comes after. Investors want to feel like they discovered something. They don't want to be the first sucker in.

**Leading with the dream, not the evidence.** "Enthusiasm attracts. Desperation repels." I've seen founders come in with incredible passion and zero data. Investors feel the desperation underneath. Lead with the traction. Let the enthusiasm come through naturally when you talk about where it's going.

**A messy cap table.** If you've made casual promises to friends, consultants, advisors, and early employees and none of it's been formalized, that's a problem. Clean it up before you raise. Investors do cap table math the second they get serious. Surprises there kill deals.

**Forgetting the audience.** Know who you're pitching. An angel investor writing their first CPG check has very different concerns than a strategic PE firm that owns four other portfolio brands. The core deck stays the same. The emphasis shifts. Understand what they're optimizing for.

## The thing no slide can do

Here's what I've learned from 44 rounds.

The deck opens the door. The deck earns the second meeting. But the check gets written because the investor believes in you. Not your projections. Not your gross margin (though that helps). You.

Can you execute? Can you handle the inevitable moment when it all goes sideways? Are you the kind of person who calls the investor before the bad news lands, or after? Do you know what you don't know?

"Dream boldly. Plan soberly." That combination, the founder who has both in the right proportions, is what investors are actually looking for. The deck is just how you demonstrate it.

At Suja, I've done presentations to maybe 15 different strategic acquirers and a few dozen institutional investors over the years. The ones that moved forward weren't always reacting to the best slides. They were reacting to clarity, honesty, and the conviction that the team in the room could execute the plan on the paper.

The deck is your story. Make sure it's a true one.
