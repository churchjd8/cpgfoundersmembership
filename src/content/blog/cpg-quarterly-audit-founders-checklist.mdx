We were in the middle of the Coca-Cola deal. Real money on the table -- $150 million, July 2015. Due diligence in full swing. Lawyers on both sides, accountants crawling through every corner of the business.

And then Coca-Cola's legal team asked about invention assignment agreements.

I didn't have them. Not from all employees.

Had to go back, one by one, and pay roughly $1,000 per employee to retroactively get those agreements signed. Not a disaster. Not a deal-killer. But an absolutely avoidable scramble that happened at the single worst possible moment -- when you're trying to close the biggest check of your career, you don't want to be chasing paperwork.

Here's what got me. That problem could have been caught in a 30-minute audit six months earlier. It's not complex. It's table stakes. But nobody was running a checklist.

Most founders don't. I didn't, for years. I learned it the expensive way. Multiple times.

---

The problems that kill CPG companies don't usually announce themselves.

They're not dramatic. They show up as a slow leak -- a deduction you didn't catch, a contract that auto-renewed because you forgot to set an alert, an evergreen clause with a co-packer that locked you into last year's pricing while your volume tripled. They compound quietly. And by the time they surface, they're expensive.

So a few years back, I built what I call the Quarterly Audit. It's not glamorous. It's not AI-powered. It's a structured 30-minute review of the things most likely to kill you -- organized into six buckets.

I run it myself. I have every brand I coach run it. And every single time we go through it, someone finds something. Every time.

Here's the framework.

## Bucket One: Legal and Governance

This is where most founders have the least visibility and the most exposure. Start here.

Are all your employees and contractors signed on Confidentiality and Invention Assignment Agreements? (This matters enormously if you ever plan to raise money or sell. I learned this with Coke. See above.) Has every equity grant gone through board approval with proper documentation? Is your cap table clean and current?

Check your 83B elections. Miss that 30-day filing window and you're potentially looking at a tax hit that simply didn't have to happen.

And then go through your contracts. Every contract. Look for anything longer than 12 months -- I prefer 3-month contracts for almost everything now. Look specifically for evergreen or auto-renewing clauses. Vendors bank on you forgetting. They often won't remind you. Set calendar alerts 90 days before every termination window without exception.

This stuff sounds boring. It is boring. But I've watched founders lose leverage in fundraising rounds because they were locked into a co-packer contract they couldn't exit. An investor's first pass through your data room will find these things. Better if you find them first.

## Bucket Two: Financial Hygiene

CPG is a "Penny Profit" business. The pennies matter.

Does your financial model reflect actual distributor deductions and promo accruals? Not aspirational ones -- actual ones? I've seen brands running 8% promo accruals in their model while paying 22% in reality. That's not a rounding error. That's a margin problem hiding in plain sight.

Are your top 3-5 profitability drivers clearly identified? Are you tracking gross margin monthly, by SKU, compared against your target? "Gross margin determines destiny." Not quarterly. Monthly.

And this one trips up a lot of founders: are you running a P&L by account before accepting new retail placements? Every new account has a cost of doing business. Some accounts don't make sense on the math. You need that analysis before you say yes, not after you've already shipped.

Cash runway. Know it cold. If you're under four months of runway and you haven't started your next raise, you're already late.

## Bucket Three: Sales and Channel Strategy

This is where the most optimistic thinking masquerades as strategy.

Before you expand distribution, ask: are your core SKUs demonstrating consistent velocity? Not "trending up." Consistent. The number one mistake in CPG is expanding doors before you've proven velocity in your current doors. "Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains." I've said it hundreds of times. Founders still do it.

Is your promotional calendar strategic, or reactive? Every promo that isn't driving trial or rewarding loyalty is just margin erosion. Chronic over-promoting trains consumers to wait for deals. Once that pattern is established, it's very hard to undo.

And after year two or three: are you auditing weak-margin products and cutting the ones that aren't earning their shelf space? The SKUs you launched with enthusiasm and kept out of loyalty... they're probably hurting you. Innovation is not churn if it improves turns. But keeping bad SKUs alive definitely is.

## Bucket Four: Operations and Supply Chain

Every quarter, I want founders answering these questions.

Have you visited your co-manufacturer in the past 12 months? You should. Things change. Ownership changes. Safety culture changes. A site visit isn't optional -- it's risk management. I've built co-man visits into the quarterly checklist specifically because founders tell themselves they'll go and then six months pass.

Are ingredient and packaging costs itemized in contracts, or are you operating on a handshake and the last invoice you can find? Benchmark your supplier pricing every 12 months. Costs that felt aggressive at launch often have room to come down as you scale.

Are you tracking retailer delivery performance? It needs to be above 95%. Below that, you're generating chargebacks, damaging buyer relationships, and in some cases creating grounds for delisting. Most founders don't track this at all until a buyer brings it up in a review. By then, you're already on defense.

And this one is critical: do you own your formulas? Not just your brand -- your actual formula. Some co-packers and flavor houses quietly retain ownership of recipes they helped develop. If a VC or strategic acquirer finds during diligence that you don't own your formula, that is a deal-stopper. Fix it now, while it's just an inconvenience, not a crisis.

## Bucket Five: Founder Discipline

This one's uncomfortable. Do it anyway.

Are you operating as a bottleneck? Are decisions sitting in your queue that should be delegated? At scale, the thing that made you successful early -- doing everything yourself -- becomes the thing holding you back. The "Popeye Syndrome" (doing it all yourself) is fatal at $5M in revenue. At $10M, it's terminal.

Are you making expansion decisions based on data, or based on enthusiasm? Enthusiasm is essential for starting a company. Data is essential for surviving past year three. The founders I watch get into trouble usually have more of one than the other.

Are you bringing in outside expertise where you have genuine gaps? Founders who think they can learn everything on the fly cost themselves months and millions. There are people who've done what you're trying to do. Use them.

## Bucket Six: Team

Quick, but non-negotiable.

Are there performance issues you've been tolerating that you haven't addressed? "Hire slow, fire fast." Most founders invert this completely -- they hire fast (because they're desperate), then fire slow (because they're conflict-averse). One weak performer in a small team has an outsized negative impact on everyone around them. You know who they are. You've known for a while. The quarterly audit is a forcing function to do something about it.

Are there cultural disconnects that haven't been addressed? They don't resolve on their own. They get louder.

---

At the end of every audit, I ask three questions. Always the same three.

Where are we leaking margin? Where are we overextended? What can we fix now before it compounds?

The answers aren't always comfortable. But they're always useful. And they're always more useful when you find them in a 30-minute review than when they find you at 11pm before a board meeting, or in the middle of due diligence with $150 million on the line.

The companies that survive aren't necessarily the ones with the best products. They're the ones with founders who catch problems early... before they become fatal, before they become expensive, before they show up in someone else's audit.

Dream boldly. Plan soberly.

Run the audit. Quarterly. Without exception.
