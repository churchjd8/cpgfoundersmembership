We had 50 Whole Foods stores approved before we had a name.

Fifty stores. Southern California. A launch date locked. A design agency waiting. And us sitting around a table arguing about what to call the company we'd been building for six months.

The front-runner was Hebe Juice. Named after the Greek goddess of youth. Clean. Aspirational. Made sense on paper. Our design agency was excited about it.

Except... "Hebe" is a derogatory term in some circles. I couldn't get comfortable with it. Kept imagining the first time a reporter stumbled onto it. Imagining the conversation with our Whole Foods buyer. Imagining trying to explain it to investors.

We were stuck.

Then Eric Ethans, one of our co-founders, went to Whole Foods for lunch. Sat next to a young woman. Started talking. Asked her name. She said Suja. He asked what it meant. She told him it meant "a long and beautiful life" in ancient Hindu.

He came straight from that lunch to my office.

We looked at each other. That was it.

Sometimes Stage 1 decisions are messy. Sometimes they're serendipitous. In our case, they were both.

But here's what most founders don't understand about brand naming... the serendipity is just the start. The real work begins the moment you fall in love with a name. Because falling in love with a name you can't actually use, or one that's going to create problems at scale, is one of the most expensive mistakes in CPG.

---

## Your Brand Name Is a Legal Asset

I've sat across the table from a lot of acquirers. And I've watched a lot of due diligence processes. Here's what I know: investors and acquirers care deeply about clean IP.

That word. "Clean." It comes up over and over. Do you have full ownership of the trademark? Is it registered federally? Are there any conflicting applications or pending disputes? Are you covered across all the classes you need?

If the answer to any of those questions is "I'm not sure" or "we haven't gotten to that yet"... you are sitting on a live wire. I've watched brands get a cease-and-desist letter after two years of building distribution and consumer awareness. New packaging. New marketing materials. New retailer presentations. New everything. Some never recover. The rebrand alone can run $500,000 and set you back 18 months.

And the thing is, none of it was inevitable. It was just work that got skipped at the beginning.

Hope is not a strategy. That applies to trademarks too.

The few thousand dollars a trademark attorney costs you at launch is the cheapest insurance policy you'll ever buy in this business. But trademark protection is just the floor. The real ceiling, the real question, is whether you've chosen a name that will *serve* you through every stage of the company's life.

## Five Things a Great CPG Brand Name Has to Do

Here's my working framework. Every name I've evaluated, for Suja or any of my other ventures, gets measured against these five things.

**1. It has to be protectable.**

Descriptive names, the ones that literally describe what you do, are nearly impossible to trademark. "Fresh Juice Co." doesn't pass. "Organic Greens" doesn't pass. You want a name that's distinctive enough to own legally. Made-up words (Suja, Zevia, Noosa) work well here. Evocative names (Honest Tea, Oatly, KIND) can work too. Just know that the more descriptive you are, the harder the legal fight becomes.

**2. It has to survive a global search.**

We almost named a product something that turned out to be a slur in another language. That happened to us twice across my career. Once with Hebe. Once with a product at a different company that I won't name here. Do a search in every major market you might ever enter. And search *adjacent* categories, not just food and beverage. Trademarks protect across classes, and a clothing brand or supplement company with a similar name can create real problems you didn't see coming.

**3. It has to fit the brand you're building to $100 million, not just the one you're starting with.**

This is the one founders underestimate most. You're building a 6-ounce green juice business today. But if you execute, you're going to be in 30,000 stores with fifteen product lines five years from now. Does the name travel? Does it give you room to expand beyond your launch SKU and launch channel?

"Suja" didn't lock us into juice. It was aspirational. It spoke to longevity and health. That gave us room. A name like "Green Cold Press Juice Co." would have been a prison. Dream boldly, plan soberly... and name accordingly.

**4. It has to work on a 2-inch label.**

Say it out loud. Write it small. Put it on a bottle. On a label, you have maybe two inches of real estate and a fraction of a second to register with a consumer moving through a refrigerated beverage aisle. Does it read clearly? Does it land fast? Is it phonetically clean?

I've watched competitors go to shelf with names that looked polished in a pitch deck and disappeared the second they were printed small. The label test is the one that actually matters.

**5. It has to be memorable for the right reasons.**

Great brand names carry a feeling. Not every name has to mean something literally. But it should *feel* like something. "Suja" felt warm and aspirational. "Rowdy Energy" was irreverent and bold. Whatever that feeling is, make sure it matches who your customer actually is and what you're genuinely selling.

You can market your way into trial. You cannot market your way into loyalty. But a great name makes trial easier. It gives consumers something to remember, something to say out loud to their friends when they recommend you.

## The Practical Steps Most Founders Skip

Before you file anything, run a preliminary search on the USPTO's TESS database (Trademark Electronic Search System). It's free. It won't tell you everything, but it tells you whether there's an obvious conflict.

Then hire an IP attorney to do a comprehensive clearance search. This runs roughly $1,500 to $3,000 and covers common law usage, state registrations, domain conflicts, social handles, and international databases. Worth every dollar.

File your trademark application across every class you need. For CPG, you're typically looking at International Class 32 (beverages, including juices, waters, and non-alcoholic drinks), potentially Class 30 (foods including snacks, coffee, tea), and Class 05 if you're making any functional or supplement claims. Filing fees run about $250 to $350 per class. Cheap compared to what a rebrand actually costs you.

One thing I've seen in 44 fundraising rounds... investors and strategic acquirers frequently discover trademark problems during diligence that the founder never knew about. You don't want that conversation happening in the middle of a deal. Fix it before it finds you.

And once you file, register your domain, your social handles, and your Amazon brand registry immediately. Not eventually. Immediately.

## Don't Let a Deadline Rush You Into the Wrong Name

Here's what I should have done with Suja: started the naming process earlier. We were down to the wire. 50 stores approved and no brand name.

We got lucky. Eric sat next to the right person at the right time.

You can't build a brand strategy around luck. The Rule of Twos applies to naming too... it takes twice as long and costs twice as much as you think to do it right. Give yourself time. Start the clearance process during product development, not after you've got retailer approval.

Because "I'll figure out the name later" is a sentence that gets expensive fast.

And one more thing. This is the thought I come back to every time I help a founder think through naming.

**Products are copyable. Brands are not.**

Your formula, your packaging structure, your price point... all of it can be replicated by a well-funded competitor who sees you working. What they cannot replicate is the name you've built trust around over five years. The name consumers know. The name on the label that's been sitting at eye level in 30,000 stores.

That's the asset. The real asset. Not the formula, not the label design, not the distribution agreement.

The brand name is the thing they can't copy. Which means it's worth protecting from day one.

Not from day 300. Not after your first raise. From the very start.
