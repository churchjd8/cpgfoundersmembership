The first time I delivered product to a Whole Foods distribution center, I drove a 28-foot truck through a line of 53-foot semis, surrounded by professional drivers who did this for a living.

Then I tried to unload the pallets with a forklift I had absolutely no business operating.

I sliced into a pallet. Beet juice went everywhere.

I'm not sure the dock crew had seen anything quite like it. I was the CEO of the company and I was staining their receiving dock a deep shade of magenta. We cleaned it up, got the product in, and I drove back to San Diego with my pride sufficiently adjusted.

That day taught me something about distribution that no course ever could: getting product from your facility to a retailer's shelf is harder, messier, and more expensive than you think. The vehicle you choose to do it... matters enormously.

## The Decision Most Founders Underestimate

DSD. Direct store delivery. It sounds simple. Your distributor's truck pulls up to the store, a sales rep walks in, stocks the product, checks the date codes, faces the shelves, and builds a relationship with the store manager. Done.

Warehouse distribution sounds equally simple. You ship to a central distribution center. The retailer sends it out to stores from there. You never actually touch the retail floor.

The decision between those two models is one of the most consequential choices you'll make in CPG... and most founders treat it like a logistics question when it's actually a margin question. A sales strategy question. A brand question.

## What DSD Actually Is

In practice, DSD is a network of independent beverage or food distributors who serve specific geographies. For refrigerated beverages, the classic DSD network might be regional. Your product moves through a Pepsi bottler in the Northeast, an independent beverage distributor in the mid-Atlantic, and a specialty food DSD company on the West Coast. You're selling to each of them. They're each selling to retailers in their territory.

That means you have in-store human beings who know the store managers, who can get you endcap placement, who can pull a near-date product before it becomes a problem, and who can reset your section faster than any retailer's own crew.

That's the argument for DSD.

The argument against it: you're paying for all of that. Typically 20 to 30 percent of your net revenue goes to the DSD distributor. Some categories run higher. And you lose visibility into what's happening at the store level unless you have a field team shadowing them.

Warehouse distribution to a retailer's DC typically costs 10 to 15 percent of net revenue. The trade-off is that you're now relying on the retailer's own distribution system to get product to the shelf, and nobody at the store is particularly watching your brand.

The pennies matter. CPG is a "penny profit" business, and the difference between 10 percent distribution cost and 28 percent distribution cost is often the difference between a viable business and a money-losing one.

## When DSD Is the Right Call

At Suja, we launched with a 22-day shelf life. Think about what that means logistically: we couldn't ship product far. We couldn't absorb long warehouse holding times. We needed product moving fast, freshness validated at the store level, and near-date product rotated before it became a write-down.

DSD was not a choice for us in those early years. It was a structural requirement.

If you're in refrigerated or any product with a shelf life under 90 days, DSD's in-store presence isn't a luxury. It's your freshness insurance policy. The $1 million spoilage deduction bill we absorbed early on, when a distributor agreement didn't properly allocate that risk, taught me this the hard way.

> Trust must be documented. And shelf life determines far more than how long a product stays fresh. It influences where you can distribute, how much inventory you can safely carry, and ultimately how large your territory can become.

DSD also makes sense when you're in a highly competitive, cluttered category where in-store execution is the battleground. Energy drinks. Craft beverages. Better-for-you snacks fighting for impulse placement. If you're counting on the retailer's warehouse team to ensure your facings are right, your shelves are stocked, and your expired product is out... you're counting on the wrong people.

In those categories, the DSD rep who has a relationship with the store manager gets endcap placement. The brand relying on warehouse allocation gets whatever's left.

## When Warehouse Makes More Sense

As our shelf life at Suja grew from 22 days to roughly 100 days, our distribution options changed dramatically. Suddenly we could ship to retailers' DCs. We could serve geographies we couldn't reach before. Warehouse gave us the national scale DSD couldn't.

That's the natural trajectory for a lot of beverage brands: DSD locally, then a hybrid model, then a gradual transition toward warehouse as the product proves itself and shelf life permits.

Shelf-stable products, broadly speaking, should default to warehouse unless there's a compelling category-specific reason for DSD. The economics just work better. You get predictable logistics costs, clean invoicing, and the ability to scale geographically without rebuilding a patchwork of regional distributor relationships every time you expand.

And here's a reality most founders don't talk about: warehouse puts you in control of your inventory position. With DSD, you often don't know exactly what a distributor is holding, what their sell-through looks like, or when they're about to dump near-date product at a discount that undercuts your pricing elsewhere.

Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains. A DSD network that tells you they're "in" 2,000 doors means nothing until you see the movement data.

## The Hybrid Model (And Why It's Harder Than It Sounds)

Most scaling CPG brands end up running a hybrid: DSD in markets where they need on-the-ground presence, warehouse for national chains where they have enough velocity to earn the DC relationship.

This sounds elegant. In practice it creates real operational complexity. Your team is managing two completely different distribution relationships, two different margin structures, two different inventory models. Your pricing architecture has to hold across both. And when a national retailer wants to bring you into their warehouse system while you're still DSD-dependent in key regions, you have to figure out how to transition without blowing up the distributor relationships you've spent years building.

The Rule of Twos applies here, as it does everywhere in CPG: whatever you think the complexity of managing dual distribution systems will be, double it. Whatever you think the transition will cost in time and money, double it.

## The Three Questions That Should Drive the Decision

Before you lock in a distribution model, answer these.

**One: What's your shelf life, realistically?** Not on your best batch, not in ideal conditions. What does a distributor actually get when they receive your product, and how much time does the retailer have before it's a problem? If the answer is under 60 days, you probably need DSD or at least a hybrid.

**Two: How much in-store execution does your category require?** If your category is mature, well-merchandised, and your primary job is keeping a facing stocked, warehouse is probably fine. If your category is competitive, requires frequent resets, and lives or dies on endcap placement and impulse, DSD is buying you something real.

**Three: Can your margins support the cost?** Run the actual math. Take your current or target gross margin. Subtract your DSD distributor cost. What's left before marketing, overhead, and everything else? If the answer is a number that makes a sustainable business, proceed. If you're engineering a loss at retail, you're building a company on borrowed time.

Revenue without margin is ego. And a distribution model that burns through your margin faster than your volume can offset it is one of the quieter ways CPG brands fail.

## The Most Expensive Mistake I See

Founders who fall in love with their DSD network.

I understand the appeal. Your DSD reps are on the ground. They know the store managers by name. They text you photos of your endcap display. They feel like a sales team you don't have to fully pay for.

But DSD relationships can also become a trap. Once you've seeded your brand through a regional distributor, exiting that relationship to go warehouse is messy, potentially litigious, and almost always slower than you planned. Some founders stay on DSD two or three years longer than the economics justify because the transition feels too complicated.

The time to think about your distribution evolution is before you sign the agreement. Know your off-ramp before you get on the highway.

Gross margin determines destiny. And in CPG, destiny often gets decided not in the pitch deck or on the buyer call, but in the distribution contract you sign at 2 a.m. when you're just trying to get the product moving.

Plan soberly. The beet juice will find a way out. Make sure your margins don't end up splattered on the dock with it.
