There's a question I hear in almost every coaching conversation with early-stage founders. It comes after we've talked about gross margin and cash runway and fundraising strategy. And it comes out a little sheepishly, like the person isn't sure they're allowed to ask it.

"How much should I be paying myself?"

The honest answer is: it depends on where you are in the business. But there's a framework behind that answer, and most founders I've worked with get it wrong in one of two directions.

They either pay themselves so little they burn out... or they start paying themselves too much, too early, and slowly lose the trust of everyone around them.

I've made both mistakes. Not at the same company. But I've made them. And across 30 years and 8 companies, I've watched dozens of other founders make them too.

Here's what I've learned.

## The Two Ways Founders Get This Wrong

**The martyrdom trap.** This one is more common. Founders pay themselves $0 or something embarrassingly small, wear it like a badge of honor, and let the financial pressure bleed quietly into every other part of their life and their leadership.

I understand the impulse. Every dollar you take is a dollar the company doesn't have. It feels noble. It signals commitment.

But here's the problem: when you can't pay your mortgage, can't take your spouse to dinner, can't stop worrying about your personal cash flow... that anxiety gets into the walls. It affects your decisions. It makes you desperate. And desperation shows.

"Enthusiasm attracts. Desperation repels." I've said that for years, mostly in the context of fundraising. But it's true everywhere. It applies to investors, to retail buyers, to the talent you're trying to recruit. A founder who's quietly drowning personally is not making great business decisions. They're making survival decisions. Those are different things.

**The entitled trap.** On the other end, I've watched founders raise a Series A and immediately bump their salary to $225,000. The company is burning $400,000 a month. Revenue is $1.5 million annually. Investors aren't paying themselves anything. And the founder just became the highest-paid person in the building.

That's not a capital allocation decision. That's a trust erosion event.

The trust you burn with early investors, the people who took a real swing on you before the product was proven, is very hard to earn back. And it compounds in the wrong direction. Future investors talk to current investors. The word gets around.

The question isn't just "what can I justify?" It's "what does this signal about how I think about this business?"

## The Stage-by-Stage Framework

Here's how I think about founder compensation across the four life stages of a CPG brand.

**Stage 1: Pre-revenue to roughly $1M in annual revenue.**

Target range: $50,000 to $80,000.

That's real money. It won't make you rich in a high-cost city, but it's enough to stay focused on the business instead of your bank account. If you're facing genuine financial hardship at that number, up to $90,000 is defensible, but have a direct conversation with your investors first. No surprises.

What you should absolutely not do is take $0 and then quietly run up company expenses that are really personal expenses. I've watched that pattern destroy investor relationships, and it destroys them in a particularly bad way, because the conversation becomes about trust rather than strategy.

**Stage 2: $1M to $5M in annual revenue.**

Target range: $100,000 to $135,000.

You've proven the concept is real. You have paying customers and (hopefully) climbing velocity. You're probably in your first institutional round. At this stage, your salary shouldn't be the company's biggest fixed cost, but it should be real enough that you're not constantly distracted by personal financial stress.

If you have a co-founder, they need to be in a similar range. I can't tell you how many companies I've seen fracture because one co-founder needed money and quietly took it, and the other didn't, and six months later the resentment was visible from across the room. Address compensation parity between co-founders early. It's uncomfortable. Do it anyway.

**Stage 3: $5M to $20M in annual revenue.**

Target range: $150,000 to $200,000.

At this point you've raised meaningful capital, built a team, taken on real operating complexity. You're not running a side project. You're running a company. The compensation should reflect that.

One rule I apply here: your salary shouldn't exceed the gross margin contribution of your top-performing SKU without a compelling reason. That sounds abstract, but it's a useful gut check. You want to be able to look any investor in the eye and show them that your personal economics are still aligned with theirs.

**Stage 4: $20M+ in revenue, approaching scale or exit.**

Target range: $200,000 to $275,000.

At this stage, you've earned it. And frankly, if you're not in this range by the time you're running a $20M business, you may start losing your best people. They can see what you're sacrificing, and they start to wonder when the picture changes.

This is also the stage where the narrative shifts. It's no longer "are you committed?" It becomes "are you sustainable?" Sustainable leaders need to be compensated like sustainable leaders.

## What Investors Are Actually Looking At

When investors review your financials (and they will, carefully), your salary is one of the most visible line items. They're not necessarily looking for the lowest number. They're looking for a number that tells them you understand capital allocation.

A salary that's too low makes them wonder whether you can sustain this for the long term. A salary that's too high makes them wonder whether you're prioritizing yourself over the business.

What they want to see is consistency and intention. The same thing they want to see everywhere else in your financials.

Here's something I've told founders for years: "Capital amplifies whatever foundation you've built." If your capital allocation habits are sloppy before the raise, they'll be sloppy after. The salary line is a window into how you think about money. Investors know this. They've seen it a hundred times.

## The Questions You Need to Answer First

Before you set your salary, there are a few things worth knowing about yourself.

**What is your current runway?** If you have less than 12 months, your salary conversation needs to look different than if you have 24. I always try to maintain 18 months of runway and I make compensation decisions around that constraint. When cash is tight, a temporary salary reduction is not a failure. It's leadership.

**What do your investors know?** The minute you change your compensation, your early investors should know. Not later. Not in the next quarterly update. When you're deciding. I've watched founder-investor relationships fracture not over the number itself, but over the fact that the founder made a unilateral decision without communicating. These aren't employees making a benefits choice. These are partners operating a shared asset. Treat them accordingly.

**What are you signaling to your team?** The people who've been with you since the beginning are watching what you pay yourself. If you take a 60% raise in the same quarter you hold compensation flat across the team... that's a culture event. Plan accordingly. Consistency between how you treat yourself and how you treat the people who built the company alongside you is one of the most powerful cultural signals you'll ever send.

## The Thing Nobody Talks About

Here's a number that shapes how I think about all of this.

Across 30+ years, I've spent roughly half my career raising capital. And I've probably spent 5 of those 30 years actually making real money on a personal basis. Most of the wealth I've generated came at exit, not in annual salary.

That's a feature, not a bug.

The equity you protect early is worth infinitely more than a salary bump if you build something real. At Suja, the angels who stayed patient and let the business grow saw their initial investment return roughly 10x. That patience, that alignment, doesn't happen when founders have been extracting value all along.

"Revenue without margin is ego." Same logic applies here. A big salary before you've built the business is ego. The real payday comes later.

Pay yourself enough to stay in the game. Not so much that you slow it down.
