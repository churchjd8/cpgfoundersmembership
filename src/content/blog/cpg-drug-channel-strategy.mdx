I watched a founder I know spend almost $400,000 to get into CVS. Paid the slotting. Got distribution set up through a DSD partner. Earned 4 feet of shelf space across 3,200 stores.

Fourteen months later, they were out. Half the stores gone by month eleven. Gone entirely by month fourteen.

The product was good. The brand had real credentials. She did everything she thought she was supposed to do.

What she didn't understand was what she was actually buying when she wrote that check.

---

The drug channel... CVS, Walgreens, Rite Aid... is the most misunderstood shelf in CPG. Founders see 10,000 CVS stores and do the math in their heads: $300M in potential revenue. And they stop thinking right there.

Here's what they miss. The person walking into CVS on a Tuesday afternoon is not in discovery mode. They came for Tylenol, their prescription, and maybe a Diet Coke on the way out. They are on a mission. They are not browsing your new functional beverage.

That's not a knock on the channel. It's just the truth. And if you don't understand it going in, you'll spend your way to zero before you figure it out.

I've watched this happen with health and wellness brands, snack brands, beverage brands. Same pattern every time. The channel is attractive because of the store count. The founder mistakes "distribution" for "velocity." And when velocity is low -- which it almost always is for emerging brands in drug -- the retailer cuts you. Because the drug channel does not have the patience to wait for your brand to build awareness.

Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains. Nowhere does it apply more than drug.

## When Does Drug Actually Make Sense?

Later than you think.

The typical sequencing for a health or wellness brand looks like this. You earn credibility in natural -- UNFI, KeHE, Whole Foods, Sprouts. You build velocity. You build a consumer base that actually knows your brand. Then you take it to Target or conventional grocery with scan data proving people buy it when they see it. You use that conventional success to open conversations with mass market and club.

Drug fits in that middle zone. Most brands should have $5M-$10M+ in annual revenue and demonstrated velocity in at least 2,000-3,000 conventional doors before pursuing CVS or Walgreens at any real scale.

Why? Because the drug category buyer is not in the business of building your brand. They are in the business of efficient assortment management. If you walk in with a beautiful pitch deck but no scan data showing consistent velocity... you are not getting meaningful placement. And if you get in before you're ready, the math will expose you. The category manager will see the numbers. And they will not be forgiving.

Most founders hear this and say "yeah but our product is different." It's not. The channel dynamics don't care about your sourcing story.

## The Velocity Math Is Brutal

Every channel has its own benchmark -- the sales-per-store-per-week number you need to hit to justify your shelf space. In natural grocery, a small emerging brand might survive at $4-6 per store per week while building awareness. In conventional grocery, the bar is typically $8-12 per store per week. Drug? You need to be at $12-20 per store per week for most health and wellness SKUs, and the tolerance for underperformers is low.

CVS runs annual category reviews. Their category managers are managing hundreds of SKUs. They are not emotionally invested in your story. They are looking at dollars per linear foot, units per store per week, and margin contribution. That's the whole conversation.

If you're below the velocity hurdle at your first review, you may not even get a second conversation.

At Rowdy Energy, I paid nearly $1 million in one-time slotting fees to get into Albertsons -- a conventional channel with similar economics. We were below velocity hurdle. Discontinued after eight months. That was a hard, expensive lesson. Drug is even less forgiving because the category manager has less patience and less shelf room to absorb an underperformer who isn't pulling their weight.

"Revenue without margin is ego." But velocity without plan is just expensive shelf space.

## The Trade Spend Math Is Different in Drug

In conventional grocery, you're running 4-8 promotional events per year. TPRs, feature ads, display programs. The economics feel familiar.

In drug, the promotional mechanic runs through loyalty programs -- ExtraCare at CVS, myWalgreens at Walgreens. These programs are deeply embedded in how shoppers engage with the channel. "Buy two, get $3 in ExtraCare Bucks." That's the language. And those programs are expensive to participate in.

The economics on a drug channel promotional event look very different from a BOGO at Sprouts. You need to model the full landed cost before you commit. I've watched brands spend 35-40% of gross revenue on trade in drug without realizing it until the P&L came back and margins had gone negative.

Revenue without margin is ego. The drug channel will expose your economics faster than almost any other channel.

Before you accept a single door at CVS, build a full P&L by account. What is the gross margin of this item after deductions, trade spend, DSD or distribution fees, and shrink? If you're not at 35%+ gross margin on the product going into drug, you're walking into a money-losing account. CPG is a "penny profit" business. The pennies matter. And in drug, those pennies are razor thin.

The other thing that surprises founders in drug: shrink. Drug stores carry a lot of high-shrink categories -- health, beauty, OTC. Loss prevention practices vary widely. If your product ends up in a high-shrink aisle in high-shrink markets, your actual sell-through numbers and your shipment numbers will diverge quickly. Factor that into your model.

## Where You Live on the Shelf Matters More Than You Think

In natural grocery, the consumer is browsing. They'll find you in the refrigerated section or supplement aisle if you're well-positioned.

In drug, the consumer has blinders on. They go directly to the category where they expect to find what they need. If you're a functional wellness brand living next to cough medicine instead of near the vitamins and sports nutrition... you will not find your consumer. If your cold-pressed juice is in the wrong refrigerator door -- near the energy drinks instead of the health section -- velocity suffers.

Category placement in drug is a negotiation, not a given. You need to come in with data that makes the case for where you belong on the shelf. Ideally, data that shows the consumer who is already walking into drug for your specific category. If that consumer doesn't exist in meaningful numbers, drug is the wrong channel right now.

This sounds obvious. It almost never is when a founder is excited about getting into 3,000 new stores.

## Building the Relationship

Drug category managers are different from natural or specialty buyers. They are more analytical, less moved by brand narrative. The Whole Foods buyer might care about your founder mission and sourcing practices. The CVS category manager wants your IRI scan data, your promotional calendar, and your sell-through projections.

Come in with data. Know your category. Know what's happening in drug specifically -- not just across all channels. The category manager needs to see that you understand their shelf, their shopper, their competitive set. Category management is a conversation about shelf performance, not about your company story.

And show up proactively when you're underperforming. Don't wait for the buyer to come to you. The founders who build lasting drug channel relationships are the ones who pick up the phone before the category review, name the gap themselves, and come with a solution. "We're tracking 15% below velocity target in the Northeast division. Here's the promotional response we're putting in place."

That's the behavior that earns a second chance. That's also, by the way, the behavior that builds trust with buyers across every channel.

If you're sitting back waiting to see how it goes... you've already lost.

## The Short Version

Drug channel is a real opportunity for the right brand at the right stage.

But it is not a distribution shortcut. It is not a place to experiment with brand awareness. And it is definitely not where you go when you need the store count to feel better about your growth story.

Go in when you have velocity data that holds up in conventional doors. Go in with trade spend economics fully modeled before you commit. Go in with a clear case for where you'll live on the shelf and why that placement works for your specific consumer.

If your gross margin on the item can't support the trade spend math of CVS and Walgreens... don't go in yet. Fix the economics first. Then go.

The best brands don't get into every channel. They get into the right channels at the right time, with the discipline to say no to everything else until the moment is actually right.

Hope is not a strategy. And "we'll figure out the velocity once we're on shelf" is not a strategy either.

Dream boldly. Plan soberly.
