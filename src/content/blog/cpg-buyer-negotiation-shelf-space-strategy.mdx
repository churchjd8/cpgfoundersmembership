I want to tell you about a smoothie Suja made that nobody on the team believed in.

It was a Walmart-exclusive plant-based smoothie. Our head of operations hated the idea the moment it landed in the room. Complex to produce. Slow line speeds. Unit economics that made grown adults wince. We were already stretched across seven manufacturing locations and barely keeping up with demand on the products that were actually working.

Production told me in no uncertain terms: this is going to be a nightmare.

I heard them. And then we said yes anyway.

Here's what was actually happening. We wanted to expand our two-ounce wellness shot distribution through Walmart. The shots were performing well. High gross margin, strong velocity, consumers loved them. Expanding that line was worth real money to us... we're talking a meaningful slice of annual revenue. But the buyer wasn't just going to hand over more shelf space because we asked nicely. He needed something from us too.

What he needed was a Walmart-exclusive product. Something that gave his customers a reason to choose Walmart over the competition. Something that made him look like he was bringing innovation into the category.

So we made the smoothie.

It struggled. The line velocity never hit what we'd hoped. The flavor profile wasn't our best work. (Pea protein is... a specific experience.) We ran it, we learned from it, and eventually we moved on. But here's what happened in the meantime: we got the expanded shot distribution. And today, Walmart is one of Suja's largest customers.

The lesson wasn't about winning that negotiation. The lesson was about understanding leverage, thinking long-term, and making selective concessions to grow the whole relationship.

---

### What Buyers Actually Want

There's a thing I see founders do wrong over and over again. They walk into a buyer meeting trying to win. Trying to convince. Trying to get the buyer to come around to their point of view.

That's the wrong posture.

Buyers are under enormous pressure. They have a category to manage. A planogram to defend. A boss who's watching sell-through numbers like a hawk. The last thing they need is another founder who wants to lecture them about why their product is great.

What buyers want is a partner. Someone who makes their job easier. Someone who brings them ideas, not just asks.

When you understand what a buyer actually needs, you stop negotiating against them and start solving problems with them. That's a completely different dynamic. And it's the dynamic that keeps you on shelf.

---

### Play Their Idea First

One of the most effective things we did at Suja was try buyers' ideas before arguing for our own.

When a buyer had an idea that felt out there, or risky, or not quite aligned with where we wanted to take the brand... we often did it their way. If it worked, great. We learned something and they were happy. If it didn't work, we had something far more valuable: proof. And proof opened the door to revisiting our original proposal.

Either way, working with buyers' ideas bought us time on the shelf. And time on shelf is the asset. Every week you're on shelf is another week of data. Another week of building velocity. Another week of earning the right to be there.

"Play their idea first" sounds like you're giving something up. You're not. You're building credibility.

### The Costco Milk Experiment

We ran the same playbook at Costco, and it was even more uncomfortable.

We wanted to expand our wellness shot footprint in clubs. Strong velocity... we were doing roughly $2,000 per club per week on shots, which is significant in that channel. Costco's buyer liked the shots. But he also wanted us to develop a plant-based milk for his region.

Nobody at Suja had ever made plant-based milk. It was completely outside our core. My operations team looked at me like I'd lost my mind when I floated it.

We said yes anyway.

The milk wasn't a hit. The pea protein flavor profile wasn't something we ever got right, and we knew it from the first production run. But the shots expanded. And the Costco relationship deepened. We'd shown we were willing to invest in the partnership, not just extract from it.

That willingness changed the nature of every conversation we had with that buyer after.

### The Thing Most Founders Get Backwards

Most CPG founders think the job is to protect their brand. Never compromise. Never make something they don't fully believe in. Never give an inch.

I understand the impulse. But here's what it actually costs you: relationship capital. And relationship capital is the currency that keeps you on shelf when velocity dips, when a competitor comes after your space, or when the buyer has to make hard choices at the next reset.

"Retailers rarely become long-term partners because of one great product. They become long-term partners because they trust the people behind it."

That trust is built one interaction at a time. The smoothie nobody believed in was one of those interactions. The pea protein milk that struggled was another. They weren't wins on their own. But they were investments in a relationship that paid out over years.

---

### The Nine-Month Clock

Here's the hard truth about shelf space: once you get it, you're on a clock.

You have roughly nine months to prove you belong. Less in highly competitive categories. And if you're not performing, the buyer isn't going to wait for you to figure it out. The next reset comes, and you're gone.

So the question isn't just "how do I get on shelf?" The question is "what am I doing every week to earn the right to stay there?"

That's a different kind of discipline.

It means watching your velocity numbers obsessively. It means knowing where you sit against the median velocity for your set... being above the 50th percentile is the floor, not the ceiling. It means proactively surfacing problems before the buyer finds them.

---

### The Counterintuitive Move: Raise Your Hand First

This one surprises most founders.

When a SKU wasn't performing at Suja, we raised it ourselves. We went to the buyer before they came to us. We said, "Here's what we're seeing. Here's what we think is happening. Here's the plan."

Buyers were shocked the first time we did it. They were used to brands hiding problems, hoping the numbers would turn around.

Being the first to name a problem doesn't make you look weak. It makes you look like a partner. It signals that you're watching the same data they are. That you're thinking about the health of their category, not just your own position in it.

It also gives you control of the narrative. You're not on defense. You're coming in with a plan. Entirely different energy in that meeting.

---

### The Framework I Use Before Making a Concession

Not every ask from a buyer is worth accommodating. Here's how I think about it:

**Say yes when:** the concession builds relationship capital that's worth more than what you're giving up... when the buyer's idea gives you something in return (shelf space, expanded distribution, favorable placement)... when the downside is bounded (you can test it and exit if it fails)... when saying no damages a relationship that took years to build.

**Say no when:** the concession requires you to violate your brand values or mislead consumers... when the economics are so bad they actually damage the business even if the relationship grows... when the buyer is asking for something that would harm your other retail partners (pricing integrity matters).

The smoothie fell into the first category. We knew the economics were tough, but the upside was real and the exit was available if it didn't work. We took the risk. It paid off.

### What the Shelf Teaches You

There's a line I heard from Jody Cnossen, one of the sharpest retail minds I've ever worked with. She said it simply: "The truth of the business is at the shelf."

She meant it literally. Not in your spreadsheet, not in your pitch deck, not in your office. At the shelf. That's where consumers decide. That's where velocity is born or dies. That's where your retail relationships are either cemented or eroded.

The best CPG founders I know spend time at shelf. They watch consumers shop. They watch their product get passed over. They watch a competitor with worse packaging get picked up because it's in a better position, or because there's a demo happening that day, or because the facing count is three versus their one.

And then they go back to their buyer and say: "Here's what I saw. Here's what I want to change. Here's what I need from you."

That's not a negotiation. That's a partnership conversation.

And it's the conversation that builds a brand.
