Early 2019.

The celery juice trend was on fire. Anthony William had written about it. *The Rachael Ray Show* had amplified it. Consumers were hunting for celery juice in every grocery store in America — and Trader Joe's, who moves faster than almost anyone in conventional grocery, was watching in real time.

Their buyer called us. They wanted celery juice. Fast. Their normal preference would have been to develop their own private label, but that process would have taken months they didn't have. So they came to Suja.

From that call to product on shelves nationwide: under six weeks.

People hear that story and they think it's about Suja's execution. It was, partly. But the real lesson is simpler.

We were ready.

Not ready for *that* call specifically. Ready for calls of *that type*. We'd built the manufacturing flexibility, the supply chain relationships, and the internal muscle to respond to a window like that. Because we'd done fast sprints before, we knew exactly what six weeks looked like.

That's the foundation of everything I'm about to share. Limited editions — LTOs — aren't a tactic you improvise when a buyer calls. The brands that win with them consistently do so because they've built the capability, and the discipline, to move.

---

## Why "Limited" Is One of the Most Powerful Words in Retail

A buyer's default posture is risk-aversion. They've been burned by brands that launched with fanfare, occupied shelf real estate for six months, and moved nothing. So when you walk in with something permanent, you're asking them to make a multi-year bet on an unproven hypothesis.

When you walk in with something limited? You've lowered the bar.

"It's a rotation. A seasonal test. Four months of data for both of us."

That's an easier yes. And an easier yes gets you in the door, gets you velocity data, and gives you real ammunition for the permanent placement conversation six months later.

I watched this play out at Costco over and over. Their rotational model is essentially a built-in LTO engine. You have a window — roughly six to eight weeks — to perform. Hit the velocity threshold and you earn your way into the everyday set. Don't hit it, and you wind down gracefully and come back with a stronger offer.

The brands who understood that weren't demoralized by rotations. They were *data-gathering*. Each one taught them something about price point, format, flavor, or positioning. The brands who got stuck demanding permanent placement too early often stumbled — not because their product was bad, but because they were asking the buyer to commit before the data supported it.

"Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains."

I say that constantly. A rotation gives you distribution. What you need to prove is velocity. Those are different things.

## Five Strategic Uses of an LTO

Not every limited edition is the same. Going in without clarity on *why* you're doing it is how you burn resources without learning anything.

Here's a framework I use with founders in my programs.

**1. Trial Generation**

The most straightforward use. A new flavor, a new format, something designed to pull in a consumer who hasn't tried your core line yet. The goal isn't the LTO itself — it's converting that trial customer into a repeat buyer of your permanent range.

If the LTO doesn't ladder back to your core brand, you're just spending money to create loyalty to something you can't keep making.

**2. Trend Capture**

This is what happened with the Trader Joe's call. A macro trend erupts, you have the capability to respond, and you respond fast. The brand picks up the halo. The retailer gets the trend. Neither of you is locked into it long-term.

The key here: be honest about whether the trend has legs. Celery juice had its moment, but that moment wasn't forever, and we weren't building a celery juice business. We were riding a wave and adding new Suja customers while the wave was up. When the wave came down, we wound down gracefully.

Trend capture only works if you don't fall in love with the trend.

**3. Retailer Relationship Building**

A well-executed LTO is one of the best ways to build credibility with a buyer. You come in with a limited program, you hit your numbers, you execute cleanly, you wind down without leaving a mess. That's capital for the next conversation.

Suja didn't earn its permanent Trader Joe's programs by demanding them. We earned them by showing up prepared, executing cleanly, and making the buyer look good on a seasonal rotation first. The trust built in those sprints was worth more than any pitch deck.

**4. Press and Brand Awareness**

"Limited edition" gets coverage. "Available now, but not forever" activates consumer urgency in a way a new permanent SKU rarely does.

If you're still building awareness, a seasonal LTO can generate earned media you couldn't buy. It gives editors and creators a reason to cover you beyond the initial launch. "Holiday flavors drop this week" is a story. "New flavor added to permanent lineup" rarely makes anyone's calendar.

The scarcity is the story.

**5. Permanent SKU Incubation**

This is the one founders undervalue the most.

Before you commit your co-manufacturer capacity, your supply chain lead times, and your working capital to a permanent SKU, launch it as an LTO. Run it in one or two channels. See what the velocity tells you. If it exceeds your threshold for permanent performance, you've done real consumer research at retail. If it doesn't, you've limited your downside to one season.

At Suja, some of our best permanent items started as seasonal offerings we just couldn't stop making. The market told us what to do — we didn't have to guess.

## The Mistakes I See Founders Make

**Going too broad, too fast.**

Your LTO is in thirty retailers in twenty markets before you've proven the concept anywhere. You have complexity everywhere, velocity nowhere, and a supply chain headache that takes six months to unwind.

Start small. One chain. One region. Prove it, then scale it. The rule applies here the same as it does for initial distribution sequencing.

**Underestimating the operational lift.**

The Rule of Twos applies here without exception. Whatever you think the LTO will cost to develop and execute, it'll be twice that. Whatever timeline you're working with, double it in your planning. The brands who go in clear-eyed about that can still make money on it. The ones who don't usually break even at best.

**No clear hypothesis.**

If you're launching an LTO without a defined question you're trying to answer — "we're testing whether our consumer wants a holiday flavor at this price point" or "we're using this rotation to open the door at a new retailer" — you're spending money on an experiment with no way to grade the results.

Know why before you start.

**No exit plan.**

Every LTO needs a wind-down strategy built in from day one. How much inventory are you making? What's the plan if the rotation ends and you have product left? How are you communicating the program close to your retail partners?

The brands who handle this well build credibility. The ones who don't leave a mess — literally and figuratively.

## How Do You Know When an LTO Should Go Permanent?

I get asked this in almost every coaching conversation.

The answer is velocity. Always velocity.

For a natural channel brand, you want to see at least 3-5 units per store per week during the LTO window before you seriously consider permanent placement. In conventional, the threshold varies by category, but you're looking for performance that at minimum matches your existing permanent items in that account.

Don't make the call based on enthusiasm from your sales rep. Don't make it because the buyer asked nicely. Don't make it because you sold through your first inventory run in two weeks.

Make it because the turns-per-store-per-week tell you the consumer keeps reaching for it — week after week, not just on the initial curiosity purchase.

"The truth of the business is at the shelf." That truth doesn't lie.

If the velocity is there, you have your answer. If it isn't — even if the feedback is overwhelmingly positive, even if your DMs are full of people asking where they can find it — stay honest with yourself.

You can always bring it back with a stronger offer next season. What you can't easily undo is the supply chain complexity of a permanent SKU that doesn't pull.

## Come Back to the Six Weeks

I want to close where I started.

The Trader Joe's call came in. Six weeks later, celery juice with a Suja label was on shelves nationwide. That wasn't a miracle. It was the payoff on operational infrastructure that had been built, quietly, over the years leading up to it.

The manufacturing partners who knew how to sprint. The supply chain relationships that could flex. The internal team that had practiced moving fast without losing quality. None of that happened because of that phone call. It happened on a hundred ordinary Tuesdays before the call ever came.

The LTO playbook isn't just about picking the right trend or negotiating the right rotation. It's about building the kind of operation that can say yes when the window opens.

Because the windows are real. And they're narrow.

Dream boldly. Plan soberly. And when the call comes, be ready to move.
