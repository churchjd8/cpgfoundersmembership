There's a story I think about every time a founder tells me they're about to hire an influencer for their launch.

In 2012, Celsius was dying. Not "struggling" dying. Actually dying. Revenue was flat. The product was decent, the concept was solid, but the business was bleeding and nobody could figure out why.

The board brought in a new CEO. Financial operator, not a brand guy. He walked in, looked at the P&L, looked at where the money was going, and made one sweeping decision on day one: cut everything that wasn't close to the shelf.

No big brand marketing. No influencers. No PR campaigns. No "awareness spend." Just trade spend. Just demos. Just in-store execution. Pure proximity to the shelf, and nothing else.

He told me later: "I stopped everything away from the shelf on day one. I didn't get back into it until about three years later" -- around 2021 -- "because by then we had plenty of money to spend on marketing further from the shelf. You go too far away from the shelf, it just bounces."

The rest of the Celsius story you probably know. Today it's one of the fastest-growing energy drinks in the country, worth billions.

What he did isn't complicated. It's just deeply counterintuitive to founders who've been told to "build the brand."

---

## The Mistake That's Easy to Make

When sales are slow, the instinct is to get louder.

Run a social media campaign. Get the product into the hands of an influencer. Pitch the brand to a food publication. Build the brand story. Invest in awareness.

I get it. It *feels* like the right move. You've got a great product, and the world just doesn't know about it yet. You're not getting the velocity you expected. So you look for ways to reach more people.

But here's the thing about early-stage CPG that nobody tells you clearly enough: the further your marketing spend is from the shelf, the harder it is to measure, the longer the lag before it shows up in velocity, and the lower your ROI.

At Suja, we ran billboards in Manhattan when we had four Whole Foods stores in the entire city. Four. We created awareness with nowhere to convert it.

That's a real number. Four stores. Billboards across New York City.

We thought we were building the brand. What we were actually doing was burning capital at a moment when we couldn't afford to. The people who saw those billboards couldn't find our product. The awareness bounced, exactly like the Celsius CEO said.

"You go too far away from the shelf, it just bounces."

---

## The Proximity Framework

Here's how I think about CPG marketing spend, especially in the early years.

There's a spectrum. On one end, you have **trade spend** -- slotting fees, TPRs, off-shelf displays, scan promotions. This is as close to the shelf as it gets. The consumer is standing in the aisle, about to make a decision, and you're incentivizing the trial right there. The ROI lag is days, not months.

Next comes **in-store demos**. Your product in someone's hand, in the store, near the shelf. The conversion window is minutes. You're spending to generate trial at the exact moment the consumer has access to the product.

Then **sampling programs** -- getting product in front of people who could realistically find it at retail. Events, gyms, wellness studios, farmers markets. Still pretty close to shelf if you're doing it right.

Further out: **digital advertising targeted to your distribution footprint**. Not national campaigns, not awareness plays -- but geo-targeted social ads pointed at people who can actually buy within a mile of a store that carries you.

Even further: **earned media and PR**. Great if you can get it, but there's a real lag between a feature in a publication and a velocity bump on Nielsen.

Furthest out: **national brand building** -- influencer campaigns, brand partnerships, national advertising. High-ceiling, but this is the stuff that requires massive scale before it moves the needle.

The mistake I see over and over: founders jump straight to the far end of the spectrum before they've earned it.

---

## The 90% Rule

When you're early-stage -- call it $0 to $3 million in net revenue -- at least 90% of your marketing budget should be close to the shelf.

Trade spend program first. Then demos, especially in the natural channel where trial converts well and brand affinity is high. Then targeted local digital once you have real distribution to support it.

The exception is online retail. Amazon and your own DTC website give you natural national distribution from day one -- no gatekeeper, no physical footprint required. For those channels, digital marketing makes sense earlier because the conversion path is direct. But for brick-and-mortar, you have to earn the right to spend far from the shelf. You don't start there.

This isn't a theory. I'm living it right now at Prota, my current venture. My partner -- brilliant, hardworking, first-time CPG founder -- wanted to lead with influencers and build social media presence first. I fought him on it. Hard. I've been through too many launches to bet capital on brand awareness before we have the distribution to convert it.

The priority order matters. Trade spend first. Then demos. Then earned media. Then broader digital. Then national brand building.

You can always move up the chain as you scale. Moving backward is brutal.

---

## When You've Earned the Right to Spend Further from the Shelf

The transition point isn't a specific revenue number. It's a set of conditions that have to be true at the same time.

You need velocity that's working in your existing doors. If your velocity is strong -- meaning consumers who find the product are actually buying it repeatedly -- then adding brand awareness spend on top of that velocity creates a multiplier effect. You're filling a funnel that converts.

If velocity is weak, more awareness just sends more people to a shelf experience that disappoints them. You've wasted the spend and potentially created a negative first impression at scale.

You also need sufficient distribution density. If you're in 200 doors spread across six states, national influencer campaigns are going to create awareness in markets where you don't exist yet. That doesn't convert. That bounces.

Once you have both -- strong velocity in enough doors to create regional or national density -- you start unlocking the higher-funnel plays. That's around the $5 to $15 million range for most brands, though it depends heavily on your category and channel mix.

The Celsius CEO waited three years. Three years of grinding close to the shelf before touching the bigger-ticket marketing categories. Then when he finally opened those budgets, the infrastructure was there to convert the awareness into actual sales.

That patience is what built a billion-dollar company.

## The Hard Conversation With Your Co-Founder

Here's the version of this conversation that happens a lot: one founder is a brand person, one is an operator.

The brand person sees the big vision. They want to build the brand story, find the right ambassador, create content that resonates. They have taste and they want to use it. This is valuable.

The operator sees the cash position and the velocity numbers. They know how thin the runway is and they want every dollar working hard against the shelf.

These two perspectives aren't wrong. They're both right -- at different stages.

The brand person's instincts become indispensable once you have the velocity to support them. The operator's instincts protect you from burning the whole thing down before you get there.

Hope is not a strategy. Neither is brand awareness at $1 million in revenue.

The sequencing is: build the velocity machine first. Then build the brand on top of it.

You can market your way into trial. You cannot market your way into loyalty. And you definitely cannot market your way out of thin velocity with spend that lives on Instagram.

---

## The Takeaway

If you're early-stage and your marketing budget is going predominantly to brand awareness, influencer marketing, or national media plays -- stop.

Pull it back to the shelf. Max out your trade spend. Double your demo program. Build regional density before building national awareness.

When the Celsius CEO turned that company around, he didn't do it with a better Instagram strategy. He did it by getting obsessed with what happened two feet from the shelf.

That's still the game. Even now. Even with all the channels that didn't exist ten years ago.

The fundamentals don't change. The shelf is where CPG brands are built. Everything else supports it. Don't let it become the other way around.
