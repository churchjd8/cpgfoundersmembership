It was an internal debate that got more heated than I expected.

We had just started prototyping wellness shots at Suja. Two ounces. Single-serve. Completely different form factor from our sixteen-ounce cold-pressed juices. And somebody in the room made a reasonable argument: "Jeff, is this really Suja? Or should this be its own thing?"

I understood the instinct. The shots felt different. They looked different. The use occasion was different — not a breakfast ritual, not a detox cleanse, but a quick functional hit on the way to the gym. Someone floated the idea of a sub-brand. Someone else suggested a sister brand entirely, with Suja as a parent company behind it.

We went back and forth for probably two meetings.

Then I asked the only question that actually mattered: *"Does our consumer follow us here?"*

The Suja customer was a health-conscious woman in her twenties and thirties, shopping Whole Foods and Sprouts, already buying our juices. She was absolutely the person buying wellness shots. Same retailer. Same occasion. Same belief system. Same "health without the punishment" ethos that had defined every product decision we'd ever made.

We kept it under Suja.

That decision — made quietly in a conference room in San Diego — turned out to be one of the most important we made. Those shots became about sixty percent gross margin at a time when our outsourced kombucha business was running at roughly twelve. When Coca-Cola pulled back and we had a cash crisis on our hands, the shots were what kept the lights on.

If we'd launched them as a separate brand? We'd have been building two velocity stories simultaneously. Two trade spend budgets. Two sets of slotting investments. Two consumer awareness campaigns... on the marketing budget of a mid-stage brand. It would have been a disaster.

---

Here's what I've learned from building eight companies and watching hundreds of founders face this same question:

**Most founders default to one of two wrong instincts.**

The first instinct is to extend the brand into everything vaguely adjacent. "We're a wellness brand, so Suja wellness tea, Suja protein bars, Suja collagen gummies..." and before you know it you've got a product portfolio that your consumer can't understand and your retail buyer can't merchandise coherently.

The second instinct is to launch a new brand every time you have a new idea. "This product is different enough that it deserves its own identity." And they're off building brand number two before brand number one has cleared four dollars in velocity per store per week.

Both instincts are expensive. The Rule of Twos applies here as much as anywhere: a new brand takes twice as long and costs twice as much to build as you think. A diluted brand takes twice as long to fix.

---

**Three architectures. Know which one you're building.**

The CPG world runs on three basic models:

**1. The Branded House.** One name. Everything under it. Apple does this. Suja did this. When it works, every product launch gets the equity lift of your master brand. Consumer trust transfers. Retailer relationships transfer. Marketing dollars compound rather than fragment.

The risk: one bad product can damage the whole brand. And if you extend too far — into categories where your brand has no permission — you erode the very equity you've built.

**2. The House of Brands.** Independent brands, each standing on their own. P&G runs like this — most consumers don't know that Tide, Pampers, and Gillette share a corporate parent. Each brand is built from scratch, optimized for its category.

This model costs a lot more. You're building consumer trust, retailer relationships, and market presence independently for each brand. Unless you have P&G's resources, most emerging founders have no business running a house of brands. It is not a startup model.

**3. The Endorsed or Sub-brand Model.** Something like "Suja Organics" or "X by Suja." You get the equity of the parent brand while creating some visual and conceptual distance for a product that serves a different occasion or sits at a different price point.

This one requires real discipline. The sub-brand has to be meaningfully different from the core — not just a different flavor or size — or you've just confused your consumer for no reason.

---

**The five questions I ask before any brand architecture decision:**

**1. Does your consumer follow you there?**

This is the foundational question. Not "does this product fit in our category" — does your *actual buyer* see this new product as something you should be making? If you're a cold-pressed juice brand and your consumer is already buying wellness shots, yes. If you're a premium hot sauce brand and you want to launch a salad dressing, maybe. If you're a performance nutrition brand and you want to launch a toddler snack line... that's a very different consumer, different channel, different occasion. You're not extending a brand. You're building a new one.

**2. What does the brand permission cover?**

Every brand has a territory of permission — the space where consumers will believe you and follow you. Suja's permission was "functional, premium, plant-based wellness." Shots fit. Kombuchas fit. A Suja sparkling water probably fits. A Suja burger patty? No. That's not what we mean by our brand, and consumers know it even if the founder doesn't.

If you're honest about where your permission ends, the architecture decision almost makes itself.

**3. What does it cost to build a new brand vs. extend?**

Here's the financial reality most founders skip. A new brand means new trade spend at every account you sell into. New slotting fees. New field marketing to build trial. New packaging design, new trademark, new website, new social. New velocity story to tell every buyer from scratch.

That's easily $500K to $1M before the brand has any real traction. For a company doing under $5M in revenue, that's not a brand decision. That's an existential risk.

An extension of your existing brand isn't free — you still need to build velocity — but you're carrying your existing equity into every conversation. That is worth real money.

**4. Does it compete with or cannibalize your hero SKU?**

If your new product is going to pull velocity from your best performer, you have a problem. Retail buyers hate it. Category managers hate it. And it muddies your data story — now you have to explain why velocity per SKU went down even though total brand revenue went up.

If the new product is genuinely additive — different occasion, different consumer need, different shelf set — extension works. If it's substitutional, you need to be careful.

**5. What's the gross margin profile?**

This is the one founders almost never ask. If your new product is going to run at thirty-five percent gross margin while your core business is at fifty-two, you need to think hard about what a "Suja-branded" product at a meaningfully different margin does to your business mix over time. A lower-margin extension can quietly drag down your overall gross margin at exactly the moment you need it improving. And remember: gross margin determines destiny. CPG is a "penny profit" business, thus the pennies matter.

---

**What I actually did with Nika Water.**

Nika was a separate brand. And it was a separate brand intentionally.

The consumer was different. The retail channel had different priorities. The brand story — premium water tied to education and social impact — didn't layer onto a juice brand in any coherent way. We weren't extending anything. We were building something genuinely new.

That's the right call when it's the right call. The mistake founders make is treating every new idea as the same situation. They haven't asked the five questions. They've just fallen in love with a new product and reverse-engineered the rationale for the architecture.

---

**The practical rule I'd leave you with:**

Extend the brand aggressively — until the brand can't go there honestly. And if you're going to build a new brand, go in with full eyes open about what it actually costs.

Don't confuse "this product feels different to me" with "my consumer needs a new brand to understand this product." Those are very different statements. The first is founder intuition. The second is a consumer insight. Only one of them should drive the decision.

Build something worth extending before you build a second thing worth launching.
