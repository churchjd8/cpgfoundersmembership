When a founder told me she was close to closing Dollar General, I didn't say congratulations.

I said: "Walk me through the unit economics first."

She paused. She hadn't done them yet. She'd gotten a meeting with a buyer, the buyer seemed interested, and she assumed that meant it was a good idea.

That's how a lot of brands end up in trouble in the dollar channel.

Dollar General has more than 19,000 stores. Dollar Tree and Family Dollar combined? Another 17,000+. That's more retail doors than Walmart and Target combined. For a brand doing a few million in revenue, a dollar channel deal can feel like the big break... the one that finally puts you on the national stage.

Sometimes it is. More often, it's the beginning of a slow-motion margin collapse -- and a brand positioning problem that takes years to undo.

Here's what I've watched happen. And here's how to actually think about it.

## The Math Doesn't Lie

The dollar channel operates on a fundamentally different economic model than grocery, natural, or drug.

In a natural grocery store, your 12-ounce beverage might retail at $4.99. Your gross margin, if you've built it right, is 45-50%. You've got room to spend on trade, absorb the distributor margin, take a slotting hit, and still run a real business.

Now put that same product in Dollar General. Their price architecture for food and beverage runs at $1.25, $2, $3, or $4. If your product retails at $4.99 elsewhere and DG prices it at $3 or $3.50... your landed economics just got repriced against a ceiling that wasn't designed for you.

Run it through: your COGS haven't changed. Freight to their distribution centers hasn't changed -- if anything, it's more complex because Dollar General's DC compliance requirements are demanding. But now you're selling into a price point that compresses your gross margin by 15-20 points. A brand running 45% in natural grocery might be running 25-28% in the dollar channel, if they're lucky.

"Revenue without margin is ego." I say that a lot. But it's never more true than when founders chase dollar store door counts without doing the margin math first.

CPG is a penny profit business. The pennies matter in every channel -- but in the dollar channel, the pennies can disappear entirely.

## The Brand Equity Risk Nobody Talks About

Here's the part that makes founders uncomfortable.

Your Whole Foods buyer and your Dollar General buyer both pull the same Nielsen and SPINS data. Retailers -- especially premium ones -- track where else your product lives.

I've watched brands close a dollar channel deal and lose a Whole Foods reset within 18 months. Not because the product wasn't good. Because Whole Foods sees dollar store placement as a positioning signal. It tells them where you've decided your brand belongs.

This isn't hypothetical. At Suja, we made a very deliberate decision to stay out of dollar stores entirely during our growth phase. We were building a premium cold-pressed juice brand. $9.99 for 16 ounces at Whole Foods in 2012. Every retail decision was a statement about who we were and who we served. Dollar General would have brought volume... and would have undermined everything else we were building.

Compare that to a mature brand with broad conventional distribution that wants incremental reach. For them, the dollar channel can be a legitimate part of the portfolio strategy. But only if they've thought through the brand architecture first.

Not the same situation. Not the same decision.

## The Pack Size Strategy That Protects You

If you're going to play in the dollar channel, here's the move: differentiate by pack size and barcode.

I learned this from a senior Target buyer before Suja launched at Walmart. She told us not to use identical barcodes or pack configurations across retailers. If we did, Target would price-match Walmart's EDLP and we'd erode our own margin in the channel we'd spent years building.

So we launched a 10.5-ounce bottle at Walmart and a 12-ounce bottle at Target. Different barcode. Different price point. Different consumer perception.

The same principle applies in the dollar channel -- but you have to be more aggressive about it. Create a product specifically engineered for their price architecture. Different size, different flavor, different UPC. This isn't about hiding your brand. It's about protecting your pricing architecture everywhere else.

The brands that get into trouble do this: they take their flagship SKU, unchanged, and try to put it into Dollar General at a markdown. That's how you hand every premium retailer in your portfolio a reason to delist you.

## When the Dollar Channel Actually Makes Sense

Let me be direct about who this is right for.

**Brands in the growth or maturity stage.** If you're still proving concept at Whole Foods and Sprouts, the dollar channel is a distraction and a brand risk. It can close doors before you've opened them. Build your foundation at premium first, then decide.

**Brands with gross margin above 40%.** If you can absorb a 15-20 point compression and still land above 25%, the unit economics at least work. If you're already running thin, there's no scaling your way to profitability in a lower-priced channel. Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains.

**Brands where the demographic crossover is genuine.** Dollar store shoppers are predominantly value-driven, skew female, and are intensely focused on price-per-unit. If your product speaks to that customer -- and you can architect a version that meets their price expectations -- there's a real audience there. Dollar General is opening 800+ new stores a year. That's not noise. That's America's fastest-growing food retail format.

**Brands with operational bandwidth.** This is the one founders underestimate most. Dollar General and Dollar Tree have demanding vendor compliance requirements: EDI, on-time delivery benchmarks, pallet configuration standards, fill rate expectations. If you're running lean, the chargebacks alone will eat your margin before you sell a single unit.

## What to Do Before You Say Yes

If a dollar channel buyer comes calling -- or if you're thinking about making the call yourself -- here's the homework first.

**One: Run the P&L by account before the meeting.** Build out the retail price, the buyer's margin (figure 40-45%), freight to their DC, and your landed COGS. What does gross margin look like at that price point? If it's below 25%, walk away before you invest another minute.

**Two: Talk to your other key retailers before you sign.** Not to ask permission -- but to understand their posture. Some retailers won't care. Some will view dollar store placement as a dealbreaker and will tell you clearly that it affects your reset position. Know this before you commit.

**Three: Design a product specifically for the channel.** Don't take your hero SKU in at a markdown. Create a channel-specific SKU -- different size, different flavor, different UPC. Protect your pricing architecture everywhere else. This takes more work upfront, but it's the only way to play both markets simultaneously without brand cannibalization.

**Four: Get the vendor compliance guide before you shake hands.** Give it to your operations team. Read it like a contract -- because it is one. The penalty structure for non-compliance can be severe. I've seen brands get chargebacked into negative margin on their first few orders because they didn't understand the palletization or labeling requirements.

**Five: Start with a regional test, not national rollout.** The dollar channel will tell you quickly whether you have velocity there. If you do, expand. If you don't, exit before you're in 10,000 stores with a product that isn't moving. The Rule of Twos applies here too -- this will take twice as long and cost twice as much as you think to prove out.

## The Walmart Parallel

This is worth pausing on, because I've been in a version of this situation.

When Walmart wanted Suja to launch a plant-based smoothie as a Walmart exclusive, the production team hated the idea. The economics were unattractive. It was operationally inefficient. By itself, the deal looked bad.

But we agreed to do it -- a limited, controlled run at less-than-ideal economics -- in exchange for expanded distribution on our higher-performing core SKUs. The calculated trade paid off. Walmart became one of Suja's largest customers.

That's the right way to think about dollar channel deals too. Not "is this deal good on its own?" but "what does this unlock, and does the strategic trade make sense at this stage of our brand?"

If the dollar channel deal is pure volume at no strategic benefit... that's a different conversation. But if there's a real reason -- geographic reach, consumer access, a specific demographic you can't otherwise serve -- then the math and the mission can coexist. You just have to be honest about both.

## The Question That Cuts Through Everything

Before you pursue the dollar channel, ask yourself one thing: Am I doing this because it's strategically right for my brand at this stage, or because I'm excited about the door count?

Door count is seductive. I've been there. And I've watched founders chase numbers that looked great in a board presentation and created a mess in the actual business -- compressed margins, damaged retailer relationships, brand positioning that took years to rebuild.

The dollar channel can be a legitimate growth lever for the right brand at the right stage with the right product architecture. But it's never a shortcut to scale. And once you're in at the wrong time, it's hard to get out without a story for why you left.

Dream boldly. Plan soberly. That's as true for channel strategy as it is for anything else in CPG.
