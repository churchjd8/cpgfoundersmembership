One of the first things I tell a CPG founder who's ready to go national is this: get the order right.

Whole Foods first. Then Target. Then Costco. Then Kroger. Then Walmart.

Most founders want to flip that sequence. They want the biggest door counts as fast as they can get them. I understand it. I felt it too. But there's a logic to the order, and if you skip steps you'll regret it.

Kroger is 2,700+ stores. It's access to roughly half of American households. It's one of the most data-sophisticated retail environments you will ever sell into. And it's sitting right in the middle of that sequence for a reason.

---

**What Kroger Is (And Why It's Different)**

Most founders think of Kroger as a traditional grocery chain. It is... but calling it that is like calling the ocean "some water."

Kroger operates through regional banners: Kroger itself, King Soopers, Ralphs, Fred Meyer, Fry's, Harris Teeter, Dillons, City Market. The company has roughly 2,700 stores in 35 states. And through **84.51°** — their analytics and consumer research arm — they have one of the most sophisticated shopper data operations in the world.

That matters because when you walk into a buyer meeting at Kroger, they already know your numbers. They know your velocity in Whole Foods. They know your repeat purchase rate from your DTC channel. They've probably modeled what you'd do in their set before you've opened your deck.

Kroger isn't just a buyer. They're an analyst who already has an opinion.

Which means you better walk in with better data than they do.

---

**Why the Sequence Matters**

We didn't launch Suja into Kroger on day one. We didn't have the data or the story. We went to Whole Foods first because Whole Foods was the right first retailer for a premium cold-pressed juice — both for the consumer fit and because a Whole Foods win gave us velocity data that every subsequent buyer respected.

Target came next. That got us national ACV at a price-sensitive mainstream retailer, and suddenly our SPINS story got much more compelling. Costco was about volume proof and the ability to manage large-scale production... that rotation model they run is a gift if you're ready for it.

By the time we sat down with Kroger, we had the data. We had the category story. We had proof that this product performed at Whole Foods, at Target, and at Costco. Kroger's buyers are sophisticated enough to see the arc you're drawing for them.

Walk in too early and you're asking them to take a risk on a brand that hasn't proven itself in a comparable environment. Kroger doesn't have the same appetite for category risk that Whole Foods does. They're not the place to find you. They're the place to scale you.

---

**What Kroger Buyers Actually Want**

Kroger has one of the most buyer-driven cultures in traditional grocery. These are smart, data-fluent people who are accountable for category performance, not just for trying new products. So what moves them?

**Velocity from comparable accounts.** If you've been in Whole Foods for eighteen months and your velocity is in the 70th percentile or above, that story travels. If you've been in Target and your turns per store are above the category median, say so specifically. Data that shows you can win in a traditional grocery environment before Kroger gives you shelf space is the most valuable thing in your deck.

**A category growth argument.** Not just "our brand is growing." The case that your brand grows the entire category, not just displaces a competitor. Kroger's buyers are measured on category health. If you can show them data that emerging brands like yours expanded category dollar sales in markets where they had meaningful distribution, you're speaking their language.

**Clean unit economics.** Kroger understands margin. They run aggressive private label programs across their portfolio (Simple Truth is a $3+ billion brand). They know what your COGS look like before you tell them. If your pricing doesn't work at their shelf price expectations without destroying your margin, that's a conversation you need to have internally before the meeting, not during it.

**Partnership intent.** Nicky, our head of sales at Suja, had a simple operating principle with every major account: if something wasn't working, we raised our hand first. We didn't wait for the buyer to come to us. That built trust in a way that no pitch deck ever could.

Kroger's buyers have seen a thousand founders come and go. The ones they invest relationship time in are the ones who act like partners, not just suppliers.

---

**The 84.51° Reality**

Here's something a lot of founders don't fully appreciate going into Kroger: 84.51° is not just an analytics vendor. It's a first-party data operation at Kroger's core.

Kroger has detailed loyalty purchase history on nearly half of American households. They know when customers switch brands, when they try new products, when they come back. And they sell access to that data back to brands through Kroger Precision Marketing.

This is worth understanding for two reasons.

First: when you're in front of a Kroger buyer, they may reference shopper panel data from your existing retailers that you haven't seen. Don't be caught flat-footed. Know your repeat rate, know your household penetration, know where you're winning. If you haven't subscribed to syndicated data yet, make sure you have at least some read on consumer behavior before you walk in.

Second: once you're in Kroger, the 84.51° advertising tools are worth taking seriously. Targeted digital offers to your exact shopper profile, in a loyalty program that actually changes behavior... this isn't billboard advertising. It's precision. We used targeted promotions at Kroger to drive trial in new markets without broad, expensive media. It worked.

---

**The Regional Division Reality**

One thing that trips up founders when they're first in with Kroger: it's not one account. It's a collection of regional divisions that each have some autonomy.

You'll often get a national approval and then discover that regional execution is inconsistent. The Central division is different from the Mid-Atlantic. Fred Meyer is different from Harris Teeter. The planograms don't all move at the same time.

This is not a flaw. It's the system. Plan for it. Build a field team presence or a broker relationship that covers your top Kroger regions. Know which divisions drive the most volume in your category. Don't wait for perfect national rollout — go find the two or three divisions where you can prove velocity first, then use that to push for broader commitment.

Your Kroger account manager, if you have one, can help you understand the division landscape. If you don't have one yet, your broker should.

---

**The Human Side**

None of this works without relationships. We used to host home dinners for our retail partners, and that wasn't just a quirky Suja thing — it was intentional. Jody, one of the best account managers I've ever worked with, navigated some genuinely difficult internal seasons with Kroger and Publix not because she had the best pitch deck, but because she had earned real trust with the people on the other side of the table.

Retail strategy is not just about doors. It is about order. And order is built on relationships.

That's true in every channel. It's true at Kroger too.

When things go sideways... and at some point in every major account, something will go sideways... the brands that survive it are the ones where the buyer knows the people behind them. Where there's enough goodwill in the account to have a hard conversation and find a solution together.

You're not trying to sell a buyer on your product. You're trying to build a partnership that outlasts the next line review.
