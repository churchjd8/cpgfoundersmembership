5:00 p.m. on July 3rd, 2018.

Scott Uzzell from Coca-Cola VEB called. Coke was not moving forward with the acquisition. I hung up the phone and walked downstairs to where my family was gathered, prepping for the Fourth of July. And I wept.

We were doing over $300 million in annual revenue. We had won national distribution, built an iconic brand, raised $110 million in capital across eleven rounds... and we had less than $100,000 in the bank.

If you haven't lived that specific hell, let me explain how it happens. Because it doesn't just happen to bad operators. It happens to great ones. It happened to us at Suja. And it's happening right now to founders you'd be surprised by.

The culprit isn't ambition. It isn't strategy. It's a three-variable equation almost nobody teaches you in CPG.

It's the cash conversion cycle.

---

## The Math Nobody Teaches You

The cash conversion cycle (CCC) has three parts. Know these cold.

**DIO — Days Inventory Outstanding.** How long does your product sit in a warehouse, a 3PL, or a retailer's DC before it gets sold? For most CPG brands, this is 30 to 90 days. If you're doing smaller runs with a high-velocity hero SKU, you're on the low end. If you have shelf-life constraints, production MOQs that force large batch runs, or inconsistent retail velocity... you're on the high end. At Suja, our cold-pressed juice required HPP (high pressure processing), specialized equipment, large minimum runs. DIO was not our friend.

**DSO — Days Sales Outstanding.** Once you invoice a retailer, how long until you actually see the cash? Most major retailers operate on net-60 terms. Some are net-90. UNFI and KeHE are typically 60 to 75 days. And this doesn't account for deductions they'll take after the fact — which are their own category of cash drag and have their own post. The point is: you shipped product, you invoiced, and you are waiting two to three months to collect.

**DPO — Days Payable Outstanding.** How long does your co-manufacturer, your ingredient suppliers, your packaging vendors give you before they want to be paid? If you're early-stage, it's probably net-30 and nonnegotiable. As you scale, you can push this out. The bigger players get to 60 or even 90 days. But most founders — when cash pressure is highest — are stuck at 30 days when they need 60.

The formula: **CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO**

Walk through the math. If DIO is 60 days, DSO is 60 days, and DPO is 30 days, your CCC is **90 days**. Every single dollar you spend on production today takes 90 days to come back to you. You are essentially lending your business 90 days of interest-free financing to your supply chain, your co-man, and your retailers.

---

## What 90 Days Means When You're Growing Fast

Here's where it gets brutal.

If your CCC is 90 days and you're growing at 50%, your cash need is growing at 50% of whatever your annual cost base is — multiplied by that 90-day lag. Every new door you open, every new PO you fill, every new retailer you bring on requires you to fund 90 days of production before you see a dime back.

At Suja, we were growing at a compound annual rate close to 200% in the early years. The growth rate sounds like a victory lap. And in many ways it was. But behind the headline, the cash conversion cycle was quietly compounding against us every quarter.

We had large production runs. We were scaling into major retailers with 60-plus-day payment terms. Our ingredient and packaging suppliers had real leverage over us in the early years — net-30 was the deal.

Rough math from our peak scaling years: **DIO around 45 days + DSO around 65 days − DPO around 30 days = 80 days.**

For every new dollar of annual revenue we were adding, we needed to fund about 22 cents of additional working capital before we'd see the cash back. That doesn't sound terrible until you realize we were adding $30 or $40 million in revenue in a single year. That's $7 to $9 million of working capital need on top of everything else.

We were raising capital to fund the gap between our revenue and our cash. That is a very different problem than raising capital to invest in growth. And investors understand the difference — even when you don't have the language for it.

---

## The Three Levers That Actually Move This Number

Most founders attack this problem the wrong way: they raise more money. Sometimes that's the right answer. But before you dilute another 15%, check whether you've pulled these three levers.

**1. Push your DPO out.** This is the fastest win and often the most underused. As your volume grows, renegotiate with your co-man and key ingredient suppliers. Get to net-45. Ideally net-60. Every 15 days you add to your DPO is 15 days of cash you retain on your balance sheet. At $10 million in annual COGS, moving from net-30 to net-45 is roughly $410,000 of cash you're not borrowing from somewhere else. That's real. That's equity you're not giving up.

**2. Pull your DSO in.** Some retailers will offer early payment discounts — typically 1 to 2% if you request payment in 10 to 15 days instead of 60. Run the math. At 1.5%, if your cost of capital is higher than that annualized (and for most early-stage brands it is), it's worth taking the discount. Some distributors will also factor your receivables for a fee — meaning you get paid in 2 days instead of 65. It costs something. It's often worth it.

**3. Attack your DIO.** This one is harder but it's worth fighting for. Smaller, more frequent production runs cost more per unit — but they free up cash. If your velocity is predictable and your co-man relationship is solid, see if you can shift from one large quarterly run to smaller monthly runs. The goal isn't perfect efficiency at the production line. CPG is a "Penny Profit" business — the pennies matter — but a penny saved on COGS doesn't matter if you've run out of cash before you can ship the product.

---

## The Number That Actually Runs Your Business

"Gross margin determines destiny." I've said that hundreds of times and I believe it.

But the cash conversion cycle is what determines whether you make it long enough for gross margin to matter. The two are not in conflict. They operate on different time horizons. Gross margin is your business model. The cash conversion cycle is your survival model.

Here's what I tell every founder who asks me about working capital: know your CCC. Write the number down. Update it every quarter. Put it in your weekly operating dashboard right next to velocity and gross margin. Because the CCC doesn't just describe your cash position today. It predicts it three months from now.

At Suja, with the benefit of hindsight, I know we didn't pay close enough attention to it in the early scaling years. We were focused on distribution, velocity, the next round, the next hire. The cash conversion cycle was sitting in the background, quietly compounding its way to July 3rd, 2018.

By that call, we had $40 million in secured debt due in October, gross margins still working their way toward where they needed to be, and less than $100,000 in the bank.

That is what happens when you let the CCC run unchecked while growing a brand at 200% per year.

"Hope is not a strategy." And hope is definitely not a cash flow model.

Know your number. It's DIO + DSO − DPO. Run it right now, today, before your next raise. Because the problem you're solving in the fundraise might not be what you think it is.

The business you save may be your own.
