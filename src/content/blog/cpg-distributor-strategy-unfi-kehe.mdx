There's a call that every emerging CPG founder gets at some point. Maybe you've already had it. Your broker calls, slightly breathless. "I've got good news. UNFI is going to pick us up."

You hang up. Text your co-founder. Maybe you crack a beer. You feel like you just won something.

Six months later, you're staring at a deduction report that looks like it was designed to confuse you, your fill rate slipped below 90%, and three Whole Foods regions are showing zero inventory on your top SKU. The dream and the reality are two very different things.

At Suja, we worked with both KeHE and UNFI. And I'll tell you honestly: we made every mistake in the book before we figured out how to work these relationships well. The good news is, once you understand how these distributors actually operate, the whole channel opens up. The bad news is, nobody tells you the rules.

Let me try.

## The Basics Most Founders Get Wrong

First, let's establish something. A distributor is not a broker. Not even close.

Your broker represents your brand in front of buyers. They're your sales arm. They carry your story into the room and fight for your shelf space. Presence Marketing, led by Bill Weiland, was essential to Suja's early Whole Foods launch. Bill was extraordinarily selective about the brands he'd take on. That selectivity was the signal.

A distributor is a warehouse operation. UNFI (United Natural Foods, Inc.) and KeHE are the two dominant national distributors in the natural and organic channel. They physically receive your product from your co-manufacturer, warehouse it in their regional DCs, and truck it to retailers on their route. They are the literal supply chain link between your production floor and the shelf.

Here's what founders miss. Getting picked up by UNFI is not the same thing as getting distributed by UNFI. Getting picked up means they'll carry your SKU in their system. Actually getting distributed means retailers are ordering it, UNFI is shipping it, and the product is turning. The space between those two things is where brands die quietly.

Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains. They are completely different scoreboards.

## How the Pickup Process Actually Works

UNFI and KeHE don't typically pick up brands because the brand is compelling. They pick up brands because a retailer is requesting that product. The pull has to come first.

What this means practically: before you even approach a national distributor, you need a retail account that's asking for you. A regional natural food buyer at a Whole Foods division, a Sprouts buyer, a Fresh Market buyer. Once a retailer says "we want that product in our system," the distributor follows. Without that demand signal, you're pushing rope.

This is exactly why distribution sequencing matters so much. At Suja, we built our Whole Foods presence first. Before conventional grocery, before mass, before anything else. That wasn't just about brand positioning or staying premium. It was also about creating the pull that made UNFI and KeHE want to work with us. The retailer relationship creates the distributor relationship, not the other way around.

Your broker facilitates the distributor introduction. If you have a strong natural channel broker, they almost certainly have established relationships with KeHE and UNFI category managers built over many years. That relationship matters enormously. Don't try to go direct to a national distributor without a broker in your corner first. You'll waste months.

## Understanding Distributor Economics

Okay. You got picked up. Congratulations. Now let's talk about what that actually costs you.

KeHE and UNFI both operate on margin. They're buying product from you at invoice and reselling it to retailers at a markup. Depending on category and program, that markup typically runs 18 to 28 percent off your invoice price. Add in freight, handling, and any promotional allowances they push through, and your dead net... what you actually receive per case after everything is settled... can be 30 to 35 percent below your target retail price.

If you haven't modeled this correctly in your financial plan, your gross margin just blew up. Silently. Because the deductions don't always show up the same month you shipped.

Here's the calculation I walk founders through:

Start with the retail shelf price. Work backward. Retailer margin in natural: typically 35 to 45 percent. Distributor margin: 18 to 28 percent. Freight and handling: 3 to 6 percent. Promotional allowances and billbacks: 2 to 4 percent. What's left after all of that is your dead net. That's what you have to cover your COGS, fund your operations, and build a business.

CPG is a "Penny Profit" business. The pennies matter at every single step. If you're not modeling distributor costs accurately from day one, you'll grow your way into a margin crisis that's very hard to escape.

A few specific line items to understand before you sign anything:

**Spoils allowance.** KeHE and UNFI typically charge 1 to 2 percent automatically to cover damaged or unsaleable product. It's in the contract. It comes off every invoice.

**Freight.** You usually own the freight from your co-man to the distributor's warehouse (inbound). They charge for outbound to retailers. Get both numbers before you build your model.

**Billbacks and deductions.** Some promotional programs push funding through the distributor as billbacks, which show up as deductions on your invoice weeks or even months after the promotion ran. If you're not tracking these in real time against your trade spend commitments, they will blindside you. Every time.

Gross margin determines destiny. I mean that literally. And your distributor relationship sits right in the middle of that equation.

## Fill Rate Is Non-Negotiable

This is the one that trips up young brands the most, and it's the one that will get you cut from distribution faster than almost anything else.

UNFI and KeHE require fill rates of 95 percent or higher on a sustained basis. That means when they submit a purchase order, 95 of every 100 cases need to ship on time and in full. Miss that benchmark consistently, and they'll stop routing orders to your SKUs. The shelf goes dark. The retailer complains to your broker. Your broker calls you. That call doesn't start with good news.

Fill rate is a supply chain problem, which means it's really a co-man relationship problem, an inventory planning problem, a demand forecasting problem, and a cash flow problem all rolled into one. If any of those links break, your fill rate suffers. And when your fill rate suffers, your distribution suffers.

At Suja, we had stretches when our supply chain was under real stress. Growing from $18 million to $44 million in revenue in a single year (2013 to 2014) put enormous strain on everything. The unglamorous work of fixing it was weekly cash projections, renegotiated payment terms with suppliers, tighter production schedules. Not exciting. But non-negotiable. Because without fill rate, distribution collapses, and everything you've built goes with it.

Keep fill rate on your operating dashboard. Track it by SKU, by distributor, by week. Do not wait for the distributor to flag a problem. By the time they flag it, you've already lost.

## KeHE vs. UNFI

Both are national. Both serve the natural and organic channel. Here are the meaningful differences.

UNFI is larger, with broader penetration in conventional grocery alongside natural. If your distribution strategy runs through Whole Foods, Sprouts, or a national conventional chain with natural sets, UNFI is likely your primary route. They're also the primary UNFI distributor in most markets for Whole Foods specifically.

KeHE has particular depth in natural, specialty, and independent retail. They tend to have strong relationships with regional chains and independent natural food stores. Some founders find the relationship more manageable at the early stage because the organization is somewhat less complex.

Here's what most people don't tell you. You'll probably end up working with both. Many retailers have preferred distributor agreements with one or the other, or split the store by department or region. Your broker will guide you on which distributor handles which retailer relationship. Don't try to manage this yourself without a broker who knows the landscape. Follow their lead.

## The Relationship Still Matters

Don't let all the economics and logistics talk obscure something fundamental. CPG distribution follows relationships.

Your distributor rep is not just an account number. That's a human being managing an enormous book of business, trying to hit their numbers, and hoping not to get calls from angry retailers about out-of-stock product. Show up for that person. Be proactive when something is going wrong with your product or your supply chain. Do not make them find out from a retailer. Call first. Have a plan. Come with solutions.

We had moments at Suja when our velocity was soft in a region, and I got in front of the distributor team with a corrective plan before they came to us. That builds trust. Trust that kept us in the rotation when other brands got cut. Buyers and distributors both have limited shelf space and limited bandwidth. Be the easy brand to work with, not the one they're managing.

People do business with founders they trust. Trust accelerates distribution. That's not a platitude. It's operational reality.

## What to Track Every Month

If you're working with KeHE or UNFI right now, here's your monthly discipline check:

**Fill rate by SKU.** Are you hitting 95 percent or higher? If not, trace it back to root cause immediately. Co-man capacity, ingredient availability, lead times, whatever it is, own it.

**Deduction reconciliation.** Are the billbacks and deductions hitting your invoices matching what you agreed to in your trade programs? Discrepancies compound quickly and quietly.

**Distributor inventory aging.** What's sitting in their warehouse, and for how long? Old inventory creates spoilage risk and distributor friction. Know your turns at the DC level, not just at retail.

**Retailer out-of-stocks.** Pull this from your syndicated data or from retailer portals. Out-of-stocks at retail trace back to distributor inventory gaps more often than you'd think.

**Dead net by account.** Know what you're actually netting by retailer after all deductions and allowances. If an account's economics don't pencil, you need to know before you've committed a year of production capacity to them.

Hope is not a strategy. And "I think we're on shelf" is not a distribution strategy. Know your numbers.

---

One of the biggest revelations at Suja was understanding that gaining distribution was the beginning of the work, not the end. The real battle is what you do after the pickup. Maintain your fill rate. Understand your economics at every step. Build genuine relationships with the people inside these organizations. And earn your shelf space every single month.

You can have wide distribution and empty shelves. Or you can have targeted distribution where product turns fast, retailers reorder, and the distributor starts routing bigger POs without being asked. That second scenario is how brands scale. Everything else is just logistics.
