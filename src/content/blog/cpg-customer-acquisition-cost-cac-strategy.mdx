We ran billboards in Manhattan when we had four Whole Foods stores in the entire city.

Four stores. Manhattan. Billboards.

I'm not proud of it. Well... part of me is. It was bold. It showed confidence. It got us press. But here's what it also did: it created enormous awareness with almost nowhere to convert it. People saw those ads, got excited, went to find Suja — and couldn't. They moved on. The awareness evaporated.

That experience is burned into my brain as the clearest example I can give you of misunderstanding customer acquisition cost. We were spending money to acquire curiosity, not customers.

There's a difference. A big one.

---

## What CAC Actually Means in CPG

In tech, customer acquisition cost (CAC) is clean. You run an ad. Someone clicks. They sign up. You divide your ad spend by your new customers and there's your number.

CPG is messier. A lot messier.

Your "customer acquisition" happens across a dozen touchpoints you don't fully control: a demo in a Whole Foods, a shout-out from an influencer, an in-store feature, a coupon drop, word of mouth from a neighbor, a trade promotion your retailer runs, a billboard... apparently... in Manhattan.

So when I talk about CAC in CPG, I'm using a broader definition. Every dollar you spend to get a consumer to pick up your product for the first time — that's your acquisition cost. Demos. Sampling events. Slotting fees. TPRs (temporary price reductions). Digital ads. Influencers. Trade show costs. PR.

All of it.

When you add it up, most founders I work with are shocked. Not because the number is high — they expected it to be high. They're shocked because they've never actually added it up.

"Hope is not a strategy." And "we'll figure out the math later" isn't either.

---

## The Equation Nobody Taught You

Here's the thing about CAC that separates the founders who make it from the ones who don't.

It's not about minimizing it. It's about understanding the ratio.

**CAC vs. Repeat Rate.** That's the real equation.

If you spend $8 to get a consumer to try your product and they never buy again — you just paid $8 for a one-time transaction. In a business where your gross margin is 40%, and your product sells for $6, you've made $2.40 on that sale and spent $8 to get it. That's not a business. That's a charity.

But if you spend $8 to acquire a consumer who buys your product 12 times over the next year? Now that $8 looks very different. Your lifetime value on that customer might be $30 in gross margin. A 3-to-1 ratio. That's a business.

The math changes completely based on one variable: repeat rate.

And this is where I see founders make the most dangerous mistake in marketing. They measure trial. They celebrate it. They promote it. They optimize for it. And they completely miss the question that actually matters.

**Are those people coming back?**

---

## The Billboards Had Good Reach. The Product Had to Do the Rest.

Here's what I actually learned from the Manhattan billboard era.

We got trial. Lots of it. And our repeat rate was real — consumers genuinely loved the product. Cold-pressed juice was new, it tasted unlike anything they'd had, and the "health without the punishment" experience was visceral. People came back.

But... we created it in the wrong order. We drove awareness before we had distribution to catch it. The acquisition math on those billboards was terrible because we couldn't fulfill the demand we created.

You can market your way into trial. You cannot market your way into loyalty. And you definitely cannot market your way into trial when people can't find your product.

That sequence matters. Build distribution to a threshold first. Then drive awareness. Then optimize for trial. Then obsess over repeat.

Most founders do it in the wrong order, or worse, try to do all four at once and do none well.

## The Benchmarks That Actually Matter

Let me give you some real numbers to work with.

**Repeat rate targets:**

At Suja, we were targeting a 40 to 50 percent repeat rate in our core markets and with our core consumers. That was the internal benchmark — what we needed to see to believe we had real proof of concept.

Under 20 percent repeat rate? That's a warning sign. It means people are trying you and moving on. You're buying customers, not building a brand. The marketing budget is papering over a product-market fit problem, or a positioning problem, or a pricing problem. But it's a problem.

In DTC specifically, I'd be very nervous with a repeat rate under 10 percent. If less than 1 in 10 first-time customers orders again, you should be surveying those non-returners with urgency. Not "eventually." Now.

**The CAC:LTV ratio:**

For a CPG brand in year one to three, a 2-to-1 ratio (your lifetime customer value is at least 2x your acquisition cost) is the minimum bar. You want to be pushing toward 3-to-1 or better as you scale. 

Below 2-to-1 and you're subsidizing your own growth. Every new customer you "acquire" is a money-losing transaction, and scaling that is just losing money faster.

**Demo costs, specifically:**

In-store demos and sampling events are the most expensive and most effective form of CPG acquisition. Effective because they convert at extraordinary rates — someone who tastes your product in a store and loves it is dramatically more likely to buy than someone who saw your Instagram ad.

Expensive because... they're expensive. A full-day demo with a skilled demo rep, the product cost, the setup, the retail program fees — you can easily spend $500 to $1,500 per event. If you sell 40 units at $6 each in a day, you covered your product cost but not your acquisition cost. The ROI calculation has to account for those repeat purchases that follow.

This is why demo strategy and velocity monitoring have to be connected. You demo. You watch velocity in that store over the following 6 to 8 weeks. If velocity lifts and holds, the demo was worth it. If it spikes and drops back, you bought a day of trial and nothing more.

## Where Most Marketing Budgets Go Wrong

I've seen this pattern dozens of times. A founder raises a round, allocates a marketing budget, and immediately starts spending on awareness — because awareness feels like momentum.

Billboards. Sponsorships. A podcast ad. Some influencer deals. A brand activation.

It all looks great in the board presentation. Impressions are up. Website traffic is up. Social following is growing.

And then the line review comes around, and the retail buyer shows you the velocity data. And the story told by your marketing materials and the story told by the shelf are two completely different stories.

"The truth of the business is at the shelf." That's something Jody Cnossen said to me years ago and I've never forgotten it. You can build all the brand equity you want above the shelf. But the shelf tells the truth.

Here's the framework I use with founders. Before you spend anything on brand marketing — awareness, content, brand activations — answer three questions:

1. Do you have enough distribution that a consumer who sees your marketing has a reasonable chance of finding your product?
2. Do you have the velocity data to tell you whether your trial converts to repeat?
3. Is your repeat rate above 30 percent (retail) or 15 percent (DTC)?

If the answer to any of those is no, your marketing dollars are better spent on trade programming and sampling. Get the fundamentals right first. Build the distribution base. Prove the repeat. Then pour fuel on it.

Capital amplifies whatever foundation you've built. Spend on awareness before you have the foundation and you're amplifying nothing.

## The Gross Margin Connection

Here's the thing nobody tells you: your acceptable CAC is entirely determined by your gross margin.

If your gross margin is 28 percent and your product retails for $5, your gross profit per unit is $1.40. To make the acquisition economics work at a 3-to-1 CAC:LTV ratio over 6 purchases, you need a CAC under $2.80. That is almost impossible to achieve with any meaningful marketing program.

If your gross margin is 50 percent on that same $5 product, your gross profit per unit is $2.50. Now you can afford a CAC of $5 over 6 purchases and still hit 3-to-1. The marketing math actually works.

This is why I say, over and over, that gross margin determines destiny. Not just for investors. Not just for your income statement. For your ability to market at all.

"Revenue without margin is ego." And low-margin brands can't build acquisition economics that support a real marketing program. They're caught in a trap — they need marketing to grow, but their margin structure can't support the cost of acquiring customers. So they either burn through capital or they don't grow.

Get to 40 percent gross margins by end of year two. 50 percent by years three or four. Not just because investors want to see it. Because your own marketing math depends on it.

## One More Thing About "Acquiring" Customers

I want to reframe how you think about this, because I think the tech-world framing of "customer acquisition" can be subtly misleading for CPG founders.

In tech, you acquire a customer and then you own the relationship. Their data, their email, their subscription. You can re-market to them directly.

In CPG retail, you don't own the customer relationship. The retailer does. You don't know who bought your product last Tuesday. You can't email them. You can't retarget them. You have almost no direct path back to them except through the shelf.

This is why repeat rate is such a critical measurement. Because if a consumer comes back to buy your product again, it's not because of your marketing. It's because your product delivered on its promise. The product earned the repeat. Your acquisition cost earned the trial. But only the product earns loyalty.

You can market your way into trial. You cannot market your way into loyalty.

That's not just a saying. It's the foundational economics of the CPG business. Spend on acquisition all you want. But if the product isn't earning the repeat... you're on a treadmill. Running hard to stay in place.

## What to Actually Measure

Here's the practical minimum you need to track if you want to run an honest acquisition program:

**In retail:** Track velocity (units per store per week) by time period. If you did a promo or demo, compare the 8-week period before vs. the 8 weeks after. Did velocity hold, lift, or drop back? That's your conversion rate on the acquisition spend.

**In DTC:** Track cohort retention by month. What percentage of month-1 purchasers came back in month 2? Month 3? If you're seeing a 10-15 percent monthly repeat rate in a subscription or repeat-purchase model, that's where you start building. Under 10 percent, go talk to your customers before spending another dollar on ads.

**Across everything:** Run the acquisition math quarterly. Add up everything you spent on marketing and sampling and trade programming. Divide by new households. That's your blended CAC. Compare it to your average gross profit per household over 12 months. If the ratio is under 2-to-1, the model is broken. Fix the model before you spend more.

The founders who build lasting businesses are the ones who are honest with this math, especially early. It's uncomfortable. It often reveals that you're not as healthy as the top-line growth implies.

But better to face it early — when you can fix it — than to raise another round, scale the model, and amplify a problem into a crisis.

Dream boldly. Plan soberly. Measure honestly.
