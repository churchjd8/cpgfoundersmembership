A few years back I was watching football with my oldest son Josh on a Sunday afternoon.

During a commercial, he asked me how many fundraising rounds I'd led in my career.

I told him I needed a few minutes to think about it.

Forty-five minutes later, I had my answer. Forty-four rounds. Two hundred and twelve million dollars raised. Friends and family. Angels. Private equity. Venture capital. And eventually, $90 million from the Coca-Cola Company.

But here's the thing I told Josh when I finished counting: the money that mattered most wasn't the biggest check. It was the first checks. The angel money. The people who wrote a $25,000 or $50,000 check before Suja was anything -- when we were still delivering green juice in a van around San Diego and trying to figure out shelf life.

Most founders treat their angel round like a temporary inconvenience. A bridge to the "real" money. Get the check, cash it, move on.

That's a mistake.

## Angels Are Founding Partners, Not Temporary Investors

At Suja alone, I led eleven fundraising rounds across seven years. Several years we did two rounds. The angels who came in early -- before we had proven much of anything -- they weren't just writing checks. They were making a bet on us as people.

And when you're raising your first $500,000, you're not raising on your business. You don't have enough business to raise on yet. You're raising on your credibility, your track record, your intensity, and your ability to make someone believe what you believe.

So the question isn't just "who will write me a check?" The question is "who do I want in this boat with me for the next five to seven years?"

Choose wrong, and you'll spend more time managing your investors than managing your business. Hope is not a strategy -- and neither is taking money from whoever says yes first.

## The Strategic Angel

There's a category of angel investor most founders overlook: the operator-angel. People who have worked in your industry -- former retail buyers, distributors, brand builders, natural foods executives.

When we launched Suja at Whole Foods in September 2012, I had relationships in the natural foods space from my time with Nika Water. Those connections opened doors that money alone can't buy. A check from a Whole Foods regional buyer in your angel round isn't just capital. It's a phone call that gets returned. It's credibility with the next buyer in the room.

Think about who you want around your cap table from a strategic standpoint:

- Someone who has been a retail buyer and knows how category reviews work
- A former distributor or broker who understands how product moves through channels
- An entrepreneur who's built and sold a brand in an adjacent space
- A family with deep grocery or natural channel relationships

The rule I've used for thirty years: every angel in your round should be able to write you a check AND make at least one introduction that could change your trajectory. If they can only do one of those things, think twice before taking their money.

Not hype. Not a big LinkedIn following. Real, specific, industry-relevant access.

## Structure Matters. A Lot.

Here's something most first-time CPG founders don't understand about angel rounds until it's too late: how you structure your early rounds determines what your angels actually receive when you win.

We ran into this in 2015 when Coca-Cola invested in Suja at a $300 million valuation -- a 4.7x multiple on trailing twelve-month net sales. Most founders would call that a win and move on.

But here's what almost happened: a private equity investor who had been in the deal for less than twelve months was positioned to capture the majority of Coke's $90 million investment due to preference provisions. Meanwhile, the angels who had believed in us for three years -- who helped us survive a $1 million spoilage crisis, who talked us up at industry events, who forwarded our pitch to their networks -- they were going to walk away with almost nothing.

That wasn't right. I wasn't willing to let that happen.

We pressed pause. Found Goldman Sachs, who came in with $60 million alongside Coke. That restructuring allowed our early angels to sell approximately 40% of their holdings at an average 3.4x return while retaining 60% of their equity. When we eventually sold Suja, those angels got a second bite. Total return for most of them: roughly 10x.

The lesson isn't that I'm a great negotiator. The lesson is that structure matters more than anyone tells you -- and you have to actively protect the people who took the earliest risk with you. If you don't fight for them, no one will.

Work with a founder-friendly attorney from day one. Understand liquidation preferences, anti-dilution provisions, and participation rights. Ask hard questions about what happens when a larger investor comes in at a higher preference stack. Most first-time founders never ask these questions. That's on you.

## The Anatomy of a Good Angel Round

When I coach founders today, I tell them to think of their first angel round in three categories.

**The Believers.** People who know you personally and believe in you as a founder. Friends, former colleagues, family with capital. Check sizes typically run $10,000 to $50,000. These folks are in because they believe in you as a person. That's the foundation.

**The Strategics.** Former buyers, distributors, industry executives, brand veterans. They write similar-sized checks but bring a network. They get phone calls returned in rooms you can't get into yet. Check sizes: $25,000 to $100,000. These are the relationships that make introductions, not just investments.

**The Lead Angel.** One investor who writes a larger check ($100,000 to $250,000), helps set terms, and signals to others that this is worth backing. Finding a lead makes the rest of the round significantly easier to close. Other angels follow conviction.

Don't try to raise $500,000 from fifty people at $10,000 apiece. That's a nightmare to manage and signals to future institutional investors that you couldn't get anyone to take meaningful risk. Better to have ten to fifteen carefully chosen angels who are genuinely engaged.

Total round size for a pre-revenue or early-revenue CPG brand: $250,000 to $750,000. Enough to prove the concept, not so much you've over-diluted before you've shown the business works.

## After the Check Clears

One thing I've noticed in the CPG world: founders raise from angels and then go quiet for twelve months. Then when they need more money, they reappear. That's backwards.

Your angels should know what's happening. Not daily updates, not a group text every time something goes wrong. A quarterly email. One page. Three bullets: what's going right, what's hard, what you need from them.

You'd be amazed what that quarterly update unlocks. An angel with distribution experience sees your note about friction with UNFI and makes one phone call. An angel with a grocery retail background reads about your upcoming category review and connects you to exactly the right person. An angel who runs a law firm catches a contract issue before it costs you six figures.

That's what you were really paying for when they invested. The check was just the entry ticket.

CPG is a "Penny Profit" business -- the pennies matter. And in the early days, when you're short on cash and long on hustle, the right angel can save your company with one well-timed introduction. One phone call at the right moment from the right person can open a door that keeps you alive another twelve months.

The angels who have stayed closest to me over thirty years aren't necessarily the ones who made the most money (though I'm proud of those outcomes). They're the ones who felt genuinely along for the journey. Real information. Real updates. Real access.

Fundraising is not proof that you're winning. It's table stakes to stay in the game. But when you build an angel network the right way -- strategically, structurally, relationally -- those early believers become something more than investors. They become advocates. They become references. They become the people who tell the story of what you built long after the exit closes.

That's the real return.
