It was the morning of July 4th, 2018.

Blue skies. Eighty-two degrees. Fireworks the night before. The kind of morning that's supposed to feel good.

I woke up sick to my stomach.

The day before, Scott Uzzell -- President of Coca-Cola's Venture & Emerging Brands -- had called to say Coke wouldn't be moving forward with the full acquisition of Suja. I'd hung up the phone, walked downstairs, and completely lost my composure in front of my boys.

We had grown Suja from zero to $100 million in revenue in six years. Faster than KIND. Faster than ZICO, Honest Tea, Annie's -- at comparable stages in their history. We had raised $115 million. We had 350 employees and product in tens of thousands of retail locations.

And we were burning more than $10 million a year.

Gross margins were below 32 percent. We had $40 million in secured debt coming due in October. At our worst moments, we were operating with less than $100,000 in the bank.

Here's what I didn't understand clearly enough early on: **more capital is not a substitute for better margins.** We funded our ambition with outside money because our product didn't generate enough gross profit to fund it ourselves. And eventually, investors asked harder questions than the ones we were prepared to answer.

---

There's a version of the CPG story that every founder knows. You build something great, you raise money to grow it, you raise more money to grow faster, and eventually someone buys you at a multiple that makes everyone rich.

That version is real. I've lived it -- five home runs, three strikeouts, $212 million raised across 44 rounds, roughly $700 million returned to investors. But the hidden variable in every version of that story is margin. The home runs all had one thing in common. The strikeouts had one thing in common too.

"Revenue is exciting, but gross margin is transformational."

That's not just a quote I use in talks. It's the sentence that reorganizes how I see every business I evaluate. Because what gross margin actually determines is how much outside capital you need to grow. The math is simple, but most founders don't work through it until they're already in trouble.

---

**The capital trap, explained simply**

Say your product costs you $4 to make and you sell it at $8. That's a 50 percent gross margin. For every $100 in revenue, you have $50 to pay for sales, marketing, operations, and overhead before you get to profit. You're generating real fuel.

Now say your product costs $6 to make and you sell it at $8. That's 25 percent. For every $100 in revenue, you have $25 to work with. You need roughly twice as much capital to get to the same operational capacity.

That gap -- 50 percent vs. 25 percent -- isn't a margin problem. It's a fundraising problem. It's a dilution problem. It's an investor patience problem. It's the problem that follows you into every board meeting for the life of the company.

At Suja, gross margins hovered around 28 to 32 percent for years. We kept pushing for revenue growth, assuming scale would fix the margins. Sometimes scale helps -- you spread fixed costs across more volume, you get better supplier pricing, you negotiate better co-packer terms. But scale alone doesn't solve a structural margin problem. We learned that slowly, and the tuition was expensive.

My standing advice to consumable-product founders now: build a plan that gets gross margins to roughly 40 percent by the end of year two, and close to 50 percent by years three or four. Those aren't arbitrary numbers. They're the thresholds where growth begins to fund itself, where investors stop dictating the terms, and where you have the breathing room to make strategic decisions instead of survival decisions.

**The Tuesday that changed everything**

There's a story I tell a lot, and it's the most useful one I know.

In 2014, four years before that July 4th morning, our culinary team started experimenting with two-ounce wellness shots. Our head of sales, Nicky Vidaurreta, started pitching them to buyers. At the time we were still building our core juice business, still chasing distribution, still trying to figure out what a sustainable company looked like. The shots felt like a side experiment.

Eighteen months later, in the summer of 2018 -- the same summer Coke walked away -- Nicky secured a critical win: she swapped our kombucha shelf space at Costco for the wellness shots line.

Here's why that matters.

Our kombucha was outsourced to a third-party manufacturer. Gross margin: about 12 percent. The wellness shots were produced internally. Gross margin: about 60 percent.

That single product swap -- not a fundraise, not a marketing campaign, not a new retail account -- moved our company-wide gross margin from roughly 32 percent to roughly 40 percent.

Eight points. From one decision. Made on a Tuesday, years before we needed it.

"The decision that saves you is almost never the one you made on the day you needed saving. It's usually one you made eighteen months earlier, on a Tuesday, when nobody was watching."

That's the CPG capital efficiency lesson in one sentence. The founders who build capital-efficient businesses aren't the ones who resist growth. They're the ones who think carefully, years in advance, about *what they're growing*.

---

**What capital efficiency actually looks like in practice**

Let me make this concrete.

**Know your fully-loaded cost per unit.** Not just what it costs to make. The landed cost with freight, storage, and any third-party margin. I've sat across from founders who can tell me their ex-works cost but have never calculated what it costs to get the product to a retailer's distribution center and what that leaves for them. That number matters. A lot.

**Understand your product mix as a margin mix.** Every SKU has its own gross margin. The mix of SKUs you're selling determines the aggregate. At Suja, we launched 275 SKUs over seven years and discontinued all but 55. We learned, painfully, that some of what we were growing was actively hurting our margins. If you have a SKU with sub-20 percent margins and you're promoting it, you're spending money to make your business worse.

**Prioritize spending that builds compounding returns.** Not all spending is equal. A dollar in marketing that generates one-time trial is a different investment than a dollar that improves your repeat purchase rate from 25 percent to 40 percent. Consumers who come back are worth dramatically more than consumers who try you once. Build your spending strategy around compounding -- the things that get better over time, not just the things that move next week's numbers.

**Don't confuse distribution gains with margin gains.** I say this constantly. Adding 500 doors is exciting. It is not a margin strategy. If those 500 doors require a price concession, additional trade spend, and more freight complexity, you may have grown your top line while shrinking your financial foundation. Productive doors -- the ones where velocity holds and economics work -- are worth more than three times as many doors that just look good on a spreadsheet.

**Think about where your capital actually goes.** At Suja, roughly $60 million of the $115 million we raised went into manufacturing equipment and facilities. That was a strategic choice -- owning production gave us the speed and flexibility that defined our competitive advantage. But we also understood the trade-off: a co-manufacturing model might have preserved 20 percent more ownership. Neither path is automatically right. The founder who maps out those trade-offs in advance is capital-efficient. The founder who builds first and calculates later is usually surprised.

---

**After the turnaround: what actually happened**

Here's the part I didn't expect.

After Coke walked away in July 2018, we went into crisis mode. We cut marketing by more than $4 million. We eliminated low-margin outsourced products. We reduced operating expenses by more than $3 million. We focused on cash flow, margin discipline, and SKU-level profitability.

And then, in 2019, revenue grew by about 10 percent.

After cutting marketing in half.

That should have surprised me more than it did. But what it revealed is something I now believe deeply: **strong brands with loyal customers don't always need massive spending to grow.** The marketing budget we'd been pouring in was partially compensating for unit economics that didn't hold. When we fixed the economics, the brand could breathe on its own.

By April of 2019, for the first time in Suja's history, we had a credible path to annual profitability. Eight years in. We got there not by raising more capital, but by changing what we built with the capital we had.

---

Gross margin determines destiny. I've used that phrase in this industry for fifteen years, and every year I'm more convinced it's true.

It's not that fundraising doesn't matter. It does. I've led 44 rounds. I know how to raise. But capital amplifies whatever foundation you've already built. If the foundation is thin, more capital just means you'll find out what's broken on a larger scale.

The founders who win are the ones who build businesses that generate their own fuel... and then use outside capital to accelerate what's already working.

That's not a fundraising strategy.

That's the whole thing.
