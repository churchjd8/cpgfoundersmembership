A Whole Foods buyer I'd known for years pulled me aside after a category review in 2019. We'd been fighting for shelf space in the cold-pressed juice set, growing, winning... and she looked at me and said, casually, almost like it wasn't a big deal: "Jeff, I have sixty-two cold-pressed juice brands requesting a meeting this year. I couldn't possibly see them all."

Sixty-two.

When Suja launched in 2012, there were three national cold-pressed competitors. Three. Evolution Fresh, Blueprint Cleanse, and us. That was the whole game.

And now there were sixty-two brands chasing the same shelf we'd spent seven years building.

I drove back from that meeting thinking about something Nicky — our head of sales — had told me years earlier, when I'd naively assumed the hard part was getting onto shelf. I'd said something embarrassing like: "Nicky, I can't wait until we have full national distribution and have nothing to worry about." She looked at me like I'd asked what a cat man was (which, at the time, I also didn't know).

"Jeff," she said, "two years from now we'll be fighting tooth and nail to maintain our shelf space. We'll need the right cat man to win."

She was right. The moment you win the shelf, you become the hunted.

---

## The Problem With How Founders Think About Competitors

Most early-stage CPG founders make one of two mistakes.

The first is ignoring competition entirely. You're so heads-down on your own product, your own velocity numbers, your own cash flow that you don't notice the category shifting around you until a buyer tells you you're getting cut at the next reset.

The second is the opposite: obsessing over every competitor move. Watching every SKU launch, panic-pricing when someone undercuts you, spinning up innovation to chase trends you haven't had time to validate. Reactive. Expensive. Usually wrong.

Both paths lead to the same place: decisions made without real information.

Competitive intelligence isn't about tracking competitors. It's about reading the market. There's a difference, and it matters a lot.

## What "Reading the Market" Actually Means

When I promoted Jason Polinsky to category manager reporting directly to me at Suja, some people didn't get it. Category management felt like a support function, not a strategic one. But I'd watched enough brands get cut from planograms to understand that the person who reads the category data is the person who sees what's coming next.

What Jason did was analyze the competitive set the way a chess player reads the board... not move by move, but pattern by pattern.

Here's what that actually looked like in practice:

**Velocity per store per week** is where you start. Not yours — everyone's. Where are you in the percentile ranking against the set? If you're in the 50th percentile, you're hanging on. If you're below the 30th, you're probably not making the next reset. But the data cuts both ways: if a competitor is sitting below 20% and they've been there for two cycles, that's an opening. That's shelf space about to become available.

**Distribution patterns** tell you where competitors are sequencing their expansion. If a brand just got into Whole Foods in two regions, they're probably pitching Sprouts and Erewhon next. That's not threatening... that's information. You can prepare your own pitch with data that shows category performance in those regions before they even get there.

**Pricing architecture** shows you where the market is heading. When a premium brand starts offering a lower-price-point line, they're feeling pressure on velocity. When a mid-tier brand starts raising prices, they've either fixed their margins or they're about to lose distribution. Either way, you're learning something.

**Innovation signals** come from trade shows, retailer conversations, and supplier chatter. Expo West is more intelligence-gathering than sales pitching, if you know what to look for. What ingredients are five brands launching simultaneously? What packaging format is everyone suddenly moving toward? These aren't coincidences. They're signals.

Now here's the thing about most of this data: you don't need a million-dollar Nielsen contract to get it. Large brands spend millions. But startups can tap free and shared data resources through distributors like UNFI and KeHE, pull retailer summary reports, monitor category reset schedules by watching shelf-tag print dates, and have direct buyer conversations that a big CPG company never gets to have.

"The truth of the business is at the shelf," as one of our Kroger/Publix buyers, Jody Cnossen, used to say. Walk the store. Watch what moves. Watch what doesn't.

## The Rowdy Energy Lesson I Learned the Hard Way

I'll tell you about a company I was involved with called Rowdy Energy. We entered the energy drink market. And we entered it as roughly the hundredth brand to do so.

A hundred.

We paid nearly a million dollars in slotting to Albertsons to get on shelf. Velocity came in significantly below the hurdle. We were discontinued eight months later. That's a million dollars and eight months of organizational energy, mostly gone.

The energy drink category wasn't wrong. Red Bull is worth over $15 billion. The market is enormous. But the category was saturated, shelf space was already owned by heavily capitalized brands, and we entered without the data to tell us where the white space actually was.

"Hope is not a strategy." I've said it a thousand times. But I didn't run the numbers on Rowdy the way I should have. I got excited about the product and the founder and moved too fast. That's on me.

Contrast that with how we handled the wellness shot category.

## Entering Third and Winning Anyway

We'd been watching the wellness shot category for years before we launched. Competitors VIVE and KOR launched their versions first. Most founders see that and get panicked. We got curious.

We watched how the category was forming. We watched what the sensory experience needed to be for consumers to believe the products were working. We saw where they were placing the shots in stores (Whole Foods initially stuck them in health and beauty, completely separate from the juice set — which told us placement would be a battle). We understood what kind of velocity benchmarks would satisfy buyers before we committed.

Then we launched. We entered third in the wellness shot category behind VIVE and KOR. Within a year, we had captured more than thirty percent market share.

We also did something counterintuitive: we said yes to Costco's request to develop a plant-based milk in exchange for shelf space for the shots. The milk struggled... but the shots hit nearly two thousand dollars per week per club. That was a fifteen million dollar annual opportunity, and it moved our company-wide gross margin from around thirty-two percent to forty percent when we stopped outsourcing kombucha to fund the shift.

The Tuesday we decided to lean into shots... nobody was watching. It didn't feel like the decision that would save the company. But "the decision that saves you is almost never the one you made on the day you needed saving. It's usually one you made eighteen months earlier, on a Tuesday, when nobody was watching."

We read the market. We saw the category forming. We timed our entry to learn from the first movers without the first-mover cost.

That's competitive intelligence working.

## The Four Moves, Named

When the competitive signals tell you something, you have essentially four moves available. Get clear on which one you're actually making, because conflating them is how you waste money.

**Double down.** The category is growing. Your velocity is strong. Competitors entering actually validates the space. This is the moment to invest in velocity, not just distribution. "Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains." More doors without stronger turns just means more places to lose.

**Pivot within the category.** Something in the data is telling you that consumers have moved and you haven't. The wellness shots weren't a departure from cold-pressed juice. They were an extension of it, built on existing consumer trust. That's a pivot that worked. Kombucha was a departure. Consumers didn't follow us there because it wasn't in Suja's lane.

**Wait and watch.** Sometimes the right competitive move is to gather more data before spending a dollar. When we saw VIVE and KOR entering the shots market, we didn't rush. We used their launch to understand the category better. The information was free; let first movers pay for it.

**Harvest.** If a category is truly peaked... if sixty-two brands are fighting for the same shrinking shelf space and promotions have trained consumers to never pay full price... the right move might be to protect margin, not defend every door. Cut the SKUs with weak velocity. Tighten the distribution to your best accounts. Improve unit economics before you fight for every inch.

The category-crowding moment in cold-pressed juice, around 2018, was exactly that. Retailers had over-proliferated the refrigerated set. Consumers were being trained to only buy on discount. Category dollars were shrinking instead of growing. That's when we cut marketing by more than four million dollars, eliminated low-margin products, and watched revenue grow nine percent in 2019. Constraint had created clarity.

## The Thing About Sixty-Two Brands

I've thought about that Whole Foods moment a lot. Sixty-two brands wanting a meeting. The instinct is to feel threatened. What I eventually realized is that sixty-two brands want a meeting because the category is real. Because what we built was worth copying.

That's not a comfort, exactly. But it reframes the problem.

The brands that don't survive category crowding aren't the ones who got outcompeted. They're the ones who stopped reading the data. They either kept pushing doors when their velocity was slipping, or they froze when they should have pivoted, or they overpaid for shelf space on the hope that velocity would follow distribution.

"CPG is a 'Penny Profit' business, the pennies matter." The pennies also tell you everything. Watch them long enough and they'll tell you where the category is heading before any analyst report does.

The market is always talking. Most founders are too busy to listen.
