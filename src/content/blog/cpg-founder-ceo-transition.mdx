May 2019. Eight years into Suja. I had just done something I'd been trying to do for the better part of two years... brought the company back to profitability for the first time in its history.

We were operating with less than $100,000 in the bank on a $100M revenue company when Coca-Cola walked away in July of 2018. We shed low-margin products. Cut marketing by more than $4 million. And watched revenue actually grow. We turned -$10M in EBITDA to +$3M in a single year.

The board was happy. Investors exhaled. We had a real business again.

And I was completely done.

Not done with Suja. Done with being who Suja needed me to be in that moment.

---

There's a version of this story where stepping away from the company I built feels like failure. I don't tell it that way anymore. Stepping down as CEO of Suja was the hardest professional decision of my career. It was also the right one.

But I almost didn't make it. And I've watched a dozen founders since then make the same mistake I almost made: holding on to the title because letting go feels too much like losing.

Here's the thing nobody tells you early enough.

Every CPG brand has life stages... and so does every founder.

The traits that make you great in year one are often the exact traits that hold the company back in year seven. The visionary energy, the ability to sprint on incomplete information, the willingness to bet big when the data says be careful... that's founder DNA. That's what gets you from zero to $30M.

It is not always what gets you from $30M to $100M. And it is almost never what gets you from $100M to a profitable, sale-ready business.

---

By mid-2019, I knew something was off. My instincts kept pulling toward growth. New categories, new markets, more product. And what the business actually needed was the opposite. Stability. Debt reduction. Margin discipline. The board was right. I just wasn't wired for it at that stage.

That misalignment is the signal.

When what you want to do as a founder and what the company actually needs are consistently pointing in different directions... you have a decision to make. Either adapt your instincts to what the business needs. Or create the space for someone who's already wired that way.

I brought Bob DeBrode in as the new CEO in August 2019. I officially stepped away in January 2020. In 2020, Suja sold to Paine Schwartz for approximately $300 million. Today the company generates over $300 million in revenue with roughly 20% EBITDA margins.

Would that have happened if I'd stayed? I honestly don't know. I do know that by 2019, I had run 80-hour weeks for eight years straight and it had taken a real toll. My judgment was compromised in ways I couldn't fully see from inside it. My family could. And eventually, I could.

"Stepping down as CEO was the hardest professional decision of my career." I've said that before and I mean it. And I'll add: "Transitions are part of building something that lasts."

---

**So how do you know when it's actually time?**

Here's what I've seen in the founders I've coached, and what I felt in myself.

**Your board is your biggest daily opponent.** Not strategic tension, which is healthy. I mean grinding resistance to every financial guardrail they're trying to install. When your board stops being a resource and starts feeling like an obstacle... one of you has the wrong priorities. And in my experience, a founder who's been at it for six or seven years is usually the one whose perspective needs to shift.

**You've hired people smarter than you and you're still the bottleneck.** This one is painful to admit. One of the best things you can do as a leader is hire people who make you a little uncomfortable. But if you've done that and every major decision still runs through you... you're not leading. You're controlling. Those aren't the same thing.

**The work you love is no longer the work in front of you.** I loved building Suja. The early days, the Whole Foods launch, watching the category take off. By 2019, the work in front of me was debt restructuring and cost rationalization. Important work. Necessary work. Just not what I was built to love. And you cannot sustain high performance in a role you've stopped caring about.

**Your body is sending signals you're rationalizing away.** I don't say this lightly. After years of 80-hour weeks running a "better-for-you" beverage company, I was doing the worst possible thing for my own health. The insidious part of physical and emotional burnout is that it degrades your judgment gradually. You can't see it from inside. The people who love you can. "Put your own oxygen mask on first" isn't advice for the airplane. It's advice for the company you've staked everything on.

---

**If you decide it's time, here's how to do it without wrecking what you built.**

Don't leave before you've earned the right to leave. I brought Suja back to profitability before stepping away. That mattered. If you hand a burning building to a new CEO and walk out the door, that's not a leadership decision. That's abandonment.

Pick the right successor and actually give them the authority. This is where most transitions quietly fail. The founder leaves in title but stays in control in practice. Undermining the new CEO in leadership meetings. Going around them to employees they now manage. Relitigating decisions that have already been made. I've seen this destroy morale faster than almost anything else. If you're not ready to hand over the real keys, don't pretend to.

Find a role in the business that plays to your strengths. "Stepping back" doesn't have to mean stepping out. Maybe it's product. Maybe it's key retail relationships you've spent years building. Maybe it's fundraising or external partnerships. Find the lane where founder instincts are still an asset, not a liability. The board and the new CEO need that clarity just as much as you do.

Give the transition time to work. Employees watch the founder carefully in the months after a handoff. They're looking for signals. Are you supporting the new leader? Or are you positioning yourself for a comeback? How you show up in that window determines whether the transition actually takes root.

---

Here's the uncomfortable truth.

The company you built doesn't belong to you. It belongs to the customers who buy it, the employees whose families depend on it, the investors who bet on the vision. Your job as founder is to be the best possible steward of that mission. Which sometimes means recognizing that someone else can steward it better in its next phase.

"Had I managed the balance better, I might still be the CEO today, and preparing to take the company public. That lost opportunity still sits somewhere in the back of my mind."

I own that. And I also know that stepping away when I did gave the company the chance to survive and eventually thrive. Suja crossed $300 million. Nearly 350 employees at its peak, supporting well over 1,000 family members.

Not a bad outcome for a company that was operating on less than $100,000 in the bank eighteen months before.

The title was never the point. The mission was.

And sometimes, the most important leadership decision you make is knowing when your chapter is over.
