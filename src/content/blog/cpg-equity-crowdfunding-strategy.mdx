A founder walked up to me at a conference last year, clearly buzzing with excitement. She'd just launched a Wefunder campaign for her juice brand. Forty-eight hours in, she'd raised $180,000 from 600 investors. She was beaming.

I asked her three questions. How much of her company had she given away? What happens when she wants to raise a Series A? And had she thought about what it meant to have 600 co-owners who all have the right to receive annual reports, ask questions, and expect communication?

She hadn't.

I'm not telling that story to be a buzzkill. Equity crowdfunding is a real tool and it has worked for some CPG brands. But "it worked" and "it was the right choice" are two different things. After 44 fundraising rounds across 8 companies, I've seen almost every flavor of creative financing. This one deserves a hard look before you fall in love with it.

---

## What Equity Crowdfunding Actually Is

Under Regulation CF (part of the JOBS Act), companies can raise up to $5 million per year from non-accredited investors — meaning your actual customers, not just wealthy angels. Platforms like Wefunder, Republic, and StartEngine facilitate these raises. You post a campaign, set a valuation, and anyone can invest, sometimes for as little as $100.

The pitch is compelling. Your most loyal customers become your investors. They have a financial reason to tell their friends, post on social, and buy more product. You raise capital AND build community at the same time.

On paper, it sounds almost too good to be true.

It is not too good to be true. But there are real trade-offs most founders don't fully think through before they launch.

---

## The Appeal Is Real

Let's be honest about why this is attractive, especially at early stage.

First, traditional fundraising is brutal. I've spent roughly half my career in rooms trying to convince institutional investors to write checks. It's a grind. The rejection rate is high. The timelines are long. The Rule of Twos applies — it takes twice as long as you think and costs twice as much in time and energy.

Crowdfunding, comparatively, feels democratic. You're not pitching to a partner at a fund who's eaten lunch with three CEOs that week. You're telling your story to people who already like your product.

Second, if you have a genuine consumer community — a cult-like following, a strong Instagram presence, a category where your customers are loud evangelists — crowdfunding can activate that community in a way traditional fundraising cannot. Brands like Chubbies, Blue Bottle (before their acquisition), and a few emerging beverage brands have raised meaningful money this way.

Third, the non-dilutive funding alternatives aren't always available at early stage. Revenue-based financing wants revenue. Bank loans want collateral. If you're at $500K in annual revenue with a great product and no institutional track record, your options are limited.

So yes. The appeal is real. Now let's talk about the costs.

---

## The Cap Table Problem Nobody Talks About

Here's the number that should give you pause: the average Regulation CF raise results in somewhere between 200 and 2,000 individual investors.

Two thousand people who own a piece of your company.

Now, these investors are technically "passive" — Reg CF investors don't get board seats, voting rights are usually limited, and platforms like Wefunder can structure things to minimize administrative burden. I understand all that.

But consider what happens when you want to raise a Series A from a professional investor. They will run a thorough due diligence process. Part of that is reviewing your cap table. When a venture fund or strategic investor sees 800 names on a cap table, each owning a tiny fractional stake, their first reaction is almost never enthusiasm. It adds legal complexity. It raises questions about information rights. Some will walk.

There's a reason experienced CPG investors talk about "clean cap tables." It matters. Not in every deal, not in every situation. But it matters more often than crowdfunding campaigns let on.

I've seen founders complicate future raises by crowdfunding early. I've also seen founders clean up messy cap tables through secondary transactions — but that takes time, legal work, and money. Don't create a problem you'll have to pay to fix later.

---

## The Valuation Trap

When you run a crowdfunding campaign, you have to set a valuation. This is where early-stage founders often stumble.

The temptation is to set a high valuation to minimize dilution. But if you set an unrealistic valuation on your Reg CF round, you're creating a mark — a number that investors in your next round will look at. If you raised on a $10 million valuation and you've grown 40% since then, a Series A investor might reasonably expect to come in at a premium. But if your metrics don't support that, you're in a down-round scenario that's awkward to navigate.

Gross margin determines destiny. It also determines valuation. In CPG, the typical rule of thumb for early-stage brands is 3–4x trailing twelve-month revenue. If your TTM revenue is $800K, you're looking at a valuation in the $2.4M–$3.2M range. That's not glamorous. But it's defensible.

Hope is not a strategy, and hope is definitely not a valuation methodology.

Set the crowdfunding valuation where you'd set it for any investor: based on real metrics, reasonable multiples, and where you can credibly defend a next round at a higher mark.

## When Crowdfunding Actually Makes Sense for CPG

I'm not here to tell you it never works. It does. Here's when:

**Your customers are your most natural investors.** Think of a mission-driven brand — organic, sustainable, community-focused. Customers who care deeply about what you're building and would genuinely feel pride in owning a small piece of it. This is the sweet spot. The investor becomes an ambassador.

**You've exhausted the right options first.** Try angels, try family offices, try strategic introductions. If you've run a proper process and come up short, crowdfunding is a legitimate bridge. Don't start with it.

**You can live with the complexity.** If you're raising $1–2 million in a Reg CF round and you've thought carefully about future round impacts, cap table management, and communication obligations, and you still want to do it — then go in with eyes open.

**Your product has a built-in community.** Fitness, outdoor lifestyle, health-conscious consumers who follow brands the way they follow athletes. These categories tend to have the most natural crowdfunding audiences.

**You're using it as marketing as much as financing.** Some founders run a small Reg CF raise primarily because the press around it generates more awareness than they could buy with the capital raised. That's a legitimate calculus if you go in knowing it.

## The Honest Alternative

Let me tell you what I tell every founder before they run toward the crowd.

Build your network first. Reach out to 50 people in your personal and professional orbit who are accredited investors or know people who are. Tell your story. Ask for introductions. Run the 8-step process I've written about elsewhere. You'll probably get a no from 40 of them. But you might get 8–10 angels who write $25K–$100K checks, who have relevant experience, who will make real introductions, and who won't complicate your cap table for future rounds.

The math on that is usually better. Not because the money is worth more, but because the relationships are worth more. "Relationships are worth more than equity. Protect them accordingly."

Institutional and semi-institutional investors — family offices, strategic angels, former CPG operators — bring far more than capital. They bring introductions to buyers, co-mans, brokers, distributors. They've been in the room when the big strategic buyers show up. That's worth ten times the check itself.

Crowdfunding investors are not that. They're customers who believe in you, which is wonderful. But they're not the same as a former VP of Sales at Whole Foods who's written a $50K check and has your cell number.

## The Five Questions Before You Launch a Campaign

If you're still considering it, here's what I want you to be able to answer clearly before you click "go live":

**1. What is the specific use of funds?** Not "growth" — actual line items. Inventory build? Marketing spend? Co-man deposit? The more specific, the more credible you look to backers.

**2. What valuation can you honestly defend in your next round?** Run the model. If this round is at $5M and you think you'll need a Series A in 18 months, where do you need to be to raise at $8–10M? Does your growth plan get you there?

**3. How will you manage 500+ investors?** Platform tools help but you still own the communication and relationship. What's your plan for quarterly updates, questions, and annual reports?

**4. Have you talked to a securities attorney?** Reg CF has real compliance requirements. Annual financial statements (for raises over $107,000). Disclosure documents. Filing requirements. This is not a "figure it out as you go" zone.

**5. Have you exhausted the traditional path?** Not thought about it. Actually pursued it. Run a proper process with 30 to 50 potential investors. If you've done that and come up short, then crowdfunding makes sense as a complement or bridge.

If you can answer all five with confidence, you might be a good candidate.

---

## What I Keep Coming Back To

After 44 rounds and $212 million raised, I've learned that the best investors are the ones who believe in you before you need them and stay constructive when things get hard.

Crowdfunding can absolutely give you believers. The question is whether they're the right believers for what comes next.

Most early-stage CPG brands don't need thousands of small investors. They need 10 to 20 people who know the business cold, who can make a phone call to a Whole Foods buyer or a co-manufacturer when you need it, and who are aligned with where you want to take the brand over five to seven years.

Capital amplifies whatever foundation you've built. Make sure you're building the right foundation — with the right people around the table — before you decide how to fill those seats.

Raise smart. Not just fast.
