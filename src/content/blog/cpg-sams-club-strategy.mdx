I've watched this play out more times than I'd like to admit.

Brand wins at Costco. Velocity is strong. The buyer relationship is real. The founder calls me riding high and asks: "Jeff... what do you think about Sam's Club?"

I already know what comes next. The logic sounds airtight: Sam's is a club. We know clubs now. We have the pack design, the merchandising approach, the proof points. Let's go get that account.

Nine months later, they're pulling out of Sam's with margin damage and a story they'd rather not tell.

Here's what happened. They treated Sam's Club like a second Costco.

It isn't.

## Same Parking Lot. Completely Different DNA.

Sam's Club is Walmart in a warehouse. That's not a knock. That's the most important thing you can understand about the account.

Costco is its own thing. Proud, independent, driven by the "Kirkland culture" that says private label can be better than national brands. Costco runs a treasure hunt. They rotate items. They test and learn. They give you 10 or 13 weeks to prove yourself, and if you perform, you might earn a longer relationship. It's actually a forgiving model if you understand the game.

Sam's is Bentonville. Full stop.

They share the same corporate DNA as Walmart. Same EDLP pricing philosophy. Same expectation that your price point delivers undeniable value per ounce, per serving, per dollar. Same discipline around item count. When you're building your Sam's Club strategy, you're essentially building a Walmart strategy for a warehouse format. The implications run deep, and most founders miss them until it's too late.

## The Pricing Integrity Problem

Here's where things get expensive fast.

Because Sam's shares a parent company with Walmart, the implied price expectation on your product bleeds across channels. If you price at Sam's in a way that undercuts what you're doing at Target or conventional grocery... you've created channel conflict that can take years to unwind.

I learned a version of this at Suja. A senior Target buyer pulled us aside before we entered Walmart and gave us one of the most valuable pieces of advice I got during those years. She told us: don't use the same barcode or the same pack size across every retailer. If you do, Target will price-match Walmart. And once you've price-matched down, you don't price back up.

We launched a 10.5-ounce bottle at Walmart and a 12-ounce bottle at Target. Small distinction. Big protection.

With Sam's Club, the pack design discipline is even more critical. Your Sam's pack should be designed for Sam's. Not repurposed from Costco. Not an oversized version of your Whole Foods SKU. A dedicated pack, with a dedicated UPC, at a price point that delivers genuine value without destroying your channel economics everywhere else.

"Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains." That's true in any channel. But at Sam's, it has a corollary: don't confuse a cheap price with a sustainable strategy.

Gross margin determines destiny. You can chase the Sam's account and win it. If the economics underneath it are bleeding out, you've just put more volume behind a business model that doesn't work.

## You Don't Get a Safety Net

At Costco, the rotation model is actually your friend if you play it right. They'll run a 10 or 13-week test. You perform, you stay. You underperform, you're out, but you've got real sell-through data and a relationship that survived an honest trial.

Sam's doesn't work that way.

When you get a Sam's Club program, you're committing to a planogram. You're in or you're out. There's no treasure hunt dynamic protecting you while you build velocity. That means you need to go in with proof. Real velocity data from Costco, conventional grocery, or wherever you've built your business. You need to show the Sam's buyer that your product moves off a shelf... not that you believe it will.

At Rowdy Energy, I learned this lesson in a hard way. We paid nearly $1 million in slotting fees to get into Albertsons, and because our velocity was significantly below hurdle rates, they discontinued us after 8 months. A million dollars of learning.

The club channel punishes speculative positioning even harder than conventional grocery does. Your numbers have to be real before you walk into that Bentonville office.

Hope is not a strategy. Sam's buyers know it too.

## The Member's Mark Reality

Sam's private label is called Member's Mark. And it's aggressive.

Kirkland Signature at Costco is powerful, but Costco usually gives national brands room to live because the treasure hunt requires variety. Member's Mark competes more directly, especially in high-velocity consumable categories. If you're going into a category at Sam's where Member's Mark is already established, you need to be crystal clear on your differentiation.

Not marginally better. Meaningfully, obviously better.

The Sam's member is looking for value-per-dollar performance, and Member's Mark has earned their trust. If you can't answer the question "why should a Sam's member pay more for ours when Member's Mark exists?"... you probably need to wait until you can answer it convincingly.

That question is your stress test. Run it before you build the program, not after you've already paid to be on the shelf.

## Sequence Before You Sprint

The distribution sequencing principle I've been preaching for thirty years applies here too.

The path I recommend for most brands: Whole Foods first. Prove your concept in natural. Then conventional grocery and Target. Build velocity data. Then Costco. Win the treasure hunt and earn that club-format proof point. Then, and only then, do you consider Sam's Club.

Every step in that sequence gives you data that makes the next conversation easier. Walking into Sam's having won at Costco, with velocity data, with a dedicated pack design... that's a completely different meeting than walking in with ambition and a pitch deck.

The brands that skip this sequence don't fail at Sam's because Sam's is hard. They fail because they walked in before they had the right to be there.

"Dream boldly. Plan soberly." That's not just about business planning. It's about retail expansion too.

## What Winning Actually Looks Like

The brands that win at Sam's Club and stay there do a handful of things consistently.

They design for the format. Not a modified Costco pack. Not an oversized Whole Foods SKU. A Sam's pack, built for Sam's economics and Sam's members. The pack has to feel like it belongs in a warehouse club, at a price that makes the member feel smart for buying it.

They go in with data. Velocity proof. Repeat rate evidence. Category performance that tells a clear story about why your product belongs in that planogram. Buyers don't take risks on potential. They take risks on evidence.

They protect their channel economics. Pricing discipline across Sam's, Walmart, Target, and conventional grocery has to be intentional and maintained. The moment those channels start fighting each other on price, you've got a problem that's genuinely difficult to fix.

They play the long game. Sam's relationships compound over time, same as any major retail relationship. The brands that grow with Sam's are the ones who focus on helping the member win, not just getting their product on a shelf. Category growth. Value delivery. Consistent execution. That's what keeps you on a Sam's planogram through the next reset.

## The Account Is Worth It. Earn It First.

There are roughly 600 Sam's Clubs in the United States. At the right velocity, with the right pack, with the right economics... that's a real revenue engine. Especially for a brand that has proven itself in other channels and is genuinely ready to scale into the club format.

But earn it first.

Go in with data. Design for the channel. Price with discipline. And understand that you're having a Bentonville conversation... in a warehouse.

The mistake isn't trying Sam's. The mistake is walking in with a Costco playbook.
