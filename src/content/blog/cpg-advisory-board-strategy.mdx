It was 2015. Coca-Cola had just written a $90 million check.

And we were getting killed online.

Organic consumers don't love it when a juice brand they trust gets bought by the world's largest beverage company. The comments were brutal. The headlines were worse. I needed to figure out how to respond, and fast.

I called Seth Goldman. Co-founder of Honest Tea, which Coca-Cola had also acquired years earlier. He'd been through this exact moment. His advice was immediate.

"Don't argue with customers. Invite them to watch. Tell them to give you a year. Ask them to judge you by your actions, not by their assumptions."

I wrote it down. Then called John Foraker -- former President of General Mills' natural sector, someone who'd navigated corporate acquisitions at scale.

He said almost the exact same thing.

Two different people. Zero coordination. Identical answer.

That's what a real advisory relationship feels like. Not a name on a slide deck. Not a logo in a pitch. Someone who's been in the foxhole you're about to enter... who picks up the phone when everything's on fire.

Most founders build advisory boards backwards. They collect impressive names. They put logos on a slide. They schedule a kickoff call and then nothing. The advisor hears from them six months later, when they need a warm intro to a VC.

That's not an advisory board. That's a costume.

## The Difference Between an Advisor and a Name

Here's the truth: anyone can collect "advisor" titles. The harder thing is getting people who actually show up.

The best advisors I've had didn't just give advice. They made calls on my behalf. They walked into rooms and said, "You need to talk to this person." They sent introductions I didn't ask for because they were paying attention.

Bill Weiland, the founder of Presence Marketing, was like that for Suja. Bill was selective. He didn't take every brand. When he said yes to representing us at Whole Foods, it wasn't just a contract -- it was an endorsement. It opened doors nothing else would have opened.

I still think about what that "yes" was worth. One introduction from Bill in those early days was worth 50 cold emails and two Expo West appearances. No exaggeration.

(At one Christmas party, after a few cocktails, Bill somehow left with my Nutcracker decoration. I've never let him forget it. But more importantly, I've never forgotten what it felt like to have someone in that corner who genuinely believed in what we were building.)

That's what you're looking for.

An advisor who's not actively advocating for you isn't an advisor. They're a line on your deck.

## The Four Types You Actually Need

I've built advisory boards across eight companies. The best ones had four distinct archetypes. When one is missing, you feel it.

**1. The Kingmaker**

Every industry has them. The people who are one degree of separation from everyone who matters. In natural CPG, it might be a veteran broker who's placed 20 brands at Whole Foods. Or a senior category buyer who just retired after 15 years. They have relationships it would take you a decade to build on your own.

"Connect with connectors" is something I say often. This is why.

You don't need 10 advisors like this. You need one. And once you find them, you protect that relationship like it's your most valuable asset -- because it is.

**2. The Category Veteran**

Someone who built the kind of brand you want to build. Not a generic "entrepreneur" or "marketing consultant" -- someone who specifically navigated the channel, the category, the scale you're headed toward.

Seth Goldman built Honest Tea from a cooler in his kitchen to a Coca-Cola acquisition. John Foraker ran the natural sector at General Mills. When I needed to navigate the Coke backlash, they didn't need context. They'd already lived it.

Find someone who is 10 years ahead of where you're going. They'll save you from mistakes they've already made.

The key is specificity. "CPG experience" is not a brief. "Someone who has done three rounds of fundraising in the natural channel and navigated a strategic investor relationship" is a brief. The closer the match to your actual situation, the more valuable their pattern recognition becomes.

**3. The Functional Expert**

The specific skill gap you have right now.

If you've never managed a co-manufacturing relationship at scale, find someone who has. If you've never negotiated retailer terms above $30M revenue, find someone who's done it. If your cap table is getting complicated, find someone who's been through six rounds of dilution and can translate what the terms actually mean.

The mistake I see: founders are too general here. They get a "marketing advisor" when what they actually need is someone who's driven velocity-per-store at Kroger specifically. Or they get a "finance advisor" when the real gap is cash flow modeling for a company scaling from $5M to $20M.

Narrow it. The more precise the fit, the more valuable the advice.

**4. The Internal Champion**

This one's underrated. Maybe the most important of all.

When I was in the final stages of the Coca-Cola deal, Mike Saint John -- then President of Minute Maid Juice -- became our internal champion inside Coke. He was willing to take reputational risk for us inside one of the largest organizations in the world. Without him, I don't believe we close the deal.

"No deal survives without an internal champion on the acquiring side willing to take reputational risk."

You often can't put the Internal Champion on your formal advisory board. They work somewhere else. But cultivating those relationships -- with people inside the accounts and acquirers you want to work with -- is some of the most valuable time you'll ever spend.

This is a long game. You build these relationships before you need them.

## What to Offer Advisors (and What They Actually Want)

Let's get practical.

Standard advisory equity in CPG is **0.1% to 0.5%** with a two-year vesting schedule, monthly. No cliff. Simple. The deal: stay engaged, make meaningful contributions, and the equity accretes over time.

Don't give equity to people who don't earn it. I've watched founders hand out 0.25% to five people they had coffee with once. That's 1.25% of the company gone for absolutely nothing. And remember -- that equity doesn't just dilute you. It dilutes every investor who wrote you a check.

Give equity to people who:
- Take five or more meaningful actions on your behalf per year
- Make introductions that lead to actual business conversations
- Pick up the phone when you call -- not when it's convenient for them

For monthly or quarterly touchpoints, fine. But don't let the scheduled call be the whole relationship. The best advisory relationships I've had... they were calling me with something useful. Not waiting for the calendar invite.

One thing I'll add: experienced advisors often don't need the equity. The Kingmaker type, especially -- they may already be wealthy, and 0.25% of your seed-stage company doesn't move their needle. What they want is to be associated with winners. They want the relationship. They want to help because they remember when someone helped them.

Lead with the opportunity, not the comp.

## When to Build Your Advisory Board

Start earlier than you think.

By the time you're at $3-5M in revenue and have identified your growth plan, you should have at least two or three advisors actively engaged.

Most founders wait until they're about to raise and then scramble to fill out an advisory page before the deck goes out. Advisors can tell. And experienced ones will pass.

"Enthusiasm attracts. Desperation repels." That's as true for advisors as it is for investors. The best time to recruit an advisor is when things are going well -- when you have a real story, real momentum, and something to offer beyond urgency.

This also means building relationships before they become transactional. I met Bill Weiland before I needed him. I got to know Seth Goldman because I admired what he built, not because I needed his Rolodex. Those relationships were genuine. And that's precisely why they held up when things got hard.

## The Mistakes I See Most Often

**No clear expectations.** You recruit someone, they join, and you never actually tell them what "good" looks like. How often should they hear from you? What kinds of asks are appropriate? What does a successful first six months look like? Clarity now prevents resentment later.

**You over-advise and under-deploy.** You get advice. You write it down. You never act on it. And then next month you ask for more advice. Advisors track this. They lose interest. They start showing up to your calls half-prepared, because they've learned their input goes nowhere.

**You pick impressive over useful.** The former CEO with a famous name who's too busy to return your calls is not a better advisor than the retired Whole Foods buyer who knows every regional category manager personally. Ask yourself: can this person open a specific door I need opened right now?

**You build the board for the pitch, not the company.** This is the version I see most often. The advisor roster is designed to impress investors, not to actually help the business. The logos look great in the deck. But when you actually need help -- when a key retailer is threatening to delist you, when your co-man missed three consecutive production runs -- those impressive names don't answer the phone.

---

## Build This Before You Need It

There's a quote I think about often from my Suja days: "Partnerships aren't transactions. They're systems."

The advisors who moved the needle for me weren't people I brought in strategically. They were people I'd cultivated relationships with because I respected what they'd built. Because I asked questions and actually listened. Because I showed up for them before I needed anything.

The most valuable network you'll ever have is built long before you need to call on it.

Dream boldly. Plan soberly. And put the right people in your corner before the next hard season arrives.

Because in CPG... it's coming.
