Imagine calling your co-manufacturer to say you're moving production. The conversation is perfectly friendly... until you ask for the formula and the production records.

“We'll need to review what we can release.”

That's a hypothetical conversation, but it's exactly the question I want you to resolve before you need another plant. You paid for development. Your logo is on the bottle. You've spent the last two years getting people to buy it. Of course it feels like the formula is yours.

Now show me where the agreement says that.

Before relying on that assumption, answer a more useful question: if you needed to move production, what could you legally take with you, and what information would the next manufacturer actually receive?

I put formula ownership on the fatal-flaw list because the issue can stay invisible while the relationship is working. Then a price increase, capacity problem, or quality dispute makes it urgent. You don't want your first serious look at those rights to happen when you're already under pressure to ship.

## Separate ownership from access

A contract may address ownership without giving your team a complete, usable production package. Conversely, having a spreadsheet of ingredients does not by itself establish your rights to use everything in it.

Review three distinct questions with your attorney and technical team:

1. **What does the brand own?** Identify the original formula, development work, and later modifications.
2. **What does the manufacturer retain?** Clarify pre-existing processes, know-how, and other proprietary inputs.
3. **What can move?** Confirm which materials and rights you can provide to a replacement manufacturer, under what conditions, and at what cost.

The answer comes from the agreements and the facts of development. Avoid treating “we paid for it” as the entire analysis.

And bring in a lawyer who understands CPG manufacturing agreements. You're dealing with a product, a process, and often several contributors. Your flavor house may have proprietary work of its own. Your co-man may bring manufacturing know-how. Get specific about how those pieces fit together so everyone understands what the brand can use and what it can move.

## Build a production transfer checklist

I'd ask your technical lead a very practical question: “If we had to qualify another facility, what's missing from the folder?” Depending on the product, that may include:

- The current formula with version history and ingredient specifications.
- Approved suppliers and acceptable substitutions.
- Processing parameters, order of addition, and critical controls.
- Packaging specifications and ownership of molds or other tooling.
- Finished-product specifications, test methods, and release criteria.
- Shelf-life work and relevant production or quality records.

Some information may belong to the manufacturer or require separate access arrangements. Identify those gaps explicitly. The checklist is a starting point for negotiation, not a claim that every item automatically belongs to you.

## Protect confidentiality as you improve access

You need the right people to have usable information without distributing it casually.

The [USPTO's trade secret guidance](https://www.uspto.gov/ip-policy/trade-secret-policy) explains that trade secret protection involves economically valuable information that is not generally known and reasonable efforts to keep it secret. A formula can be part of that information, but protection requires attention to how it is handled.

Use controlled access, confidentiality obligations, and a clear record of which version is current. Avoid leaving the only copy in one person's inbox. Have counsel distinguish confidentiality obligations from ownership and licensing terms; those provisions answer different questions.

## Agree on the exit while the relationship is healthy

Ask what happens if either party ends the relationship. How much transition support is available? What gets delivered? Who pays? What happens to remaining ingredients, packaging, finished goods, and work in process?

Address modifications as well. If the manufacturer improves the process or changes an ingredient, who approves the change, who documents it, and what rights does the brand have afterward?

A good manufacturing relationship can handle this conversation. You're planning how the business works if circumstances change, including circumstances neither of you controls. You can value a partner and still document the exit.

I'd also ask who on each team maintains the current records. A beautifully drafted agreement won't help your operations person find the approved specification if every department has a different version. Decide who signs off on a change and where that approved version lives.

## Ownership does not make a transfer instant

Even with clean documentation and agreed rights, a new facility may need trials, validation, new sourcing, and packaging changes. The same recipe can behave differently on different equipment.

Build transition time into your continuity plan. Ask a qualified technical lead to identify what must be tested before commercial production. Avoid promising a retailer that a replacement plant can ship on a date you haven't established with that plant.

Give the transfer a realistic timeline. I talk a lot about the “Rule of Twos” because founders, myself included, can be wildly optimistic about what a project will take. Here, build room for the trials and the surprises. Don't book your whole transition around everything working the first time.

## Your next action: request the current package

Ask your team to locate the signed development and manufacturing agreements, the current formula version, and the list of missing transfer information. Assign an owner to resolve each gap.
