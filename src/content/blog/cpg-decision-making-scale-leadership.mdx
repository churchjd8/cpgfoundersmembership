I drove a forklift into a pallet of beet juice once.

Not metaphorically. Literally. First delivery to a Whole Foods distribution center, me maneuvering a tiny 28-footer into a loading dock surrounded by people in 53-foot semis who clearly knew what they were doing. I did not know what I was doing. Grabbed a forklift to unload our pallets, clipped the edge, and watched a few hundred bottles of beet juice spill across the floor.

Now here's the thing. I was the co-founder and CEO of Suja Juice at the time. We had a dozen people who should have been operating that forklift. But I was there doing it myself, because that's what founders do in Stage 1. You do everything. You do it scrappily. You learn fast. And most importantly — you're building a culture by example, so you show up.

That part was right. The problem comes later.

The problem comes when the company is doing $10 million and you're still trying to run that forklift.

---

Here's what nobody tells you about scaling a CPG brand: the thing that works brilliantly to get you to $5 million becomes the thing that strangles you on the way to $25 million. And the strangler isn't usually your product. It's not your margin. It's not your retail strategy.

It's you.

At some point, every founder becomes the bottleneck.

You're the one approving label changes. Approving purchase orders. Reviewing every vendor quote. Being cc'd on every retailer email. Attending every production call. And it feels right because you care more than anyone and you know the business better than anyone... and those things are both true. But none of that changes the math.

You have 24 hours in a day. If decisions have to cycle through you, your business can only move as fast as you can personally decide. In a fast-moving CPG environment — where Trader Joe's is asking you for product in six weeks and a distributor issue is escalating at 2 a.m. — that's not a sustainable model.

Growth doesn't create strength. It reveals whether you ever truly had it in the first place.

---

At Suja, somewhere around the $30–40 million mark, I had to confront something I was pretending wasn't happening. Every decision that mattered was still coming to me. My head of sales, Nicky Vidaurreta, was brilliant... but waiting on approvals. Our operations team was incredible... but calling me when anything went sideways.

I had hired great people and then basically told them their judgment wasn't good enough to trust.

That's what micromanagement is, by the way. It's not a management style. It's fear dressed up as oversight. Fear that someone will make a mistake. Fear that the business will go sideways without you holding it together. Fear, usually, that you have no idea how to lead without being in the weeds.

I've had it. Most founders I've worked with have had it. The question isn't whether it shows up. The question is whether you catch it before it breaks something.

---

So here's the framework I built that actually worked. I call it cul-de-sac management.

A cul-de-sac has a lot of houses. Everybody can walk to the center. Everybody has a perspective. The mistake is thinking that means everyone has to vote.

The idea is simple: gather input from the widest possible group of people who have relevant knowledge or a stake in the outcome. Put everyone in the cul-de-sac. Let them all talk. Then one person — one accountable decision-maker — takes all of that input and makes the call.

Not consensus. Informed decision-making.

This matters because consensus kills speed. If you need five people to agree before you can change a label or respond to a buyer, you're operating on the timeline of your slowest person. The cul-de-sac approach gives you the best of both worlds: broad intelligence, fast execution.

Here's how it looks in practice:

**First, decide what requires you and what doesn't.** Not every decision is equal. Pricing changes? Yes, that comes to you. A $3,000 trade spend activation at a regional account? No. Write it down. Be explicit. "Anything over $X requires approval. Anything under $X is yours." Your team can't trust the rules if the rules aren't clear.

**Second, push decisions to the person nearest the decision.** If information has to cycle up two or three levels to reach someone who can act, you've built a system that's slower than your competitors by design. The person running your Kroger account knows more about Kroger today than you do. Let them act on it.

**Third, build the reporting that keeps you informed without keeping you involved.** The Monday Morning Dashboard — seven questions, seven numbers, fifteen minutes — is how I stayed connected to the business without being the center of it. If the number is in the range, the team runs. If it's not, I get involved. That's the system.

---

About two years into Suja's growth phase, a transformer blew on our production floor at 2 a.m. Power went out mid-run. Orders due in the morning.

The person with a key to the roof unit was our landlord, asleep at home. So our production crew did what they had to do: climbed a tree next to the building, jumped from the branch onto the roof, and reconnected the transformer themselves.

Orders shipped on time. Our customers never knew what it took.

I wasn't there. I didn't even know it happened until the next morning.

And that's the story I tell every time someone asks me what good delegation looks like.

The team didn't call me to ask permission. They didn't wait. They looked at what needed to happen and they did it — because they knew the culture we'd built rewarded trying over waiting. They knew they wouldn't get fired for a creative solution, even if it had gone badly.

Culture is just the decisions that compound. Every time you let someone solve a problem without you, you're sending a signal. Every time you swoop in to fix something your team should have handled, you're sending a different one.

The signal you send at $5 million becomes the culture you're stuck with at $50 million.

---

There's a phrase I've used for thirty years: "I realized how much smarter *we* are than *I* am." I believed it when I said it. But believing it and acting on it are two different things.

Acting on it means hiring people who know things you don't, then getting out of their way. It means catching yourself when you're reviewing decisions that aren't yours to make anymore. It means understanding that "staying close to the business" is not the same thing as "being in the business."

The founders who scale well make this shift. The ones who don't... they become the thing that limits their brand. They stall at $8 million or $12 million or $15 million and then wonder why they can't get over the hump.

You know what the hump is? It's you.

"Discipline over hustle. Precision over passion. Specialists over generalists."

That's the Stage 3 mantra. And it starts with the hardest decision most founders ever make: trusting the team they built.

Raise your hand. Let someone else carry the forklift.
