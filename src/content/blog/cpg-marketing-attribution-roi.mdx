We ran billboards in Manhattan when we had four Whole Foods stores in the entire city.

Four stores.

I'm not kidding. We thought we were being aggressive. Building the brand. Creating buzz. What we were actually doing was creating awareness with nowhere to convert it. Thousands of people walked past those billboards, got curious, went looking for the product... and couldn't find it.

That's not marketing. That's expensive frustration.

It was one of the early lessons that forced me to confront a question I see CPG founders dodge constantly: do you actually know what's driving your sales? Not your gut feeling. Not what your marketing agency is telling you. Real, traceable evidence that connects a dollar spent to a bottle moved.

Most founders can't answer that. And that's a problem.

## The Attribution Illusion

Here's the trap. You run a demo program, do some social media, send a few PR pitches, put up some digital ads. Sales go up 20% in a quarter. Everyone high-fives. The marketing team takes credit. The sales team takes credit. The founder feels great.

But which of those things actually drove the lift?

Almost certainly, it was shelf space.

I've watched founders pour six figures into consumer marketing when the real constraint was distribution. "Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains." I say that a lot because I've lived it. When you go from 200 doors to 600 doors, sales triple. Has nothing to do with marketing. You just put your product in front of three times as many customers.

The attribution gets muddy fast. And without clean attribution, you can't make smart allocation decisions. You end up funding what feels good instead of what works.

## The Two Questions That Actually Matter

Over forty-four fundraising rounds and eight companies, I've developed a simple mental model. Two questions:

1. **Are more people finding the product?** (distribution and discovery)
2. **Are the people who find it buying it?** (velocity and conversion)

These are not the same question. They require completely different interventions.

Here's the thing that trips founders up... marketing doesn't answer the first question. Distribution does. You can market your way into trial, but only if someone can find the product.

Marketing's real job is the second question. It builds preference. It drives repeat purchase. It makes someone pick up your product off a shelf stocked with three competitors.

So before you spend a dollar on consumer marketing, you need clean velocity data. Specifically:

- **Units per store per week (USPW):** the bedrock metric. Are you selling more per door than you were last period? If yes, your marketing or product might be doing something. If no, you have a placement or product problem, not a marketing problem.
- **Repeat rate:** what percentage of customers come back? Below 30% repeat in the natural channel means you have a product or value perception problem. Marketing can't fix that.
- **Promoted versus non-promoted velocity:** what percent of your sales happen on promotion? Above 40% means you've built a deal-seeking customer base, not a loyal one.

Pull these numbers before you add spend. The sequence matters.

If velocity is flat while you're adding distribution, marketing might be working fine... but you need more doors before it shows up in aggregate revenue. If velocity is declining while distribution is holding, you have a shelf problem. If both are flat, you might have a marketing problem. Run the diagnosis before you write the check.

## What to Actually Measure by Channel

Let me get specific about each major channel.

**Demos and sampling.** This still works. It's the oldest form of CPG marketing for a reason. But you need the velocity lift data around it. In weeks when you demo a store, track per-door velocity versus weeks you don't. I want to see a demo that lifts per-door velocity at least 15-20% in the week of the event, and ideally shows some residual lift the following week. If demo velocity isn't higher than baseline, you're paying $400 a day for brand awareness you can't measure. That's not what demos are for.

**Earned media and PR.** Genuinely hard to attribute to shelf velocity. A major placement can spike your website traffic, Amazon search volume, and DTC sales in a measurable way. What it doesn't do cleanly is lift your retail velocity in stores that weren't affected by the media reach. The honest answer: PR builds the category story and the investor narrative. Measure it by what it unlocks, not by week-over-week USPW. Retail doors opened. Investor meetings booked. Strategic partner conversations started.

**Digital advertising.** For DTC, this is entirely measurable. Cost per acquisition, return on ad spend, lifetime value versus customer acquisition cost... track it precisely or don't run it. For retail brands using digital to try to drive in-store lift, you're measuring a correlation at best. Run a geo-test: heavy digital spend in one market, no spend in a control market, compare velocity by market. Rough but directional. Better than nothing.

**Trade spend and promotions.** This is where most emerging brands spend the most and think about it the least. Track promotional lift every single time. Pull velocity during a TPR versus your everyday velocity. If promoted velocity is 3x your everyday velocity, you're selling to deal hunters. You need to also see everyday velocity climbing over time, otherwise the promotion is subsidizing volume, not building it.

A useful benchmark: you want at least 20-25% incremental units during a promotion that you wouldn't have sold at everyday price. If you're just pulling forward purchases from the same loyal customers, you're discounting margin for nothing.

CPG is a "Penny Profit" business. The pennies matter. Every trade dollar that doesn't convert new buyers or lift repeat rates is just giving margin away.

## What Jason Taught Me About Real Data

Jason Polinsky ran category management at Suja, and I promoted him to report directly to me. Best move I made during that scaling phase.

He was the one who taught me to separate what was real from what felt real. We'd have a quarter with great aggregate sales numbers and he'd walk in and say: "Jeff, about 80% of that growth came from the Costco rotation and the new TJ's launch. Our velocity in the natural channel is actually down 4% year over year."

That changes everything. Are you growing the brand, or are you growing distribution? Those require different strategies, different investments, different conversations with your board.

He loved the data the way I love a cold-pressed green juice on a hot day. It gave him energy. And when you have someone who genuinely loves the numbers and isn't afraid to tell you what they mean, you stop chasing the wrong things.

If you can't afford a dedicated category manager yet, do the analysis yourself. The data is there in your retailer portals, your distributor dashboards, your SPINS subscription if you have one. You don't need a million-dollar Nielsen contract to run a meaningful velocity analysis. You need discipline and a willingness to face what the numbers are telling you.

## The Hardest Truth About CPG Marketing

Here's what I've seen sink more marketing budgets than anything else.

Some mistakes slow you down.

Some cost you money.

Some end companies.

The marketing mistake that ends companies is this: treating marketing like a solution to a velocity problem that isn't actually caused by marketing. You dump in budget trying to fix awareness when the real issue is pricing, placement, or product.

The brands that survive aren't necessarily the ones who spend the most on marketing. They're the ones who understand what each dollar is doing. They stay consistent in the channels where they can measure return, and they resist the temptation to buy growth they haven't earned.

Your marketing budget should be built around one question: what creates a measurable improvement in velocity at the shelf?

Everything else is either brand insurance or ego.

Those Manhattan billboards taught me that. We eventually learned from them. We channeled that energy into velocity-building programs, into demos at the exact stores where our velocity was strongest, into trade spend tied to performance thresholds. And we went from five percent of the cold-pressed juice category to over forty-five percent.

Not because we spent more. Because we stopped wasting what we had.

"Hope is not a strategy." Neither is marketing spend without a measurement framework behind it.

Know where every dollar is going. Know what it's doing. Run the numbers. That's not optional. That's the job.
