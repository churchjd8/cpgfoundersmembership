About two years ago I was coaching a founder who was absolutely convinced his sales team was crushing it.

Three hundred new store doors in six months. A VP of Sales who looked like a rockstar on paper. The pipeline was full. The board was pleased. He was starting to feel like maybe he'd figured out the retail game.

Then the reorders stopped coming.

He called me in a panic. I asked him one question before he even finished explaining: "Walk me through how you're paying your sales team."

He told me. Three percent commission on shipped cases, plus a quarterly bonus for hitting door count targets.

There was the problem. Right there.

His team hadn't been building a business. They'd been loading stores. Shipping big opening orders into accounts that weren't ready, on shelves that were too deep, with no in-store support, and no plan for what happens when the product just... sits there.

The commission plan had turned his salespeople into door-openers. Not market-builders. Not velocity drivers. Just people very good at getting product out of a warehouse and into a store that would eventually ask for a return.

This is the CPG sales compensation trap. And I've watched it sink more brands than I can count.

## Why CPG Is Different

Here's what gets founders in trouble. They come from tech, or they hire from Procter & Gamble, or they read a generic sales management book... and they build an incentive structure designed for some other business.

In SaaS, you pay on closed ARR. Makes sense -- once a customer signs, they're recurring. In traditional sales, you pay on revenue. Fine for most industries.

In CPG? Shipping cases is table stakes. It is not the achievement. The achievement is sell-through. The achievement is velocity. The achievement is getting a consumer to pick up your product, try it, love it, and come back.

Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains. I say this all the time, and I mean it. Getting into 500 stores doesn't mean anything if those stores aren't moving product. And if your comp plan is rewarding distribution without requiring velocity, you've built a machine designed to flood retailers with inventory and eventually get you delisted.

CPG is a "penny profit" business. The pennies matter. And a comp structure that optimizes for the wrong metric will bleed those pennies out every single quarter, quietly, until you've got a margin problem that looks like a sales problem.

## The Three Mistakes I See Over and Over

**Paying on shipments, not sell-through.** The moment you pay a commission purely on cases shipped to a retailer, your salespeople are motivated to get as much product in the door as possible. Big opening orders look great on their scorecard. It doesn't matter to their paycheck whether those stores sell through or ask for a credit. You've misaligned the incentive entirely.

**Door count bonuses without velocity floors.** Some founders try to fix the first problem by adding a velocity component... but then set the threshold so low it's meaningless. Or they make it a soft goal instead of a gating requirement. "Hit 200 new doors AND maintain 50th percentile velocity or better" is very different from "hit 200 new doors, and by the way we're tracking velocity separately."

**Uncapped commission structures in new distribution windows.** Opening orders at retail are inherently lumpy. A brand getting into a regional Kroger chain might ship 20,000 cases in one quarter and then nothing for the next two. If your commission is uncapped on that opening order, you've paid an enormous commission on a trial that might not convert to reorders. Structure matters -- opening order commission should be different from reorder commission. Opening orders are harder to get. But reorders are what build a business.

## What the Structure Should Actually Look Like

Here's the framework I give every founder before they make their first VP of Sales hire.

**Base salary: $80K-$120K.** For a VP-level hire in a region or a channel, this is the anchor. It needs to be real money -- enough that they're not desperate to chase every commission dollar -- but not so high that you've taken away the incentive to perform. Junior brokers and territory managers should sit lower, $55K-$75K base depending on market.

**Commission on net revenue from reorders: 2-4%.** Not on opening orders. On reorders. You can pay a flat 1% on the opening order as an acquisition fee. But the real commission kicks in when the product actually moves. This single structural change shifts behavior more than any quota setting or accountability process you'll ever implement.

**Velocity bonus: 10-15% of base, paid quarterly, gated by performance thresholds.** This bonus is only paid if the account is performing at or above the 50th percentile velocity for the category in that retailer. Below threshold, the bonus doesn't accrue. Period. This creates a hard incentive for salespeople to actually care what happens after the product hits the shelf.

**Door count is a multiplier, not a primary metric.** I've seen founders make door count the headline. It shouldn't be. Doors are a leading indicator. Velocity is the lagging indicator that actually matters. Set door targets as part of a territory plan, but tie the real comp dollars to what happens in those doors after they open.

## The Quota Question

Most early-stage CPG founders set quotas wrong. They take their revenue target, divide it by the number of salespeople, and call it a quota. That's not a quota. That's arithmetic.

A real CPG quota needs to account for channel mix (a Sam's Club or Costco account has completely different economics than a natural grocery set), territory maturity (you can't hold a salesperson opening new markets to the same velocity targets as someone managing an established account base), and seasonality.

When I was building the team at Suja, Nicky Vidaurreta -- who ran sales for us across four different companies over the years -- was relentless about territory-level quota discipline. He'd build account-by-account plans, velocity projections, and promotional calendars before the year ever started. That kind of rigor is how you avoid the end-of-quarter fire drill where your team is doing crazy deals to hit numbers that have nothing to do with whether the business is actually healthy.

"Hope is not a strategy." Certainly not a sales strategy.

## The Broker vs. Direct Hire Equation

There's a compensation question that comes before the commission question, actually. And that's whether to hire direct sales at all, or lean on a broker network.

Brokers don't cost you salary -- they cost you a commission, typically 3-6% of net revenue. For early-stage brands, that can be the right call. You get coverage and relationships without fixed overhead.

But brokers have their own incentive structure, and it doesn't always align with yours. They're managing dozens of brands. Your brand is one line item in their portfolio. If it's not moving, they move their energy somewhere else.

When you're ready to bring sales in-house, you're not just replacing broker commissions with salaries -- you're taking on the management overhead, the benefit costs, the training costs, and the expectation of institutional knowledge. "The Rule of Twos" applies: it'll take twice as long and cost twice as much to build a real in-house sales function as you think.

Do it when you can afford to do it right. Not when you're desperate.

## The Behavior Test

Here's the simplest way to evaluate whether your current comp structure is working.

Ask yourself: what would a perfectly rational salesperson do to maximize their income under this plan, every single day?

If the answer is: call on buyers, open new doors, make sure reorders are actually happening, track velocity, support in-store execution and demos, flag problems early... you've designed it right.

If the answer is: book as many opening orders as possible, push for the biggest initial SKU count at each account, and not worry too much about what happens six weeks later... you've got a problem. A big one.

The best sales cultures I've built reward the full cycle. Not just the acquisition. Not just the opening handshake. The whole arc from first pitch to reorder to relationship that survives a line review.

"You can market your way into trial, but you cannot market your way into loyalty." Same principle applies to your salespeople. You can't incentivize your way into great distribution. You can incentivize your way into great velocity.

That's the difference between a brand that gets on shelf... and a brand that stays there.
