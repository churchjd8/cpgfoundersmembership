I still remember sitting across from a Whole Foods buyer with a sell sheet I was genuinely proud of. We'd spent three weeks on it. Hired a design firm. Multiple rounds of revisions. Color-coded everything.

He glanced at it for maybe twenty seconds. Then he set it down and started asking questions.

I realized in that moment that I had completely misunderstood what a sell sheet is for.

It's not there to impress the buyer. It's there to earn the next thirty seconds of the conversation.

---

Here's what nobody tells early-stage CPG founders: most buyers see dozens of sell sheets a week. Hundreds at Expo West. The brands that get a follow-up meeting are almost never the ones with the prettiest page. They're the ones who made the business case fastest.

A sell sheet has one job: get the buyer to lean forward instead of lean back.

That's it. Every choice you make on that page... the layout, the data, the language, the SKU selection... should serve that single objective.

And if you don't nail it, you may never get the chance to say anything else.

---

## What actually needs to be on a CPG sell sheet

**1. The single sentence that explains your product**

Not your mission. Not your founding story. Not your values. One sentence that tells the buyer exactly what you make and who it's for, positioned against the existing shelf.

Something like: "Organic cold-pressed juice at $3.99, not $9.99, designed to bring better-for-you nutrition to everyday shoppers, not just premium health food stores."

Specific. Clear. Already tells the buyer why you belong in the set and at what price point.

If you can't get it into a sentence, your positioning isn't sharp enough yet.

**2. Pack configuration, size, shelf life, UPC**

Buyers think in planograms. They need to visualize where your product lives before they can evaluate whether it belongs there. Include your pack dimensions, your sell unit, your UPC, and your shelf life. If they have to ask for it, you've already introduced friction.

**3. Your velocity data, in context**

This is the most important line on the page.

Not your launch velocity from your best week at your best store. Your average velocity, measured against the category set. If you have SPINS or scan data, use it. "Our flagship 16-oz SKU averages $15 per store per week, versus a category set average of $9."

That sentence does three things at once: it tells the buyer you have data discipline, that you understand how to benchmark, and that your product is actually performing.

If you don't have scan data yet, use your demo results, your DTC repeat rate, your best verified store numbers. But never put a velocity claim on a sell sheet you can't defend under direct questioning. Buyers ask follow-ups. Hope is not a strategy.

**4. Your distribution infrastructure**

Are you authorized through UNFI? KeHE? A regional DSD network? Direct to store? And in what states?

A Kroger buyer in Dallas doesn't care about your UNFI authorization in the Pacific Northwest. Your distribution story needs to be relevant to the specific retailer in front of you. Know before you walk in the door what infrastructure you have that serves their stores.

**5. Marketing support that's specific**

Not "we're active on social media." Specific and credible.

"We have 60,000 email subscribers in the region. We're running $12,000 per month in targeted digital advertising in your top markets. We have 18 demos scheduled in Q1 in comparable formats."

That's marketing support. The rest is noise. Buyers want to know you can drive traffic to the shelf, because the shelf is not a warehouse. Every inch of space has to justify itself in velocity, and they need to believe you can help it earn its spot.

**6. Your pricing architecture**

MSRP. Wholesale. Landed cost to the retailer. Your promotional cadence (one TPR per quarter, for example). Gross margin at full price and at promoted price.

Buyers are doing margin math on you in real time. Make it easy for them. If they have to reverse-engineer your economics, you're already on the back foot.

**7. One or two social proof callouts**

A credible publication that's covered you. A major retailer you're already in. One notable voice who's endorsed the product.

Not five. Not a full press page. One or two. Credibility comes from specificity, not volume.

## The mistake most founders can't see they're making

We ran billboards in Manhattan during the early days of Suja when we had four Whole Foods stores in the entire city. Four stores. And we were buying outdoor advertising in one of the most expensive media markets in the world.

We created awareness with nowhere to convert it.

The sell sheet version of that mistake is loading your page with lifestyle photography and brand language... and burying your unit economics in a footnote. Buyers don't fall in love with packaging at the sell stage. They ask: is this going to sell? Does it make the category healthier? Can I trust the people behind it?

Everything on your sell sheet should be answering one of those three questions.

---

## The second most common mistake: too many SKUs

Founders want to show the full product line. More is more, right? The buyer sees complexity. Complexity looks like risk, especially from a brand they haven't worked with before.

Lead with your hero SKU. The one with the strongest velocity, the best margin, the clearest positioning. Prove that one first. You can expand the conversation to the rest of the line after you've established credibility with the first item.

Don't confuse distribution potential with distribution readiness. A buyer isn't buying your portfolio. They're buying a bet on one SKU that they believe can earn its own space.

---

## The page that earns the relationship

Jody Cnossen used to say something that stuck with me: "The truth of the business is at the shelf." He meant that no amount of pitch deck polish or data manipulation survives contact with actual consumer behavior. The shelf reveals everything.

But before you get to the shelf, the truth of your business is on that one page you hand across the table.

Make it specific. Make it fast. Make it about the buyer's question, not your story.

The sell sheet doesn't close the deal. But a bad sell sheet closes the door.

And a great one... it's just the beginning of a conversation that, if you're doing everything else right, might lead somewhere worth going.
