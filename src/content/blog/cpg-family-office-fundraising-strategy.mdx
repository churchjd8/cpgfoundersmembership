I was twenty-two minutes into a pitch when the investor stopped me.

"What's your exit timeline?" he asked.

I'd been talking about repeat purchase rates. About the category tailwind we were riding. About why the demographic we were targeting was willing to pay a premium and would keep paying it. Twenty-two minutes of signal. And he wanted to know when he was getting out.

That's not a bad investor. That's a misaligned one.

Over forty-four fundraising rounds across eight companies, I've taken money from every type of investor you can imagine: angels, institutional VCs, strategic partners, PE firms, Goldman Sachs. Each has a role. Each has a logic. But the category that most CPG founders completely underutilize — and that I'd argue is the best fit for most consumer brands in their $5M–$50M phase — is the family office.

Let me tell you what I've learned.

---

## The CPG Timing Problem

Here's the honest truth about building a consumer brand. It takes longer than anyone wants to admit.

The Rule of Twos applies everywhere: twice as long, twice as expensive as your original projections. I've seen it play out across eight companies. The first year you're getting your formula and manufacturing right. Year two you're finding distribution. Year three you're learning which channels actually build margin and which ones just build ego. By year four, if you've been disciplined, you're starting to see something real.

"Revenue without margin is ego." I've been saying that for thirty years. And it's just as true for capital structure. Raising money from investors who have a four-year fund life when you're in a business that takes seven years to mature is a recipe for misalignment.

A VC fund has LPs. Those LPs expect capital back on a schedule. That schedule has nothing to do with your brand's natural growth cycle. When the fund needs an exit and you're not ready, things get complicated. Boards get fractious. Pressure mounts to sell early, cut corners on growth, or take a deal that wasn't the right one.

At Suja, we had an investor — a PE firm — who came in less than twelve months before our Coca-Cola deal was supposed to close. They had contractual first-money-out preference. When Coke's check came in at $90 million, it would have largely bypassed the angels who'd built the company over three years. Those angels had been there from the beginning. Some of them had taken our calls at midnight. Some of them had written checks when we weren't sure we'd make payroll. And they almost got squeezed out by a late entrant's paperwork.

We fought for them. We found Goldman Sachs to put in $60 million to rebalance the structure. The angels sold roughly 40% of their holdings at 3.4x their cost... and held the rest. They eventually returned about 10x.

The point isn't just about loyalty. It's about alignment. Investors who've been with you from the beginning, who understood the journey, who weren't watching a fund clock — they acted differently when the moment came.

That's the family office.

## What a Family Office Actually Is

A family office is a private wealth management firm set up to manage the investments of a single ultra-high-net-worth family. Some are run by the founding patriarch or matriarch directly. Some have professional investment teams. Most have somewhere between $50M and $500M in investable assets, though the large multi-family offices can run into the billions.

The key difference from an institutional investor is structural. A family office has no LP to answer to. No fund lifecycle to manage. No quarterly returns presentation where your portfolio company is one of twelve line items competing for air time.

They can hold forever if they want. Or they can exit tomorrow. The only person they answer to is the family.

For CPG founders, this matters enormously. When you raise from a family office that genuinely believes in what you're building, you get something rare: a patient partner.

And "patience" in CPG isn't just a nice word. It's the difference between being forced to sell at Year 4 when you're just getting to scale... or holding until Year 7 or 8 when the multiples are real.

## What Family Offices Look for in CPG

I've been in enough of these rooms to know the questions. They're different from VC meetings. Not better or worse — different.

A VC starts with: market size, competitive moat, unit economics, path to $100M revenue. All valid. All important.

A family office starts with: *Do I trust this person? Do I believe in this product? Does this fit my family's values and interests?*

That's not a soft standard. It's actually a harder filter in some ways. They've watched plenty of polished pitches that went nowhere. What they're trying to figure out is whether you're the real thing.

A few things they consistently care about:

**1. Founder character.** Have you built something before? How did you handle adversity? What did you do when things went wrong? I've always been willing to talk about my failures as readily as my wins. The family office respects that. The hustle-porn crowd doesn't.

**2. Category conviction.** They want to believe in the space. Many family offices that invest in CPG do so because someone in the family loves the product, understands the category, or has a health/food/wellness thesis. Don't pitch the Tito's family on a wine brand without knowing their portfolio.

**3. Brand soul.** "Your company and your brand need to have a 'soul', otherwise you're just selling products." That's not marketing talk — it's what separates brands that endure from brands that fad out. Family offices, more than any other investor category I've worked with, can feel the difference.

**4. Path to profitability.** Not just top-line growth. "Gross margin determines destiny" — and a family office, which is protecting real family wealth, cares deeply about whether your unit economics eventually support a real business. Get to 45–50% gross margin before you walk into that room. Below that, the math of the investment gets harder to justify.

**5. Your cap table.** A family office doesn't want to invest alongside a mess. Clean structure, reasonable dilution per round (target 15–20% per round max), no landmine terms from prior investors. If your cap table is a disaster, clean it up before you raise again.

## How to Find Family Offices

This is where most founders give up too early. Family offices don't advertise. They're not listed in a database. They don't have booths at Expo West.

But they're there. And the CPG world is smaller than you think.

**Start with your existing investor network.** If you've raised from angels, ask each one who they know in the family office world. Angels and family offices often run in the same circles. One warm introduction is worth a hundred cold emails.

**Trade associations and industry groups.** The Consumer Brands Association, Specialty Food Association, NOSH events, the Fancy Food Show — these attract the kinds of investors who care about food and beverage. Walk the floor with a different eye. Look for the people who aren't selling anything. Some of them manage serious family capital.

**Your broker and distributor relationships.** UNFI, KeHE, and major regional brokers have seen thousands of brands. They know who's invested in the space before. They make introductions. Don't treat these as transactional relationships — they're a source of warm capital referrals if you've earned their respect.

**LinkedIn and founder communities.** Search "family office CPG" or "food and beverage investor" on LinkedIn. Look at who's in your industry slack groups, WhatsApp communities, and founders' networks. You'd be surprised who's quietly managing nine figures.

**Direct outreach through portfolio signals.** If a family office has invested in a brand adjacent to yours, they understand your business. That's a qualified prospect. Find the investment and trace it back to the investor. Ask your mutual contacts for an introduction.

## How to Pitch a Family Office

The pitch structure is different. Don't just repurpose your VC deck.

A VC deck is built to impress a committee. A family office pitch is built to build a relationship. That's a meaningful distinction.

**Tell your story first.** Why did you start this? What do you personally believe about this category? What do you see that others don't? Family offices back people they believe in. Give them a reason to believe in you as a human being before you show them a single number.

**Be specific about the product.** Have them taste it. Show them your packaging. Walk them through what you're putting in that bottle or bag and why it matters. I still believe, after all these years, that the best investor pitch I ever did was the one where I just handed someone the product and watched their face change.

**Walk through the business honestly.** Show them where you are, not just where you're going. The good and the hard. "Hope is not a strategy" — don't build your pitch around projections that require everything to go right. Show them you've stress-tested it. Show them the downside case. Show them you've already thought about what could go wrong.

**Know their check size sweet spot.** Most single-family offices write checks in the $500K–$3M range for early-stage CPG. Multi-family offices can go $5M–$15M. Don't ask for $10M from an investor who writes $1M checks — it's not a compliment, it's a mismatch. Know their range before you walk in.

**Give them a role, not just a check.** Family offices often want to be more than passive LPs. A board observer seat, an advisory relationship, a chance to leverage their network — these matter to them. Ask what they want from the relationship beyond financial return. It changes the conversation.

## Red Flags to Watch For

Not every family office is patient capital. Some behave exactly like the VCs I described earlier — same short time horizons, same pressure tactics, just wearing a different shirt.

A few things that should make you pause:

**They're primarily motivated by the return, not the brand.** You'll hear it in their questions. If every conversation is about exit multiple and entry valuation and nothing else, they're thinking like a fund even if they're not running one.

**They have no relevant network.** A good family office investor opens doors — to retail buyers, to other investors, to suppliers, to talent. If they can't name a single person who would be valuable to your business, ask yourself what you're actually getting beyond cash.

**They want more than 20% for early-stage money.** Some family offices misread their own leverage. Patient capital is valuable, but it shouldn't cost you twice what an angel would ask.

**They've never invested in CPG before.** This isn't disqualifying — every investor has a first. But if they have no reference points for how long it takes to build a shelf presence, you may spend more time educating them than running the company.

**They want control without earning it.** Veto rights, approval requirements for routine decisions, liquidation preferences that strip founders — read every term carefully. Being "family" doesn't mean "fair."

## The Honest Truth About Patient Capital

Over the past thirty years, I estimate that nearly half of my time has been spent raising money. That number still startles me when I say it.

I've raised from the best capital in the world and I've raised from the wrong capital at the wrong time. The check size matters. The valuation matters. But what matters most — and what I couldn't see clearly until I'd done forty-four of these — is the alignment between your investor's expectations and your brand's natural timeline.

CPG is a "Penny Profit" business. The pennies matter. And so does the calendar.

A family office that genuinely believes in what you're building, that has the liquidity to stay patient, that has a network that opens doors... that investor is worth more than twice the check from someone watching a fund clock.

"Gross margin determines destiny" — I've said it a thousand times. But so does the investor in your capital stack who decides when it's time to go.

Find the ones who'll let you build it right.
