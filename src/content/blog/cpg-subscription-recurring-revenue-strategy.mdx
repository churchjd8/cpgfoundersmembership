Monday mornings used to feel like opening a report card I hadn't studied for.

You pull the weekly replenishment numbers. Some retailers reordered heavy — a promo pop, maybe a display program hit. Others went quiet. A new account that promised big turns barely moved product. The numbers are all over the map, and you're sitting there trying to build a 13-week cash forecast out of data that changes by the day.

That's the retail CPG model in a nutshell. You earn distribution, then you fight to earn it again every week. The revenue is real. The velocity matters. But it's lumpy, it's unpredictable, and there's a ceiling on what you can control.

Then I started watching what happened to brands that layered in a subscription business alongside their retail footprint.

The ones doing it well weren't replacing retail. They were building a floor under it.

---

## The Fundamental Problem With Only Playing in Retail

Don't misunderstand me — retail is where CPG brands are built. If you don't have strong velocity at shelf, nothing else matters. I've said it a hundred times: shelf space is rented every week, and velocity is your rent payment.

But retail alone gives you a business where you don't know your customer.

Think about that for a second. You ship to a distributor. The distributor moves it to a retailer. A consumer picks it up at the checkout and scans it. You get a scan report with units moved, maybe some demographic overlay if you're lucky. But you don't have their email address. You can't reach them when you launch a new SKU. You don't know if they bought once and left, or if they've been buying every week for two years.

That anonymity is a liability. And it only compounds as you scale.

A subscription business flips that equation. The consumer becomes yours. You know exactly who they are, how often they buy, what they spend, when they're about to churn, and whether they're raving about you to friends. That data doesn't just improve your marketing — it improves your entire business.

---

## The Metric That Tells You If a Subscription Business Is Even Worth Building

Before you think about subscriber acquisition, you need to answer one question honestly: do consumers actually want to repurchase your product?

My benchmark: if your repeat purchase rate is under 20%, you don't have proof of concept. You have trial. Those are very different things. You can market your way into trial, but you cannot market your way into loyalty.

At Suja, we targeted a 40–50% repeat rate on our direct channels. Under 10% repeat on DTC? That's a signal to pause and talk to your consumers — figure out why they left before you spend another dollar trying to bring in new ones.

A subscription model accelerates repeat purchase. But it can't create loyalty that wasn't there. The product has to earn it first.

If your repeat rate is strong — if people are going out of their way to find you, stocking extras, telling friends — that's the foundation. Build on it.

## Subscription Economics 101: The Numbers That Actually Matter

Here's the thing about subscription that most CPG founders don't fully grasp until they've tried it: the unit economics look worse in month one and dramatically better by month six.

A one-time DTC customer might convert at $35 on a first order. After acquisition costs — Meta, Google, whatever channel brought them in — you might net $5–10 on that transaction. Painful.

A subscriber with an average order value of $45/month and a 15-month average lifetime generates $675 in revenue before they ever cancel. With the same acquisition cost amortized across 15 orders instead of one, the math looks completely different. The LTV-to-CAC ratio flips from marginal to meaningful.

The number most subscription brands obsess over is monthly churn. And rightfully so. At 5% monthly churn, the average subscriber stays about 20 months. At 15% monthly churn, they're gone in 7. That's not a linear difference in business outcomes — it's the difference between a subscription business that compounds and one that constantly refills a leaky bucket.

Rule of thumb I give founders: aim for monthly churn under 7%. Under 5% is excellent. If you're running above 10%, stop acquiring and fix the product or the experience first.

## The Channel Conflict Question (And Why Most Founders Overthink It)

The first objection I hear: "If I sell direct at full price, I'm undercutting my retail partners."

Here's how I actually think about it. Retail pricing is your anchor. Your DTC subscription should be priced at or slightly above your retail price per unit — with the value proposition being convenience, consistency, and exclusive access to new flavors or formats, not a discount.

The trap is launching a subscription that's meaningfully cheaper than retail. That creates conflict with your retail buyers, it trains your best customers to skip the shelf, and it erodes the brand equity you've worked to build. Promotions should create trial, not dependency.

What subscription can offer that retail can't: flexibility, personalization, and the first look at limited releases. Your best customers — the ones who've already proven they love the product — often want those things more than a 10% price break.

That said, a small introductory offer for first-time subscribers is fair. I've seen 15–20% off the first box work without cannibalizing retail. The key is rolling to full price by month two and building the value proposition around something other than the discount.

## What to Build, and When

Not every CPG brand is ready for a subscription program. Here's roughly how I think about the sequencing:

**Stage 1 — Prove the product:** Retail or DTC sales, strong repeat rates, customers seeking you out. If you're here, you have the foundation.

**Stage 2 — Build the DTC baseline:** Launch a simple subscribe-and-save option on your existing DTC store. Keep the offer tight — your hero SKU or a curated starter bundle. Don't overcomplicate it. Test churn, test reorder cadence (monthly vs. bi-monthly), and learn before you build.

**Stage 3 — Invest in the subscription experience:** Subscriber-only content, early access to new flavors, the ability to skip a month without canceling. These retention levers matter more than acquisition spending once you have 500+ active subscribers.

**Stage 4 — Use subscriber data to inform retail:** This is where it gets powerful. You know exactly what your most loyal customers want. That intelligence feeds your retail pitch — velocity data, flavor preferences, pack size insights. The subscription business starts improving your retail business.

Gross margin matters here too. Subscription economics work best at margins above 50%, because you're absorbing fulfillment costs directly. If your margins are still in the 30s, subscription is a harder game. Get the gross margin right first.

---

## The Mistake I See Most Often

Building a subscription program as a last resort.

I see founders who've struggled with retail velocity, burned through their marketing budget chasing new distribution, and then pivot to "maybe we should do subscriptions" as a rescue play. It almost never works in that order.

Subscription isn't a turnaround strategy. It's a growth multiplier for a product people already love. The sequence matters.

"Capital amplifies whatever foundation you've built." The same logic applies here. Subscriptions amplify loyalty. If the loyalty isn't there yet, you're just spending money to auto-ship a product consumers aren't excited about.

Do the retail work first. Prove the velocity. Confirm the repeat rate. Then build the subscription engine on top of something real.

---

## The One-Line Test

Before you commit resources to a subscription program, ask one question: *Do my best customers ever wish they could get more of this product more conveniently?*

If the answer is yes, you have a subscription business waiting to be built.

If the answer is "I'm not sure," go talk to your top customers first. The data will tell you everything. And the data always wins.

Dream boldly. Plan soberly. That applies here too.
