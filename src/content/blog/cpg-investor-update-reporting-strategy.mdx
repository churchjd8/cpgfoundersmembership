It was sometime in 2017. Suja had Coca-Cola on our cap table, $110 million raised over five years, and product in tens of thousands of stores. From the outside, it looked like we had it figured out.

We didn't.

Gross margins were sitting around 32 percent. We were burning cash at a pace that required near-constant focus. Some weeks we had less than $100,000 in the bank. The business was growing, but not profitably — and I knew it. Our investors knew it too, because I told them.

That last part is the point.

Every month, without fail, I sat down and wrote our investor update. Good months and bad ones. When the numbers were exciting, I wrote it. When they were uncomfortable, I wrote it faster. Because the moment you go quiet on your investors — the moment you decide the news is too bad to share — you've started a countdown clock you can't stop.

## The Update Is Not a PR Exercise

Most founders treat investor updates like a fundraising deck. They polish the wins. Soften the losses. Emphasize the narrative over the numbers. They send something beautiful when things are good and go dark when things get hard.

I've been on the receiving end of that as an investor. It doesn't feel like discretion. It feels like panic.

Here's the thing: your investors already know something is wrong when you go quiet. The absence of information is itself information. And it's the worst kind, because it leaves them filling in the blanks with their imagination — which is almost always worse than reality.

"Enthusiasm attracts. Desperation repels." I've said that for years. But there's a corollary: silence breeds suspicion. The founder who over-communicates during hard times is the founder who gets supported through them.

## What Every Monthly Update Should Cover

This isn't a template. It's a discipline. Here's what I've sent, and what I've seen work for the best founders I've worked with over thirty years:

**1. Revenue and gross margin versus plan**

Not just revenue. Gross margin. If you're a consumer goods business, gross margin is the number that tells the real story. I've seen founders hit their revenue target while silently watching their gross margin erode from 42 percent to 34 percent. That's not growth. That's a category-two hurricane in slow motion.

Always show the variance to your plan. Up or down. Explain the delta in one sentence. No editorializing needed — just the facts and what drove them.

**2. Cash position and burn rate**

This one sentence tells your investors whether they're going to get a call in three months. Give them the actual bank balance on the last day of the month and the trailing 90-day burn rate. Don't make them ask. The founder who makes investors ask for cash balance is the founder who loses their trust.

**3. Top three wins**

Be specific. "We got into Sprouts" is a win. "We got into Sprouts, 350 stores, launching in March, projected $180,000 in year-one revenue at our current ACV velocity" is a useful win. The difference matters.

**4. Top two or three challenges**

This is where most founders get squeamish. Don't be. If your largest SKU is underperforming at Kroger and you're at risk of a reset, say so. If your contract manufacturer just told you costs are going up 12 percent, say so. If you lost a key person, say so.

Here's the counterintuitive truth: sharing problems builds more confidence than hiding them. Your investors have seen things go sideways before. They've been through restructurings, pivot decisions, rough quarters. What they haven't survived — as investors — is being surprised. The surprise is what kills trust. The problem itself, almost always, is solvable.

**5. What you need**

Do you need an introduction? A channel recommendation? A potential board advisor? Tell them. Investors who are actively helpful are investors who feel connected to the business. That connection is worth something when you're raising your next round.

## Frequency and Consistency

Monthly. That's the cadence I recommend for founders who have raised institutional capital.

I know, I know. You're running a startup. You have three fires burning right now. The update feels like one more thing. Here's my answer to that: the update doesn't take that long when you're doing it every month. The discipline forces you to know your numbers cold. It forces you to be clear on what's working and what's not. The 90 minutes you spend writing the update is not time away from the business — it is work on the business.

Quarterly works for earlier-stage companies with angels and friends-and-family investors who are more patient. But the moment you have institutional investors on your cap table, go monthly. And never miss.

## The Trust Reserve

Over eleven fundraising rounds at Suja, I built something I call a trust reserve. It's not a thing you can put on a balance sheet, but it's real.

The trust reserve is built month by month, update by update, bad news shared by bad news shared. When you've been straight with someone for two years — when they've seen you name problems before they became crises and solve things you said you would solve — they extend you something invaluable: the benefit of the doubt.

That benefit of the doubt is what got us a $60 million commitment from Goldman Sachs when Coca-Cola passed on a full acquisition in 2015. We walked into that conversation with years of honest communication behind us. Goldman knew exactly who they were dealing with. We hadn't managed our image with them. We'd managed the truth.

"I have never successfully sold or financed a company from a position of weakness." That's true. But it's also true that I've never successfully raised capital from investors I'd been less than straight with. The trust reserve is what determines whether you can raise from weakness when you have to.

## What to Never Do

Don't go dark. Even if the news is bad, send something short. "Rough quarter, here's what happened, here's what we're doing about it. More next month." That's enough to maintain trust. Silence is not.

Don't spin. Investors can smell it. The founder who calls a velocity miss "a strategic pause to optimize the channel mix" is the founder who gets a call from their board member asking what's actually going on.

Don't send it late. If you said monthly and you send it in month six of a quarterly cadence, you've trained your investors to wonder what you're hiding. Consistency is its own signal.

Don't conflate activity with progress. "We attended Expo West, met with 40 buyers, had amazing conversations" is not a business update. It's a highlight reel. What decisions were made? What pipeline was opened? What committed?

## The Update That Builds the Raise

Here's the practical application of all of this: the best fundraising process is one that starts eighteen months before you need the money. And the way you start it is with consistent, honest monthly investor updates.

By the time you go out to raise your next round, your investors have twelve or eighteen monthly snapshots of your business. They've watched you manage through challenges. They've seen your numbers trend. They know your team by name. They've had the chance to watch you do what you said you would do.

That's not a fundraising deck. That's a track record. And track records are what actually close rounds.

Gross margin determines destiny. Cash determines survival. And investor trust determines whether you get to raise the capital you need to get to both.

Don't skip the update.
