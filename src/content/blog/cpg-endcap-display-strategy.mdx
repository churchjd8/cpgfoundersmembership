Sometime around 2015, our Target buyer called with news I'd been waiting months to hear.

Suja had been invited into Target's Made to Matter program... a curated set of emerging health and wellness brands that got end cap placement, increased visibility, and real marketing support from one of the country's most beloved retailers. Front of the aisle. Eye level. The whole thing.

I remember walking those Target stores, watching customers stop at our endcap display, pick up a bottle, turn it over, read the label. For a lot of them, it was their first time encountering Suja. Not because we weren't on shelf — we were. Because we were buried on shelf. Four feet down. Third row from the top. Right next to twelve other cold-pressed brands fighting for the same eyeballs.

That's the thing nobody tells you when you're celebrating your distribution win.

Getting a slot on the planogram is half the battle. Maybe less.

**The shelf is where you survive. The endcap is where you grow.**

---

## What an Endcap Actually Does

In basic terms, an endcap is secondary placement. Product placement beyond your normal shelf location. The end of an aisle. A freestanding display unit near the front of the store. A clip strip hanging off a neighboring product's shelf. A shipper that drops near checkout or in a high-traffic zone.

What they all share: they interrupt shoppers who weren't looking for your product.

That's the whole point. Your primary shelf location serves the consumer who already knows they want your category. Maybe even your brand. Secondary placement targets everyone else... the shopper who wasn't planning to buy a $4 wellness shot today, but walked past the right display at the right moment and something caught their eye.

"You can market your way into trial, but you cannot market your way into loyalty."

That's the exact equation an endcap is designed to solve. It's a trial-generation machine. It buys you the first sale. Everything after that belongs to the product.

## The Math Most Brands Get Wrong

Here's where I've watched founders spend money they can't get back.

They get an endcap. Maybe $500 to $5,000 per store per promotional window, depending on the retailer and duration. They pay it. Product goes on the endcap. Sales bump for a few weeks. Endcap ends. Sales drop back to where they were before... or lower, because now consumers have been trained to wait for a deal.

That's not a secondary placement problem. That's a strategy problem.

The question you need to answer before you commit to a single endcap dollar: what am I actually trying to accomplish?

If the answer is "generate trial among new shoppers in a set where my base velocity is already solid" — that's a legitimate use case. You're buying awareness from foot traffic you couldn't otherwise reach, and you have the product performance to convert that trial into repeat.

If the answer is "boost my velocity numbers before a line review" — you better understand what happens after the boost disappears. Buyers read data. They've seen the temporary lift trick a thousand times. If the underlying velocity doesn't sustain post-promotion, you haven't helped yourself. You may have hurt yourself.

"Promotions should create trial, not dependency."

I've watched brands spend $30,000 on a national endcap program and come out the other side with a lower velocity trend... because the promotional mechanics trained their consumer to buy only when the product was on discount. "Revenue without margin is ego" applies to promotional programs too. Revenue you can't sustain isn't a win.

## What Retailers Actually Want

Every major retailer has an endcap economy. Whole Foods has feature programs. Target has Made to Matter and seasonal destination sets. Costco has its rotation model near the entrance and back of warehouse. Walmart has a structured promotional vehicle framework that costs real money to access.

The buyers I've worked with most closely want three things when they give you secondary placement.

**First: volume that justifies the real estate.** They're giving up space that could serve another brand. At Costco, the bar is roughly $1,000 per club per week. At most grocery chains it varies by category, but you're expected to significantly outperform your normal shelf velocity during the feature window. If you don't, you haven't just missed an opportunity... you've signaled something to the buyer about your product's ceiling.

**Second: brand investment, not just price.** The best secondary placements combine a compelling price point with real storytelling at the fixture. Just because you're on an endcap doesn't mean the shopper knows why they should care. Your visual, your headline, your price point have to earn the sale in about three seconds. If your display looks like a last-minute Staples print job, that's what the shopper sees.

**Third: a track record of execution.** Buyers remember who fills endcaps on time, who delivers materials early, who follows through. It's a trust signal. When a brand drops the ball on one program, it becomes a two-or-three-program hole to dig out of. When a brand executes cleanly every time, the buyer starts looking for reasons to give them more opportunities.

The team at Suja internalized this early. If a SKU underperformed, we didn't wait for the buyer to notice — we brought solutions. "The goal was simple: help the retailer win." That approach is what got us to Whole Foods Supplier of the Year. Not our marketing. Not our growth rate. The consistency and the partnership mentality.

## When to Say Yes and When to Say No

Not every endcap is worth taking. I've said no to secondary placement opportunities, and I don't regret any of them.

**The math doesn't work at the required price point.** Some programs require a promotional discount deep enough that you're generating trial at a gross margin that doesn't make sense for your stage. Know your numbers. "Gross margin determines destiny" — and if the endcap requires another 15 points of promotional discount on top of trade spend you're already absorbing, you're essentially funding a retailer's marketing budget with equity you can't get back.

**Your base velocity isn't established.** There's a principle I come back to constantly: "Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains." An endcap at a retailer where your base velocity is weak doesn't fix the problem. It temporarily masks it. Fix the foundation first. If you're below the 50th percentile in your set at a retailer, more placement isn't the answer. Better product-market fit, better pricing, better fixture execution... that's the answer.

**You can't support the volume.** This surprises founders more than it should. Endcaps at major grocery or club stores represent real volume surges. If you're supply-constrained or if your co-manufacturer can't surge production, running out of stock mid-feature is one of the worst signals you can send a buyer. You've created demand you couldn't service. That relationship doesn't just reset to neutral.

## How to Actually Negotiate Secondary Placement

The approach that worked for Suja: bring data, bring a story, and lead with the retailer's interest.

When we walked into feature conversations, we came with data showing that when Suja had secondary placement in comparable markets, category lift was X percent. Not just Suja lift. Category lift. Buyers care about the category. If your endcap grows overall category sales rather than just relocating your own volume, you have a compelling case they can take to their own leadership.

We also came with a clear creative brief. Messaging, promotional price, fixture design — all ready to evaluate. Buyers don't want to imagine what the endcap will look like. They want to see it. When we showed up with a finished concept, we were one meeting further into the process than the brands who showed up with a conversation.

And we were honest about what we needed in return. Some programs we absorbed fully because we believed in the trial-generation value. Others we structured as exchanges — secondary placement in one area for expanded distribution of a different SKU elsewhere. Buyers have programming needs and relationships with multiple vendors. There's almost always a way to build a deal that works for both sides if you approach it like a partner, not a supplicant.

"Retailers rarely become long-term partners because of one great product. They become long-term partners because they trust the people behind it."

The endcap conversation is a trust-building exercise. Win it once cleanly and you'll get more chances.

## A Framework Before You Spend

Before committing to any secondary placement program, run through these:

**What's my base velocity at this retailer?** Secondary placement built on weak base velocity is a bet, not an investment. Be above 50th percentile in your set before you add promotional complexity.

**What incremental lift am I modeling?** Feature programs in CPG typically drive 30 to 100 percent velocity lifts during the window, depending on price reduction, fixture quality, and category. Model a range. Stress test the low end.

**What's my all-in cost per incremental unit?** Add reduced margin, placement fees, materials, and labor. Divide by estimated incremental units. This is your cost of trial acquisition. Compare it to your other trial channels.

**What's my repeat rate?** If your repeat purchase rate is below 20 percent, generating trial at premium cost is burning money. Fix repeat first.

**What happens after the endcap ends?** Have a retention plan for new buyers. The endcap earns you the introduction. A loyalty offer, a digital follow-up, a sample at purchase... that's what turns a one-time trial into a second sale.

I still think about those first Target Made to Matter displays. Customers who picked up Suja that day had no idea where we'd come from or what we'd built. They just knew the product looked interesting, the price was right, and someone trusted it enough to feature it prominently.

Three seconds of attention. That's all you get.

"Sometimes moving six inches on a shelf can have a bigger impact than spending thousands on advertising."

The endcap is your six inches. The shopper is already there. They're already in motion. You don't have to manufacture the moment — you just have to be ready for it.

Use that moment wisely. Build the ROI case before you spend a dollar. Know whether you're buying trial or just buying a temporary velocity spike. And treat every feature program as a chance to deepen a relationship with a buyer who has dozens of other brands competing for the same real estate.

That's the endcap edge. Not the placement itself. The discipline behind it.
