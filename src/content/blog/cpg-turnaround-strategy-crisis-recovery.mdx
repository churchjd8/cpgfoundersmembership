July 3rd, 2018. My house was full of family getting ready for the Fourth of July. My boys were running around outside. Linda was in the kitchen. The whole thing felt like a normal holiday weekend.

Then my phone rang.

It was Scott Uzell from Coca-Cola. In less than five minutes, he told me that Coke would not be moving forward with the acquisition of the remaining portion of Suja.

Six weeks earlier, Coke's management and HR teams had visited our facility in San Diego. We talked about integration. We talked about what the combined company would look like. I had mentally moved on to the next chapter. We had basically won.

I walked downstairs after that call and wept in front of my boys.

---

Here's what our numbers looked like at that moment: $40 million in secured debt coming due in October 2018. Burning more than $10 million per year. Gross margins sitting below 32%. And at various points over the next several months, we were operating with less than $100,000 in the bank.

On a $100 million revenue company.

That's not a cash flow problem. That's a crisis.

And here's what nobody tells you about a crisis like that: you cannot grow your way out of it. Growth was the thing that got us there. We had been so focused on the top line, on the velocity, on the doors, on the headline revenue number, that we let the foundation crack underneath us.

Revenue without margin is ego.

I had been an optimistic founder. The kind who thinks the next retailer, the next round, the next strategic partner will fix it. That optimism is an asset when you're building. It becomes a liability when you're bleeding.

---

## The First Move: Stop Rationalizing

The hardest thing I had to do was be brutally honest about what was actually happening. Not what I hoped would happen. Not what the model projected six months from now. What was actually happening today.

We were carrying outsourced kombucha at roughly 12% gross margin. Twelve percent. We had manual assemblies that were hemorrhaging labor hours. We had marketing spend that felt essential, that the team believed in, that I had signed off on, and that probably wasn't doing what we thought it was doing.

Hope is not a forecasting model.

Every founder in crisis mode wants to believe the answer is out there somewhere. A bigger strategic partnership. A new retailer. A bridge to the next round. Sometimes the answer is much quieter. It's a conference room, a spreadsheet, and three or four very hard conversations about what you're going to stop doing.

---

## The Second Move: Kill the Low-Margin SKUs Without Mercy

This sounds obvious. It is not obvious when you're staring at a SKU that's generating revenue, that a buyer you respect actually likes, that your team worked nights to build. You want to save it. You think you can fix it.

Kill it.

Suja's kombucha was technically excellent. We had actually solved the fermentation consistency challenge that trips up most kombucha brands. The product itself was good. But outsourced at 12% gross margin, it was a slow leak in the hull. And consumers didn't see Suja as a kombucha brand anyway. That was the signal we kept missing.

So we killed it.

Meanwhile, our wellness shots, produced internally at our own facilities, were generating gross margin closer to 60%. We had entered that category as the third player, behind VIVE and KOR. Within a year, we captured more than 30% market share.

That product-mix shift, kombucha out, shots in, moved our company-wide gross margin from roughly 32% to roughly 40%.

Think about what that means. Not a pricing change. Not a new distribution partner. Not a fundraising round. A product-mix decision.

Gross margin determines destiny. I say that constantly and I mean it literally. The margin improvement that came from killing one underperforming product category did more for Suja than almost anything we could have done on the revenue side.

---

## The Third Move: Cut the Spend That Isn't Building Loyalty

In 2019, we cut our marketing budget by more than $4 million.

Revenue grew by about 10%.

Read that again.

We spent significantly less, and the business grew. Stronger. That result shouldn't have surprised me as much as it did. What it actually taught me was this: we had been spending a lot of money to acquire customers who weren't staying. The marketing wasn't building loyalty. It was buying trial, over and over, from people who didn't come back.

You can market your way into trial. You cannot market your way into loyalty.

If your marketing budget disappeared tomorrow and your velocity disappeared with it, that is a signal. Your brand hasn't earned a reason to return. No amount of promotional spend fixes that. The fix is at the product level, the experience level, the repeat-purchase mechanism. That's where the real work is.

Strong brands with genuinely loyal customers don't need massive spend to keep growing. Cutting that $4 million exposed what was real in our business, and what was noise.

---

## Living Inside the Cash

My CFO Todd Fisher and I spent months living inside weekly cash projections. Not monthly. Weekly. We knew exactly how much money was in the bank on any given Monday, what was coming in from retail partners, what was going out to suppliers, and where our exposure sat.

When you're operating with less than $100,000 in the bank on a $100 million revenue company, you cannot afford surprises. You don't get to be vague about cash. Not weekly, not even daily.

A lot of founders delegate cash management entirely when things are going well. Especially founders who are big-picture operators, growth-oriented, creative. They hand it off to the CFO and stop looking until something goes wrong. And then when something goes wrong, they're flying blind in a storm.

Know your 13-week cash runway at all times. Know when your liquidity events are. Know what your weekly net cash position looks like. If you can't answer those questions off the top of your head, you're not managing your business, you're hoping for the best.

Hope is not a strategy.

---

## What Came Out the Other Side

2018: Suja lost roughly $10 million on an EBITDA basis. We were burning cash, carrying heavy debt, and had just been left at the altar by the biggest beverage company in the world.

2019: We generated positive $3 million EBITDA. Revenue grew. Marketing budget shrank. Product mix improved. For the first time in Suja's history, the company was profitable.

That swing, from -$10M to +$3M in a single year, didn't come from a new investor or a new channel or a new product launch. It came from discipline. From honesty. From being willing to stop doing things that weren't working.

By May 2019, we had turned the corner. We hosted management presentations with more than 15 potential buyers. None made an offer. That was fine. We kept going.

In 2020, Suja sold to Paine Schwartz Partners for approximately $300 million. Today the company generates more than $300 million in annual revenue with roughly 20% EBITDA margins.

None of that happens without July 3rd, 2018.

---

"Coca-Cola's decision felt devastating at the time. In hindsight, it was a gift. It forced discipline. It forced rigor. It forced maturity."

I believe that completely. Getting left at the altar was the event that forced us to confront every weakness we had been rationalizing. Every outsourced product with terrible margin. Every dollar of promotional spend that was buying noise instead of loyalty. Every assumption we had built into our model that was more hope than reality.

The companies that survive a real crisis don't survive because they got lucky. They survive because someone had the discipline to stop growing for a minute, look honestly at what they had actually built, and fix what was broken.

Most CPG founders in trouble want to raise their way out of it. New investor. New strategic partner. New round. Sometimes you don't need more capital. You need more discipline.

Dream boldly. Plan soberly.
