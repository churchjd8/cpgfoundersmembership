There's a call you don't expect.

Your team is deep in a conversation about a Kroger reset. Someone is arguing about whether you need more facings in the juice section. And somebody walks in with a message: a procurement manager from a national airline wants to talk about adding your product to their in-flight service.

First instinct? File it away. Retail is the plan. Retail is where the volume is, where the brand gets built, where the real work happens.

Here's what 30 years and 8 companies have taught me: that instinct is often wrong.

Foodservice isn't a distraction from your retail strategy. At the right moment, with the right product, it's one of the most powerful trial-driving and volume-building channels available to a CPG founder. And most founders either fall into it by accident... or they miss it entirely.

Let me tell you what I know.

---

## What We're Actually Talking About

When I say "foodservice," I mean everything that isn't a retail shelf:

- Airlines, hotels, and cruise lines (travel foodservice)
- Corporate dining (the cafeteria at Google, Goldman Sachs, your local hospital system)
- University dining programs (30,000 students, three meals a day, nine months a year)
- Healthcare systems (hospitals, senior living, rehab facilities)
- Fast casual and QSR chains (Panera, Sweetgreen, Shake Shack)
- Casual dining restaurants
- C-store foodservice (the prepared food and grab-and-go section at Wawa, Sheetz, or Buc-ee's)
- Stadium and arena concessions

These are very different buyers, very different economics, very different relationships. But they share one critical trait: they put your product in front of a consumer who did not actively choose it off a shelf.

That's the whole game.

---

## The Real Value: Trial in High-Attention Moments

You can market your way into trial. But you cannot market your way into loyalty.

I've said that a hundred times, because I believe it. Repeat purchase is what builds a brand. But trial still has to happen first. And trial is expensive.

Demo programs at natural grocery run $30-80 per hour in labor. Digital ads cost $2-5 per click and convert at 2-3%. Influencer content gets views but rarely closes a first purchase.

Foodservice changes the math entirely.

A university dining operator puts your juice on the lunch line. Five thousand students drink it a day. No ad spend. No slotting fee. No endcap negotiation. The product speaks for itself in a moment when the consumer is engaged, not scrolling past another sponsored post.

And here's the thing that doesn't show up in your marketing dashboard: when consumers encounter your brand in a foodservice setting they trust (an airline they fly regularly, a corporate cafeteria they visit every day, a campus dining hall they've eaten at for three years), something happens in how they perceive the brand at retail.

The "oh, I've had this before" effect. The brand shortcut. The trust transfer.

You can't always see it cleanly in syndicated data. But you can feel it in your repeat purchase numbers in markets where institutional exposure is high.

## How the Economics Actually Work

Here's where founders get tripped up, and I want to be direct about this because gross margin determines destiny.

Foodservice margins are structurally lower than retail. If your retail price is $4.99 and your gross margin is 45%, don't expect to walk into foodservice with the same economics.

The typical flow looks like this: your product sells to Sysco or US Foods (the distributors who run the foodservice world) at roughly 20-30% below your retail-equivalent price. Sysco or US Foods marks it up to their operator customer. The operator may or may not pass that cost to the consumer.

So your net realized price in foodservice is often 60-70 cents on the dollar versus retail. Gross margins can drop 10-15 points.

CPG is a "Penny Profit" business. The pennies matter. So why accept that?

Because the cost to acquire trial in foodservice is effectively zero. You're not buying attention. You're selling product into a channel where the operator is running your sampling program for you... and paying you to do it.

The math changes when you factor in customer acquisition cost, not just gross margin per unit. A unit sold through a corporate dining account that converts to a repeat retail buyer is worth dramatically more than its foodservice margin suggests.

That said: never let foodservice become a margin drain that silently poisons your P&L. Revenue without margin is ego. Foodservice has to be a strategic tool, not a volume chase with bad economics. Know your floor before you set a price.

## Sysco and US Foods: A Different World Than UNFI and KeHE

If you've spent time in natural and specialty grocery distribution (UNFI, KeHE, Presence Marketing), foodservice distribution will feel like a foreign country. Different language, different buyers, different performance metrics.

Sysco and US Foods together move roughly 60% of U.S. foodservice volume. They are enormous, complex, and not built for emerging brands. Getting listed at Sysco doesn't mean anything until a local distribution center actually stocks your product and a sales rep recommends it to their operator accounts.

The smart entry point is regional. One Sysco region in the Southeast, one US Foods division in the Midwest. Prove sell-through. Build the relationship. Expand from there.

The smarter entry point is through operators first. Find a corporate dining company, a regional restaurant group, a university system that wants your product. Get them to "spec" it (meaning they write it into their purchasing contract with Sysco or US Foods). Institutional demand pulls product through distribution. That's the cleaner path.

There are also regional foodservice distributors, specialty natural distributors with foodservice divisions (UNFI actually has a foodservice arm), and direct-to-operator relationships for smaller independent accounts. For early-stage brands, those smaller accounts are where you start. Get a few local restaurants or a regional university system. Prove the model before you try to go national through Sysco.

## What Foodservice Buyers Actually Care About

Retail buyers care about velocity, category performance, and TPR compliance. Foodservice operators care about something different.

**Consistency.** Can you deliver the same product, every order, on time, to spec? One bad delivery hurts a foodservice operator in ways that a retail stockout doesn't. They have a menu. They have a contract. They have a customer expecting what's on the board.

**Shelf life.** Most foodservice operators won't touch refrigerated products with less than 60-90 days of remaining shelf life when it arrives. If you're in fresh or refrigerated, this is often the gate that keeps you out. At Suja, our shelf life grew from 24 days at launch to approximately 100 days over time. That evolution opened distribution options that were previously impossible. If you're not there yet, foodservice is probably not the priority.

**Case economics.** Foodservice pricing is case-based, not unit-based. Know your case pricing, your case weight, and your delivered cost to the operator before you walk into the meeting. Operators making high-volume purchasing decisions are not thinking in units. They are thinking in cases, pallets, and weekly delivery volume.

**Spec compliance.** If an operator's purchasing spec requires your product in a specific size or format, you deliver that exact format. If you need to make a change (packaging redesign, size shift, formula adjustment), you communicate it 90-120 days in advance. Foodservice relationships die fast when brands surprise their operators.

The mistake most emerging brands make is treating foodservice buyers like retail buyers. They're not. The brand story matters less. The operator's operation matters more. Go in understanding that.

## When NOT to Pursue Foodservice

Let me be direct, because the wrong move here will cost you.

**If your shelf life is under 45 days**, foodservice is brutally difficult. Operators can't risk product arriving with 10 days of shelf life. The supply chain absorbs too much transit time.

**If your gross margins are already below 35%**, don't add 10-15 points of compression from foodservice pricing. Fix your retail economics first. Then revisit this channel.

**If your production capacity is inconsistent**, don't start the conversation. A foodservice operator who specs your product and then can't get consistent supply is a relationship you will never recover. In retail, a stockout is a missed sale. In foodservice, it's a menu failure.

**If you're still in proof-of-concept mode**, stay focused. Foodservice adds operational complexity, a new distribution relationship, new pricing tiers, and new buyer management. An early-stage brand with limited bandwidth doesn't need that distraction. Get retail velocity right. Prove the brand. Then call the airline.

Hope is not a strategy. And neither is a great foodservice relationship built on a product that isn't ready for it.

## How to Start (Practically)

Start with operators, not distributors. Identify a corporate dining company in your city, a regional restaurant concept, a university dining program. Get a meeting. Understand what they need. Get them interested in speccing your product before you go anywhere near Sysco.

Attend the National Restaurant Association Show in Chicago (May). That's where foodservice operators and distributors actually make buying decisions. If you're targeting university dining specifically, the NACUFS Conference is worth attending.

Price deliberately. Don't give away margin to land a logo. Build a case pricing model before the first meeting. Know your contribution margin at every pricing tier. Know your floor.

Track the brand-building impact. Set up a loose test: compare your retail velocity trajectory in markets where you have meaningful foodservice presence versus markets where you don't. Over 12-18 months, you should start to see a signal.

And be patient. Foodservice moves on a different clock than retail. There are no planogram resets. If a university dining program specs your product, you might be on that menu for five years without a competitive review. That kind of durability doesn't exist in most retail environments.

---

Most CPG founders spend years fighting for every facing on every retail shelf. And that fight is real and necessary.

But the brands that figure out how to own multiple channels at once... how to show up in the places consumers encounter products they didn't go looking for... those brands build something harder to displace.

Retail gives you the shelf. Foodservice gives you the memory.

Done right, it's not a distraction. It's a trial engine, a volume driver, and a brand legitimizer that compounds over time.

Dream boldly. Plan soberly. And don't dismiss this channel before you've actually looked at the math.
