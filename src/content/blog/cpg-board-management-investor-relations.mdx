Here's the story. Pre-board dinner, about 14 of us around the table at a nice restaurant. We were doing well — revenue climbing, brand momentum building, big plans on the horizon. Cocktails, good energy.

And then a board member looked at me across the table and said: "Jeff, don't you see that what you think is disruptive innovation is just causing unnecessary churn?"

The room went quiet. Every set of eyes shifted.

I took a breath. Answered as calmly as I could. "We would prefer to have a single hero product like Red Bull or Coca-Cola instead of continuously innovating." I meant it as both a concession and a reframe. The truth was more complicated than either of us made it sound.

But here's what that moment crystallized for me: your board is not your cheerleading section. They're measuring you. Every meeting, every update, every number you put in front of them — they're building a picture. And when that picture starts looking blurry, they lean in harder.

## The Rule Nobody Tells You

"Boards support strategy while performance supports confidence. When performance weakens, control increases."

Read that again.

It took me about 30 years and eight companies to fully internalize that sentence. When things are going well, your board gives you room. They trust your instincts, they back your big swings, they talk you up at cocktail parties with their LPs.

When things start sliding? That dynamic flips. Quietly. Suddenly there are more questions. Longer pre-reads requested. Advisors introduced — "just to help." The autonomy you thought you had gets tighter.

That's not a character indictment of any particular investor. That's just how boards work. They're fiduciaries. They have obligations to their own LPs. When you own a piece of something, you need to understand what's happening inside it.

The founders who navigate board dynamics well are the ones who understand this before they're deep inside it — not after.

## What Happened in July 2018

On July 3rd, 2018 — my house full of family, Fourth of July preparations underway, kids everywhere — I got a phone call from Coca-Cola.

They were not moving forward with the full acquisition.

Six weeks earlier, their management and HR teams had visited to talk about integration. We'd been building toward this for years. After the call, I walked downstairs and wept in front of my boys.

And then I had to figure out how to tell the board.

We had $40 million in secured debt coming due in October. We were burning more than $10 million a year. Gross margins were below 32%. At certain moments, we were operating with less than $100,000 in the bank on a $100 million revenue company. Todd Fisher (our CFO) and I lived inside weekly cash projections. I was on calls with lawyers and investors around the clock.

That's what it looks like when your board shifts from supportive to intensely, nervously engaged. Every conversation carries more weight. Every slide in the deck gets scrutinized harder.

And the thing that saved us wasn't a brilliant strategic pivot or a white-knight investor appearing out of nowhere. It was discipline. And it was transparency.

## What Actually Works

Here's what I learned about managing a board — through crisis, and honestly, at all stages:

**Show up with bad news first.** The worst thing you can do is let your board discover a problem through the numbers before you've framed it for them directly. Get ahead of it. Call your lead investor before the board packet goes out. "Here's what's happening. Here's what I think caused it. Here's the plan." They want to know you see it clearly. The number itself is almost never the problem. The surprise is.

**Separate strategy from performance.** When your numbers are down, your board will be tempted to question everything — your positioning, your channel strategy, your team. Those are different conversations. Don't let a bad quarter become a strategic re-evaluation unless you're the one proposing it. Control the narrative by being precise about what is a performance issue versus what is a strategy issue. One is usually fixable. The other is a harder conversation.

**Know what they actually track.** Most board members have three or four metrics they return to in every meeting. Gross margin. Velocity per store. Cash burn. Repeat rate. Find out what your lead investor watches most closely and make sure you have a tight, honest story around those numbers every single time. Generic updates feel evasive even when they're not.

**Build your internal champion.** When Coca-Cola was evaluating Suja, Mike Saint John — then President of Minute Maid — became our champion inside the building. Without someone willing to take reputational risk on your behalf, deals and relationships die quietly. The same thing applies with your own cap table. Who inside your investor group actually advocates for you in their own firm's meetings? Identify that person. Earn their trust. Invest in that relationship way before you need it.

**Don't confuse engagement with a vote of no-confidence.** A board member who's asking hard questions isn't necessarily against you. That's sometimes just what good governance looks like. I've had directors challenge me sharply in the room and then send a follow-up note saying they respected how I handled it. Engage the questions head-on. Don't deflect. Don't get defensive. Treat it like a test you can pass.

## The Numbers That Rebuilt Trust

After Coke's rejection, we made a series of unpopular decisions. We cut marketing spend by more than $4 million. We eliminated low-margin outsourced products — kombucha was generating about 12% gross margin while our internally produced wellness shots were running close to 60%. We killed inefficient manual assemblies. We launched a $4 million raw material cost initiative. We reduced operating expenses by over $3 million.

Revenue grew about 10% in 2019.

That taught me something I didn't expect. Strong brands with genuinely loyal customers don't always need to outspend the market to grow. But what those cuts also did was give the board something concrete to point to. Not just chaos and crisis — but a clear-eyed response to both. Discipline as the answer.

When we swapped kombucha for shots, company-wide gross margin moved from roughly 32% to roughly 40%. That's not a small thing. That shift alone changed what was possible for the business. "Gross margin determines destiny." I believe that down to my bones.

"Hope is not a strategy." That's especially true in a board meeting.

## The Real Point

You don't earn investor trust during the good years. Not really. Anyone can look good on a rising tide.

You earn it by how you behave when things go sideways. By how quickly you surface problems. By how honestly you diagnose them. By whether your recovery plan is grounded in real data or just optimism wearing the costume of strategy.

"I have never successfully sold or financed a company from a position of weakness."

That's still true. But I've also learned you can transform a position of weakness into one of earned credibility if you're transparent, rigorous, and relentless. Your board will follow you through hard seasons if they believe you see the terrain clearly and aren't hiding from what it's telling you.

Show up. Tell the truth. Have a plan. Execute it.

That's the job.
