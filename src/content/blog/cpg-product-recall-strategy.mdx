The call came on a Thursday afternoon.

We were deep in due diligence with Coca-Cola. Their team was in our offices. The $300 million valuation was on the table and we were weeks away from closing. And someone on our team flagged a potential quality issue.

Not a recall. Not a public problem. Just a concern that hadn't been fully resolved.

I had a choice.

Let it ride. Finish the deal. Handle it afterward. The lawyers probably could have buried it in the disclosure schedules.

I paused the deal.

"There is no wrong time to do the right thing."

I told Scott Uzzell, the President of Coca-Cola VEB, that we'd identified a potential issue and needed to resolve it before moving forward. The look on his face in that moment... I'll never forget it. Not disappointment. Something closer to respect.

What I earned that day was worth more than a few weeks of delay. We earned the trust of the world's largest beverage company at the exact moment they were deciding whether to hand us a nine-figure investment.

Integrity under pressure is visible. And in CPG, where consumers are literally ingesting what you make... that integrity isn't optional.

But here's the thing. Pausing a deal is the easy version of a quality crisis.

A full product recall is a different level of pain entirely.

---

**Why recalls are different from everything else**

Most CPG crises are recoverable. A bad line review. A distributor walkout. A co-man that misses a production window. You swallow the short-term hit and rebuild.

A recall during your first three years... or during a fundraise... can end the company entirely.

I've seen it happen. Not from the newspaper headlines. Up close, watching founders who had everything going for them suddenly spending sixty hours a week managing a crisis that consumed their executive team, their legal budget, and their investor relationships at exactly the moment they needed those investors to stay calm.

Here's what I know now that I wish I'd known earlier: the brand that survives a recall already had the systems in place before it happened. The brand that doesn't... was hoping it would never come.

Hope is not a strategy.

---

**What actually causes a recall**

Undeclared allergens are the most common. Someone changed a formula. A supplier substituted an ingredient without notifying you. A label got printed with old copy. And suddenly you've got a product on shelf that can send someone with a tree nut allergy to the emergency room.

Microbial contamination is the scarier version. A break in cold chain. A sanitation issue at the co-man. An HPP machine that wasn't calibrated properly. For a cold-pressed juice or a refrigerated protein product, the window between "fine" and "dangerous" is small.

Foreign object contamination. Mislabeled net weight. A co-manufacturer error that shipped before your QA team caught it.

None of these require bad intent. Some of the best-run CPG brands in this country have been through voluntary recalls. KIND, Enjoy Life Foods, Once Upon a Farm... these aren't careless operators. Recalls happen. What matters is whether you had the infrastructure to catch them fast and respond with discipline.

---

**The systems you need before the phone rings**

Most early-stage founders treat quality infrastructure as overhead. It's what you build after you've proven product-market fit. After you've got distribution. After you've closed your Series A.

That's exactly backwards.

Here's what recall-ready looks like:

**Lot traceability from day one.** Every production run, every batch number, every unit that left your facility. Retail, DTC, Amazon, foodservice. If a complaint comes in, you need to know within hours which lots are affected and where they went. Brands without lot traceability don't get to do a targeted recall. They pull everything. The difference between pulling one batch and pulling your entire retail footprint... is your lot number system.

**Supplier certificates of analysis on every run.** Every ingredient, every production lot. If your co-man doesn't require CoAs from their ingredient suppliers, that's a conversation you need to have today, not after you have a problem.

**A HACCP plan that actually reflects your operation.** Not the template you downloaded three years ago. The live document that maps your current co-man, your current process, your current suppliers. Have someone with food safety expertise review it annually. Ignorance is expensive in any business, but especially here.

**Product liability insurance.** Minimum $2 million in coverage. For any brand doing $5M+ in revenue, I'd want $5 million. A recall costs more than most founders expect... lab testing, retailer pull fees, consumer notification, FDA counsel, logistics of pulling product. Budget it like overhead, because it is.

**A written recall protocol.** Before you ever need it. Who calls the FDA? Who notifies retailers? Who manages incoming consumer contacts? Who talks to press? This document should exist and should be accessible before the crisis arrives.

---

**The first 72 hours**

The protocol isn't complicated. But it has to move fast.

First call is internal. Get your leadership team together, determine scope immediately. Which lots? Which retailers? Which dates of production?

Second call is to the FDA. Voluntary recalls are always better than FDA-ordered ones. The agency rewards transparency. Don't wait to be told. Call them.

Third call is to your retail buyer. Not an email. A call. They're going to find out either way... you want them to find out from you, and you want them to hear a plan, not just a problem. Retailers have seen recalls. What they haven't always seen is a founder who gets out in front of it cleanly. That's how you protect the relationship.

Your investor communications happen in parallel. Brief, factual, with a clear action plan. Investors panic when they don't have information. Give them information first.

---

**The fundraising problem**

Fatal Flaw #7 in my catalog: a product recall during your first few years, or during a fundraise, can be catastrophic.

Not because recalls automatically kill investor confidence. But because investors invest in founders, not just businesses. How you handle a crisis tells them everything about who you are. A founder with clean systems, transparent communication, and a disciplined response... they can close that round. I've seen it done.

A founder who hid problems, had no traceability, and scrambled to manage information after the fact? That story kills deals. Every time.

---

**The thing most founders don't want to hear**

You don't get to skip this infrastructure.

I know you're moving fast. I know recalls feel like a problem for later, when you have more resources, a bigger team, a real operations function. But the time to build recall-readiness is before you ever need it. Not the week after the phone rings.

The brand that handles a recall well often comes out stronger than one that never had to face it. Because it proves to retailers, to investors, and to consumers that your integrity holds under pressure.

Some mistakes slow you down.
Some cost you money.
Some end companies.

The difference is almost always preparation.

That's what we proved to Coca-Cola on that Thursday afternoon. Not that we were perfect... but that when something was wrong, we said so immediately.

That is worth more than any headline number on a term sheet.
