One year after Suja launched into Whole Foods, we had a line review coming up with Target. Our head of sales Nicky wanted to prepare a pitch deck. I wanted to do something different.

So we invited the Target buying team to our house.

Linda made a home-cooked dinner. There were cocktails. There was real laughter. At one point, someone suggested a group photo in the room with the oversized bed. Perfectly harmless, but the kind of moment you only have when people actually like each other. My wife was right there with us. We were laughing.

Years later, I was pitching Target on a completely different company -- a protein soda called Proda. I reconnected with one of the same buyers from those early Suja days. They remembered. It opened the door again.

That's what Joint Business Planning is really about.

Not the deck. Not the meeting. The relationship that makes the meeting possible.

---

Here's what most CPG founders get wrong: they treat every retailer conversation like a negotiation they're trying to win.

You walk in with your numbers. You ask for more shelf space. You defend your promotional spend. You leave, wait to hear back, and repeat the whole cycle six months later hoping for a better result.

That's vendor behavior. Retailers have thousands of vendors.

What they don't have enough of... are partners.

Partners think about the category, not just their brand. Partners bring data that helps the buyer make better decisions -- not just data that makes themselves look good. Partners show up to the annual planning conversation with a genuine perspective on what the whole set needs to succeed. Not just what *they* need to grow.

The formal ritual for this kind of partnership is called Joint Business Planning, or JBP. And if you're not doing it, you're leaving real leverage on the table.

---

## What JBP Actually Is

Joint Business Planning is the annual conversation where a CPG brand and a retailer align on mutual objectives for the year ahead. Not just your velocity targets. Not just your promotional calendar. Both sides. Together.

In practice, it usually happens in Q4 for the following year. Large brands have entire teams dedicated to it. For a founder-led company, it often falls to you and your VP of Sales, sitting across from the buyer and their category manager.

Done well, a JBP meeting covers five things:

**Mutual goals.** Start by asking the buyer what they're trying to accomplish in the category this year. Not what they want from you specifically -- what a win looks like for *them*. You'd be surprised how rarely vendors ask this question. And how much buyers appreciate it when someone finally does.

**A shared look at the data.** Bring the syndicated data. Bring your store-level velocity. Bring the category trend lines that support your case. Great founders do more than tell a great story. They tell stories supported by the data. The best JBP conversations are anchored in facts, not feelings.

**Your promotional investment plan.** Walk through your promotional calendar for the year: what events you're planning, what the ROI targets look like, what the buyer can expect in terms of category lift. Be specific. Buyers don't respect vague commitments. They respect specificity.

**Your innovation pipeline.** What's coming in the next twelve months? What's being discontinued? Give them a window into your thinking. Retailers hate surprises. They love founders who let them see around corners, even when the corners are uncertain.

**A clear ask.** Don't leave without one. What do you actually want from this relationship in the next twelve months? More facing? A new SKU? An endcap placement? A program like Target's Made to Matter -- which put Suja on end caps with real visibility in 2015? Name it. Buyers can't champion what they don't know you're asking for.

The goal is to walk out of the room with a shared one-page document that both sides believe in. Not a contract. A commitment.

## The Velocity Conversation Nobody Wants to Have

Here's the part of JBP that most founders avoid: it forces you to have the velocity conversation *before* the buyer does.

At Suja, if a SKU wasn't performing, our sales team didn't wait for the buyer to raise it. They raised it themselves. They came to meetings with a clear-eyed read on what was working and what wasn't -- and they brought a solution.

Buyers appreciated that we were paying attention and thinking like partners, not just protecting our own shelf space.

That kind of transparency does something most founders don't expect: it builds trust. Because the buyer knows that when things go wrong, you'll be honest about it. Honesty is rare in this business.

The velocity benchmarks matter here. At most grocery retailers, you want to be at or above the 50th percentile in your set. Below 30% and you're likely not making the next reset. Below 20%... you probably won't finish the full cycle. These aren't arbitrary numbers. They're the rent payment on the shelf space you've leased.

"Shelf space is rented every week. Velocity is your rent payment."

At Costco the expectation is roughly $1,000 per club per week. Most grocery retailers you're looking at $15 per store per week on a solid performer. That's not just a different scale -- that's a completely different business model, and your JBP conversation at Costco has to reflect that. The Costco buyer understands your COGS in detail and will collaborate aggressively to lower your delivered cost because volume is their engine. Walk in without that understanding and you've already lost the room.

## Why Most Founders Skip JBP (And Why That's a Mistake)

The real reason is simple: JBP feels like extra work when you're already stretched.

You've got production problems. You've got a fundraise running. You've got three buyers who haven't responded to your emails in two weeks. The last thing you want to do is prepare a detailed annual plan for every major retail relationship.

But the founders who skip JBP are the ones who end up in reactive mode -- defending their shelf space at every review instead of expanding it. They're always one bad velocity quarter away from a discontinuation conversation they didn't see coming.

The founders who invest in JBP control the narrative. Because they shaped it twelve months earlier.

"Retail strategy is not just about doors. It is about order." And order means planning. It means showing up to every major retailer relationship with a perspective on the year ahead, not just a defense of the year that just ended.

"Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains." This is probably the biggest JBP mistake I see. Founders spend the annual planning conversation asking for more stores when the velocity in existing stores hasn't been proven. Buyers see right through it. Before you ask for expansion, show them what the current stores are doing. If you can't defend the existing distribution, asking for more is a credibility problem.

## How to Prepare for Your First Real JBP

If you've never done a formal JBP, start here.

Three months before the annual review season, pull your velocity data by retailer. Not just your top-level numbers -- get store-level. Find your best performing DMA or region and lead with that. Find your weakest and have a plan ready before they ask.

Talk to your broker, if you're using one. A good broker has done hundreds of these meetings and knows what a specific buyer cares about, what they've rewarded in the past, and what language lands. That's the other thing about brokers people underestimate: they've built the relationship equity you're still earning.

Then build one slide that answers the question: "If I give this brand everything they're asking for this year, what do I get?" That's the buyer's question. Answer it on your first page and you've earned the rest of the meeting.

---

## Relationship vs. Process

One more thing I want to be clear about: JBP is a process, but it isn't *just* a process.

The dinner at our house wasn't a JBP meeting. But it was the kind of moment that makes JBP meetings actually work. Because when you sit across from a buyer who trusts you as a person, not just as a vendor, the conversation changes completely.

Retailers rarely become long-term partners because of one great product. They become long-term partners because they trust the people behind it.

The deck, the data, the promotional calendar... all of that matters. But none of it substitutes for being someone a buyer actually wants to do business with. The two things reinforce each other. Build the relationship. Do the planning. Show up with data, an ask, and an honest read on what's working.

That's how you go from vendor to partner.

And in CPG, that distinction makes all the difference.
