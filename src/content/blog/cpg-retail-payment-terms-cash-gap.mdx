Picture this. You just landed your first big regional grocery chain. A six-figure purchase order. Your team is celebrating. You ship the product. The pallets leave your facility. You update your cash flow model.

Then you wait.

Thirty days out, you expect a check. Nothing. Forty-five days out, a partial payment arrives... with deductions you didn't budget for. Sixty days out, another partial. Somewhere around day 90, the full settlement is finally done. Meanwhile your co-manufacturer has been waiting for payment on the next production run. Payroll came and went. You borrowed against your personal line of credit to cover it.

The product sold. The business is working. And you're still scrambling for cash.

Welcome to retail payment terms. The part of CPG that nobody explains until it's already bleeding you.

---

## The Gap Is Bigger Than It Looks

Here's the thing about "Net 30." It sounds clean. Ship product, get paid in 30 days. Simple.

It is not simple.

Net 30 in retail typically means 30 days from the date the retailer enters your invoice into their system... which can be a week or two after the product actually arrives at their distribution center. Add in deduction processing time, compliance review, and whatever internal approval queue they're running, and your "Net 30" is effectively Net 45 to Net 55 before any money moves.

Then come the deductions. Promotional allowances. Short-shipment claims. Label compliance fees. Slotting adjustments. Sometimes legitimate, sometimes not. Either way, they come off the check... and now you're waiting another few weeks to dispute the ones that aren't right.

Add it up. Ship on Day 1. Retailer acknowledges receipt around Day 8. Their Net 30 clock starts Day 8. First partial payment arrives Day 38, minus 12% in deductions. Balance remits somewhere around Day 60 to 75. You spend the next two weeks on the phone disputing the deductions.

Effective days-to-cash: 90. Sometimes more.

Early in Suja's run, we made an agreement with a distribution partner that we'd absorb the spoilage risk if they'd go deeper on our orders. Made sense at the time. The velocity looked good. The relationship was strong. Ninety days later we had over a million dollars in spoilage deductions hit our accounts receivable. A million dollars. We had to run an emergency capital raise not because the business was failing... but because the timing of money going out and money coming back was completely misaligned.

That experience forged something I say to every founder I work with now: **the retailer's clock and your clock are not the same clock. The gap between them is cash you need to have in the bank.**

## What Terms Actually Look Like by Channel

Every channel has different payment norms. Here's what I've seen across thirty years and dozens of brands:

**Natural and specialty (UNFI, KeHE, smaller natural grocers):** Generally Net 30 to Net 45. More predictable timing, but deduction rates can be high because natural channel distributors take promotional commitments seriously and penalize non-compliance liberally.

**Regional conventional (Kroger, Publix, HEB, Albertsons):** Net 30 to Net 60 on paper. Effectively Net 45 to 60 with processing lag. Deduction workflows add more. You're not seeing clean cash in under 60 days at most of these accounts.

**Mass and drug (Target, Walmart, CVS, Walgreens):** Net 60 as a baseline. Some have early payment programs (more on that below). Compliance standards are strict and the penalties are real... the deduction rate on a compliance failure at Walmart or Target can be jarring the first time you see it.

**Costco:** Genuinely one of the better payers. Net 30, relatively accurate, fairly clean on deductions. If you have the velocity to get in, Costco's payment reliability is a meaningful operational advantage that doesn't get talked about enough.

**Sam's Club:** Similar profile to Costco. Net 30, reasonably clean. Volume exposure is large, so cash risk is still real, but the timing is better than conventional grocery.

**DTC and Amazon:** You collect before you ship. Cash positive by design. This is one of the structural reasons I'm an advocate for DTC as part of the channel mix, even for brands primarily sold in retail. Collecting in advance fundamentally changes your working capital equation.

For a typical emerging brand selling through two or three retail channels? You're looking at an average effective cash cycle of 60 to 80 days from production to payment. Build your model around that number, not the number on the contract.

## Three Ways to Close the Gap

**1. Factoring and invoice financing**

Factoring means selling your receivables to a third party at a discount to get cash today. A factoring company will advance you 70 to 85% of your invoice face value immediately and remit the balance (minus their fee) when the retailer actually pays. Fees typically run 2 to 4% of invoice value depending on the retailer's creditworthiness and the length of the terms.

Is it expensive? Yes. Is it sometimes the right tool? Also yes.

If your gross margins are 45%+ and your constraint is cash cycle rather than unit economics, factoring can make sense as a bridge. The rule I use: if the annualized cost of factoring still leaves you with a viable business after absorbing it, it's a rational choice. If factoring fees are the difference between profitability and loss at the unit level... you have a different problem that factoring won't solve.

CPG is a "penny profit" business. The pennies matter. Factor into the model, not around it.

**2. Early payment discount programs**

Most founders don't know these exist. Several major retailers now offer programs that let suppliers accept slightly discounted payment in exchange for very early settlement... sometimes within 10 to 15 days of invoice submission. Walmart's Supply Chain Finance program. Kroger and Target have similar programs.

A 1.5% discount for 40 days of acceleration is roughly 13% annualized cost of capital. That sounds bad until you compare it to the cost of missing payroll, blowing a co-man relationship, or doing a panic bridge raise at punitive terms.

Ask your retail buyer about these programs in your initial terms discussion. Many brands never ask. They leave the option on the table.

**3. Negotiate your terms before you ship**

The moment before you start shipping to a new account is the moment you have the most leverage you will ever have with that buyer. They want your product. You want in. That's when to negotiate.

Ask for Net 30 when they're offering Net 45. Ask about their payment program options. Ask what happens if you ship direct vs. through their preferred distributor. You won't always get what you ask for. But you will get nothing if you don't ask... and most founders never ask because they're so grateful to have the account, they treat the terms as non-negotiable.

They're not.

## Build the Model Around Reality

Before you commit to any new retail account, model the actual cash impact... not just the revenue line. If you're shipping a $200,000 order:

- When will you actually collect cash? (Not when do terms say — when do you actually collect?)
- What deduction rate are you reserving? (Industry average is 3 to 8% depending on channel. Budget for it.)
- What is your co-man payment obligation in the meantime?
- Do you have the working capital to bridge the gap at your current burn rate?

The brands that get this right are almost boring about it. They track days sales outstanding by account. They maintain a rolling 13-week cash forecast that distinguishes between "expected" receivables and "confirmed" ones. They know which accounts are slow payers and what their historical deduction rates look like.

Hope is not a strategy. Hoping a retailer pays you faster because you have a great relationship is not cash flow management. **Knowing exactly when they will pay you, under what conditions, and what you will do in the gap — that is cash flow management.**

The 90-day cash trap is real. It catches almost every founder at least once. The ones who build durable businesses learn the lesson early... and build a model that accounts for the gap before they ever ship the first pallet.

Now you know. No excuse for the surprise.
