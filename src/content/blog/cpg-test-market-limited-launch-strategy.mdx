I made the mistake. I know it intimately.

We had a brand called Rowdy Energy. We ran a 100-store test. The numbers were... incredible. Velocity looked insane. Everybody in the room was high-fiving. The buyer wanted to push us everywhere.

So we went everywhere. Twenty thousand doors. Fourteen thousand 7-Elevens.

And then the buyer called me and said, "Jeff, what happened? Your velocities are down 70% from the test."

I wanted to say I was shocked. I wasn't. Not really. I knew what had happened. I just hadn't wanted to believe it.

The test stores were over-indexed with our target consumer. We'd selected them (consciously or not) for favorable geography, favorable shopper demographics, favorable product mix. We ran demos. We talked to store managers. We cared. We pushed. And then we tried to replicate those results in 20,000 stores where we were invisible, anonymous, and had nowhere near the marketing budget to get people to even look at us.

That's how a CPG brand can get everything "right" in a test... and then get it very, very wrong.

---

Here's the thing nobody tells you when you're starting out: **getting on the shelf is not the hard part.** Getting off the shelf is.

A retailer will put you on shelf. Sometimes because they believe in you. Sometimes because they have a hole to fill. Sometimes because a buyer wants to look progressive. But they don't care why they listed you. They care about one thing: are you moving? Are you generating enough dollars per square foot to justify the real estate?

Shelf space is rented. Every single week. Velocity is your rent payment.

And if you don't pay... you get evicted. Not right away. Usually around the 9-month mark. That's the window in CPG. That's roughly the outer edge of how long a retailer will carry a new product before the data forces a decision. Nine months to prove you belong.

Which means you can't waste that nine months learning lessons you should have learned in a smaller, more controlled market.

---

When we launched Proda, our protein soda, we made a deliberate choice. One retailer. One year. Full commitment on both sides.

We went exclusive with Sprouts.

A lot of people told me that was stupid. "Jeff, you're leaving distribution on the table. You could be in Whole Foods and Target by now." And I understood the logic. If you've got product and retailer interest, why not take it?

Here's why not.

You don't have the marketing budget to genuinely support twenty retailers across all geographies simultaneously. You think you do. You don't. What you have is enough budget to make one market look really, really good. And a retailer that feels like a true partner will lean in with you in ways that a retailer who listed you as a hedge on three other protein sodas simply won't.

Sprouts gave us both ambient placement and cold placement. That's unusual. They leaned in heavy because we gave them an exclusive. We were aligned. We wanted the fastest-turning protein soda in the country and so did they. That alignment made everything different.

We got it. And that data became the foundation for everything that came next.

---

The principle is simple even if it's uncomfortable: **go deep before you go wide.**

Most founders do the opposite. They treat distribution like proof of viability. The more doors, the more legitimate the company looks. To investors, to their own family at Thanksgiving, to themselves. I get it. I've done it. It's a very human thing to want to show scale.

But here's the problem: distribution is not scale. Distribution is potential. And potential without velocity is just a lot of shelf space waiting to become a discontinuation notice.

"Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains." I've said this a thousand times and I'll say it a thousand more because I see the same mistake over and over. A founder gets into 500 new stores and treats it like a growth milestone. It's not. It's a liability until it's proven otherwise. Those 500 stores now need to be supported, stocked, managed for out-of-stocks, and generating enough turns to stay in the set.

---

So what does a good test market actually look like? Here's how I think about it.

**Start with one retailer, ideally your natural home base.** If you're better-for-you, that means Whole Foods, Sprouts, Fresh Thyme, Earth Fare. These are the consumers who will give you the highest initial velocity and the cleanest repeat data. You want a signal, not noise.

**Negotiate something that makes them care.** A year exclusive, an exclusive SKU, an endcap commitment in exchange for meaningful margin, your personal presence at key stores. Whatever makes this feel like a real partnership rather than a listing agreement. The retailer that has skin in the game will help you win. The one that listed you alongside forty others will not.

**Define your benchmarks before you start, not after.** This is critical. Before you launch, write down what success looks like. For us, that was velocity at or above the 50th percentile for the set, repeat purchase rate above 40%, and a sell-through rate that left no spoilage story. Know the numbers going in. If you let yourself grade on a curve, you'll rationalize your way into a national launch you're not ready for.

**Run the market hard for 6-9 months.** This is not a soft launch. You should be in those stores. Your team should be in those stores. You should be doing demos, talking to shoppers, understanding why people are or aren't coming back. You're not just testing whether the product sells. You're testing whether you know how to sell the product.

**Then read the data honestly.** If you're at the 70th percentile and repeat rate is 50%... you've passed. You have a story. Go wider. If you're at the 35th percentile and repeat rate is 22%... you have a problem. It may be the product. It may be the placement. It may be the price. But you need to solve it now, with 50 Sprouts stores, not discover it in 2,000 Kroger stores.

---

There's a number that matters more than any other at this stage of the game: your repeat purchase rate.

Under 20% repeat? That is not proof of concept. That is evidence that you're marketing your way into trial and not building a brand. And you can keep doing it, pumping money into demos and sampling and digital spend... but you're running on a treadmill. The day you stop spending, the sales stop.

At Suja, we targeted 40-50% repeat. When we hit that number in our early stores, that was the moment I knew we had something real. Not because the sales were impressive. Because people were coming back without being convinced to.

"You can market your way into trial. But you cannot market your way into loyalty." That's not a cute aphorism. That's the financial physics of a CPG business.

---

Here's the other side of this, though. And this matters.

When the test works... when the data is clean, the velocity is strong, the retailer is happy... you need to move fast. Because the market won't wait for you to get comfortable.

When we launched Suja in 50 Whole Foods stores in Southern California, we were in our second month when they wanted us in the Rocky Mountains. Third month, their national group in Austin called. They wanted us coast to coast.

I'm a startup junkie. When opportunity knocks, you don't say "hang on, let me finish my dinner." You grab it and you wrestle it to the ground.

But here's the thing: I could move that fast because we had the proof. The velocity data was undeniable. The repeat was there. When a retailer calls and says "we can't keep this on shelves," you don't need to do more testing. You need to execute.

The goal of the test market isn't to stay in the test market forever. The goal is to earn the right to move fast when the moment comes. That's why you do it right the first time. That's why you go deep before you go wide.

---

One last thing.

There's a fundraising dimension to all of this that founders often miss.

Institutional capital, the VC and PE money that lets you actually scale, wants to see velocity in what I call "Middle America" grocery. The H-E-Bs and Wegmans and Meijers of the world. That's what proves the concept travels beyond your early adopter base. That's what gets you to a Series A.

But you can't get to those retailers with clean data if you've diluted your early velocity story by going too wide too fast. If you're in 20,000 doors and averaging mediocre turns... that's the story the data tells. And that story does not get you funded.

If you're in 150 carefully chosen stores and you're the fastest-turning item in your set... now you have a story. Now an investor can project what that means at scale. The concentrated data is worth more than the diluted data, every time.

Build the proof. Protect the proof. Then scale it.

Gross margin determines destiny. But velocity determines whether you stay in the game long enough to get to the gross margin conversation. Don't skip the step that earns you the right to grow.
