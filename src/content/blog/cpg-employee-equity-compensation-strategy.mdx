Years after the Suja sale closed, I was at a trade show when a woman I didn't immediately recognize walked up to me. She worked for Presence Marketing — our broker, the one Bill Weiland had built into one of the premier natural food broker networks in the country. Mid-level account manager. Someone who'd been in the trenches selling Suja for years.

She grabbed my arm and her eyes welled up.

She told me that Bill had set up a program years earlier letting Presence employees allocate a portion of their retirement savings into Suja stock. She'd done it. She'd almost not, figured it was a long shot, but she did it.

"Jeff," she said, "I put both of my kids through college."

I think about that moment a lot.

Not because it confirms anything about me. But because it's a reminder of what equity, done right, actually means to real people. It's not a line item on a cap table. It's a future. A kid's education. A retirement someone didn't have to sacrifice. The kind of outcome that changes lives.

And most CPG founders... they never give their people that chance.

---

Here's the problem. You're competing for talent against companies that have been around for fifty years and can offer stock compensation on publicly traded shares, 401k matching, gold-plated benefits, and a business that isn't going to run out of money six months from now. Kraft Heinz doesn't need to explain what equity means. Nestlé doesn't have to sell the dream.

You do.

Which means equity isn't just a nice-to-have in your comp package. It's your primary competitive weapon. It's the offer you make that the big companies literally cannot match: a piece of something that could be worth real money, if you all build it right together.

The question isn't whether to offer equity. The question is whether you're doing it correctly. Because done wrong, it creates resentment, it complicates your cap table, and it can blow up your culture at exactly the wrong moment.

Here's what I've learned across eight companies and more hiring rounds than I can count.

---

**Get your option pool right before you need it.**

Most early-stage founders think about the option pool the moment they're trying to close a critical hire. That's too late. You should establish your employee option pool — typically 10 to 15 percent of fully diluted shares — before your first meaningful fundraise. Here's why: investors will push to have the option pool created pre-money rather than post-money, which dilutes you, not them. If you've already done it, there's no negotiation.

When you get to growth stage, you'll likely expand the pool. That's normal. But start thinking about this before you're desperate for a VP of Sales at midnight on a Monday.

**Vesting schedules: use the standard, then be generous around the edges.**

The industry standard is four-year vesting with a one-year cliff. This means nothing vests in the first twelve months, then 25% vests at month twelve, and the remaining 75% vests monthly over the next thirty-six months. Use this structure. It protects the company.

The cliff is not punishment. It's the most important part of the arrangement. If someone leaves or doesn't work out in the first year, you haven't given away equity you'll regret. I've seen founders skip the cliff out of goodwill and then spent two years watching a departed employee collect quarterly vesting while their replacement actually did the work. Equity without vesting is a time bomb. The cliff diffuses it.

Where you can be generous: extend the exercise window. The standard gives departing employees ninety days to exercise their vested options or lose them. For many early-stage employees, exercising in that window requires cash they don't have, on options that may not be worth anything for years. Progressive companies are now extending that window to five or seven years post-termination for good leavers. This signals that you're in this together — and it makes the equity feel real, not like a lottery ticket that expires before you can cash it.

**Get your 409A valuation done right and done on time.**

The 409A is an independent valuation of your company's fair market value, used to set the strike price on employee stock options. If you undervalue the company, the IRS can reclassify options as deferred compensation, creating a tax nightmare for employees. If you do it wrong, good luck explaining that to the person you just recruited.

Get a reputable firm to do it. Update it every twelve months, or whenever you've closed a significant funding round or had a material change in business value. When I went to build out my team at Suja and needed to offer options compellingly, the last thing I wanted was an outdated 409A creating uncertainty about the offer on the table.

**ISOs versus NSOs: it matters more than founders think.**

Incentive Stock Options (ISOs) and Non-qualified Stock Options (NSOs) are not interchangeable. ISOs offer better tax treatment for employees — if the employee holds the stock long enough after exercising, gains are taxed at the lower long-term capital gains rate rather than as ordinary income. NSOs don't get that benefit.

You can only grant ISOs to employees, not to advisors or contractors. And there are limits — no more than $100K in ISOs can vest in a single calendar year per employee. For higher-value grants, you're issuing a mix of ISO and NSO. Work with an attorney who knows startup equity. This is not a DIY project.

**Communicate equity as if your culture depends on it. Because it does.**

This is where I see most CPG founders fail. They create a solid option plan, then put it in an envelope with an offer letter and hope the new hire figures it out.

Don't do that.

Sit across from every key hire and walk them through what their grant means. Use specific scenarios. If the company reaches a certain revenue level and exits at a certain multiple, here's approximately what their options could be worth. If the company doubles, here's the range. If the company fails, here's what happens.

That conversation does something powerful. It tells the person: I believe this is worth your time. I believe we're building something real. And it makes equity feel like the asset it is, not an abstract line in a document.

Culture compounds just like capital. The difference is it compounds quietly. When people around your table feel ownership — real, documented, understood ownership — they think differently. They make decisions like owners. They stay later, they care more, they recruit their own networks.

"Show me your team," I've always believed, "and I'll show you what your company is about."

---

The woman at the trade show wasn't a co-founder. She wasn't a VP. She was a mid-level broker partner employee who took a small bet on something she believed in.

You have employees right now who would take that bet on you, if you gave them the chance.

The CPG journey is long. It's expensive. It's uncertain. The Rule of Twos applies to everything — twice as long, twice as expensive. But the people who go the distance with you, the ones who haul the product at two in the morning and troubleshoot the co-man at six a.m. and pitch the buyer for the fourth time... those people deserve a chance to participate in what you're building.

Equity done right is how you give them that chance.

Dream boldly. Plan soberly. And make sure the people building the dream alongside you have skin in the game.
