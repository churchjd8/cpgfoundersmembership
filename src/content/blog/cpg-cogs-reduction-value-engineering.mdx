There was a moment in 2016 when I looked at our financials and felt sick.

Not because of revenue. Revenue was great. We were approaching $100 million in sales, the fastest-growing CPG brand in America by some accounts, and the phones were ringing constantly from retailers who wanted us on shelf.

The sickness came from a different number. Our gross margin was sitting at around 28 percent.

For context: that's the number that tells you whether you actually have a business or just a very expensive hobby.

We had built something extraordinary on the top line. But the engine underneath it was hemorrhaging. Every dollar we generated, we kept less than thirty cents of it before even touching overhead, marketing, payroll, or debt service. We were growing ourselves into a corner.

That's when I understood something that changed how I think about building CPG brands. Growth doesn't fix the margin problem. Usually, it makes it worse. Because the margin problem is structural, and structure doesn't get better just because the numbers get bigger.

**"Revenue is exciting, but gross margin is transformational."**

I say this to every founder I mentor. It is not a slogan. It's a survival principle.

---

Here's what most early-stage CPG founders get wrong. They treat cost of goods sold as fixed. They build a product, land on a cost per unit, do the math on retail price, and lock in those numbers as "the model." Then they go run as fast as they can and hope the margin works itself out at scale.

It won't.

COGS is not a constant. It's a lever. And if you're not actively pulling it, you're leaving margin on the table every single day.

What I eventually built at Suja was something I'd call a systematic value engineering process. Not a one-time audit. Not a panic response to a bad month. A structured, disciplined approach to attacking cost at every layer of the product... repeatedly, over years... with the goal of buying back margin while maintaining or improving quality.

Here's how that works.

## Start with the Five Components

Your COGS breaks down into roughly five buckets.

**Raw materials and ingredients.** Typically the largest cost driver, often 40 to 60 percent of total COGS. This is what founders obsess over, usually because it's the most visible. They're right to focus here, but wrong to stop here.

**Packaging.** Bottles, cans, pouches, labels, corrugate. Often 15 to 25 percent. Almost always underattacked, especially in the early years.

**Contract manufacturing fees.** The processing fee you pay your co-man to run the line, fill, and seal. Often 15 to 20 percent. Very negotiable with the right leverage.

**Inbound freight.** Getting your ingredients to your co-manufacturer. Founders almost never look at this one. Big mistake.

**Quality control and testing.** Lab work, quality holds, spoilage reserves. Smaller but real, and sometimes hiding costs that belong somewhere else in the analysis.

Most founders only look at the first bucket. That's natural. But the other four are just as real, and just as improvable.

A value engineering project attacks all five. Not all at once. In sequence, with specific targets.

## How to Actually Run It

This is not something you hand to your CFO and walk away from. It needs a champion... usually the CEO or COO in the early stages... and a cross-functional team: operations, procurement, your R&D or culinary lead if you have one, and critically, your contract manufacturer. They are your partner in this, not just a vendor.

The process runs in four phases.

**Phase 1: Diagnosis.** Pull your COGS statement and break it into the five buckets. Benchmark each against comparable category companies. If you're early stage, ask your co-man, your ingredients broker, or other founders in your network what "good" looks like. Know your landed cost per case down to the penny. Know your margin by SKU, not just by brand.

Most founders are genuinely surprised here. They discover one SKU is running at 45 percent margin and another at 18 percent, and they've been treating them as the same business. That gap is information.

**Phase 2: Attack the top two buckets.** Ingredient costs are almost always the richest target. Work with your suppliers on volume commitments, alternate ingredient sources, and formula optimization. I've seen founders buy back 150 to 200 basis points just by negotiating a full-year volume commitment with a supplier they were buying from month to month. That supplier had been pricing risk into every order. Give them certainty, and they'll give you something back.

Packaging is the other big lever. If you're at 5 to 10 million in revenue, you're probably paying component prices sized for a one to two million dollar brand. A single packaging line review with competitive bids can deliver real savings, especially in bottles, cans, and corrugate. This is not a betrayal of your current supplier. It's a conversation. Good vendors respect it.

**Phase 3: Attack manufacturing and freight.** When you have leverage with your co-man... and you have leverage when you're bringing consistent, predictable volume that fills their capacity... that's when you renegotiate processing fees. Know your utilization rate on the line. Know theirs. If you're bringing reliable volume, make that worth something in the conversation.

Inbound freight is often ignored entirely. But if your ingredients are coming from multiple suppliers with separate freight bills, there is consolidation and routing efficiency to find. Run a freight audit. The savings aren't always dramatic, but they're real, and they compound.

**Phase 4: Measure and repeat.** A one-time project gets you one shot at improvement. A sustainable process runs every year. Build it into your annual operating plan. Set a target. I recommend 100 to 200 basis points of gross margin improvement per year through a focused value engineering effort. That may not sound like much. But 150 basis points on a 25 million dollar business is 375 thousand dollars of additional gross profit. That's the difference between making payroll in a bad quarter and not.

## The Product Mix Angle Nobody Talks About

Here's the thing that changed everything at Suja.

Around 2016, one of our co-founders Bryan had been experimenting with two-ounce wellness shots as a side project. At the time, we had a licensed kombucha product in our portfolio. The economics on that kombucha were brutal... roughly 12 percent gross margin, because we were outsourcing the fermentation and paying through the nose for it.

The shots? About 60 percent gross margin.

Now I'm not saying to cut every low-margin product. Sometimes a low-margin product is your trial driver, your entry point into new channels, your brand ambassador on shelf. You make strategic tradeoffs. That's fine.

But when you have a product at 12 percent margin and a product at 60 percent margin, and you have limited capital to invest in growth, you need to be very clear about where you're pointing the gun.

We leaned into the shots.

That decision... made on a random Tuesday, not in some grand strategic planning offsite... ended up being one of the most important financial decisions we ever made. When the Coca-Cola acquisition fell through in 2018 and we were looking at 40 million dollars in secured debt due in October, it was the shots that saved the company.

The decision that saves you is almost never the one you made on the day you needed saving. It's usually one you made eighteen months earlier, on a Tuesday, when nobody was watching.

This is the product mix dimension of COGS management that most founders skip. Your value engineering project is not just about reducing the cost to make what you're already making. It's about asking whether the mix of what you're making is serving your margin structure at all.

Look at your top five SKUs. What's the gross margin on each one? Where is your volume concentrated? Is your best-selling product your best-margin product? If not... that's a conversation worth having with yourself.

## The Targets to Hit

My standing advice to founders: build a plan that gets your gross margins to 40 percent by the end of year two. Close to 50 percent by years three to four.

If you're at 28 to 32 percent and growing fast, that's a code red. Not because you're failing, but because you're one bad quarter, one supply chain disruption, one lost retailer away from a capital crisis. The low-margin business requires constant capital infusion to function. That is a dependency you don't want.

Higher margins reduce your dependence on outside capital. They create more options. They allow growth to fund more of itself. And when you eventually get to exit, buyers put a multiple on EBITDA, not revenue. Every point of margin you buy back is worth dramatically more than the revenue you'd have to generate to get to the same place.

Suja went from 28 percent gross margin to nearly 50 percent by the time I left. That journey took years of deliberate, disciplined work. It didn't happen by accident. We attacked it systematically, and we attacked it every single year.

That's the work. The unglamorous, unInstagrammable work of running value engineering reviews, renegotiating with suppliers, and making honest product mix decisions.

CPG is a "penny profit" business. The pennies matter.

Gross margin determines destiny.
