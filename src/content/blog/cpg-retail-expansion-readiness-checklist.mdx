At Rowdy Energy, we paid nearly $1 million in slotting fees to Albertsons. Our velocity came in below what the account needed, and we were discontinued after eight months.

That was an expensive lesson. We got the distribution, spent the money, and didn't have the sales to hold it.

So when I ask a founder to slow down before accepting another 500 stores, I understand how annoying that sounds. You've worked hard to get the buyer's attention. Your team is excited. You finally have something big to put in the investor update!

I get it. I've felt that excitement. I also know what happens when it gets ahead of the operating plan.

Before accepting, ask the team to show how the expansion works after the opening order. What supports replenishment? How much cash goes out before you collect? Who makes sure the product is actually available and selling?

I want five answers before that commitment. These are the questions I'd work through with you across a table, with your numbers in front of us.

## 1. Show repeat sales in comparable stores

Show me what shoppers are buying in your current distribution. Separate the initial shelf fill from replenishment, and separate promotional periods from regular-price performance. That opening order can make a month look terrific. I'm interested in what happens when the retailer needs to order again.

Look at units per store per week alongside availability. A weak sales number at a store that never received product means something different from a weak number at a well-stocked store. Compare similar formats, regions, and shopper profiles where possible.

Don't borrow a universal velocity target from another category. Ask the buyer how your category and item will be evaluated, then document the agreed expectations and review dates.

Your approval question: **What evidence suggests shoppers in the new stores will keep buying?**

If we're still learning which shopper buys the product and why they come back, that's useful work to finish in a smaller footprint. Give your best stores attention. Learn what the weaker ones are telling you. You've paid to get that information; use it before buying a much larger experiment.

## 2. Calculate what the account contributes

Build the account economics using your actual commercial terms. Include product cost, applicable freight, commissions, promotions, allowances, and other account-specific costs without double-counting them.

Here is an illustrative example, not an industry benchmark. Suppose your brand receives $2.40 per unit before account-specific charges. Product cost is $1.20, freight is $0.15, commissions are $0.12, and planned promotional support averages $0.35. That leaves $0.58 before other account costs and company overhead.

Change the promotional support assumption to $0.55 and that becomes $0.38. A modest-looking change in the plan has removed $0.20 of contribution on every unit.

Work through a slower-sales case as well. List which expenses stay committed when volume falls short. This is a “Penny Profit” business. A few cents multiplied across a big account can change the whole decision, so let's put them on the page before we celebrate the revenue.

## 3. Fund the gap between production and collection

Positive contribution does not tell you whether you can afford the launch.

Map the dates for ingredient deposits, packaging purchases, production payments, freight, and expected collections. Include the possibility that a payment arrives later than planned or a deduction reduces the amount.

Then compare the lowest projected cash balance with the cash you need to keep the rest of the business operating. A proposed launch that consumes your operating buffer needs a smaller scope, different terms, or committed funding before approval.

Record the funding source. An investor conversation is not the same thing as available cash.

I'd also ask what gets squeezed if the launch needs more money. Your existing retail support? A production run for your best SKU? The people already keeping the business going? A new account has to fit inside the business you have, including the promises you've already made.

## 4. Prove the supply chain can handle the actual requirements

Confirm production slots, ingredient availability, shelf life, case packs, warehouse capacity, and shipping readiness. Check the retailer's current supplier requirements directly.

For example, [Walmart's supplier requirements](https://corporate.walmart.com/suppliers/requirements) address areas including product identifiers, insurance, and supply-chain packaging. That illustrates why gaining distribution involves more than producing additional units. Each account's requirements need their own review.

Assign unresolved requirements to specific people with dates. “Operations has it covered” is too vague for a launch commitment.

## 5. Name the person responsible after the first shipment

Who monitors availability, sales, deductions, and replenishment? Who talks to the buyer when the plan needs adjusting? Who can approve corrective spending?

Set an early review cadence and agree on decisions in advance. If velocity trails expectations, will you improve execution, test support, adjust the assortment, or pause further expansion? Define what evidence would justify each action.

I'd rather agree on those decisions while everyone is thinking clearly. Once you've announced the launch, pride gets involved. It's harder to say “let's pause” when you've spent three months telling everyone how big this is going to be. Give your team permission to bring you bad news early.

## Make the decision visible

Put the five checks on one page: repeat sales, contribution, cash, supply-chain readiness, and execution ownership. Give each an owner and a status. A missing answer is work to finish before the commitment.
