Picture this. Your warehouse agreement renews in December, so you put a review on the calendar for November. You're being responsible. You're getting ahead of it. Maybe you've even found a better warehouse and you're feeling pretty good about the savings.

Then you pull the contract. Notice was due months ago.

This is why I keep auto-renewing contracts on my list of fatal flaws. The mistake can look so small. One date. One paragraph nobody revisited after signing. And now you're trying to negotiate your way out of another term while also running the business.

I want you to catch it while you still have choices.

The renewal date tells you when the next term begins. The notice deadline tells you when you need to act to preserve your choices. Put both in your operating calendar.

The fix is pretty straightforward. Give every important agreement an owner, a verified notice deadline, and enough review time to make a good decision. This is one of those boring operating habits that can save you a very expensive conversation later.

## Start with the notice clause

Here's where I'd start. Pull the actual signed agreement, including amendments. All of them. The version somebody attached to an email before the last negotiation may be different from what you signed (and that's the version you need).

For each recurring vendor relationship, record:

- The end of the current term and the length of the renewal term.
- The deadline for giving notice of nonrenewal.
- Whether the deadline depends on notice being sent or received.
- The required delivery method and recipient.
- Any separate deadline for price changes or minimum-volume commitments.
- The person on your team responsible for the decision.

If the language is unclear, have counsel resolve it before you rely on a date. A friendly account manager saying “we'll work it out” is a poor substitute for understanding the agreement.

## Calendar backward from the decision you need to make

Imagine an agreement ends December 31 and requires notice 90 days beforehand. A reminder on December 1 cannot give you back the review time you've already lost. This is an illustrative example; your actual deadline depends on the contract's wording and date-counting rules.

Set reminders before the **notice deadline**, with enough time to evaluate performance, compare alternatives, and deliver any required notice. The workshop's 90-, 60-, and 30-day reminders are useful starting points, but anchor them to the deadline that requires action.

Give each reminder a job:

- **First review:** confirm service performance, pricing, and the next year's needs.
- **Decision review:** choose renewal, renegotiation, or a transition plan.
- **Final check:** confirm that any required notice was delivered properly and save the evidence.

An alert called “contract renewal” is easy to dismiss. “Decide whether to renew warehouse agreement; notice due on [date]” tells somebody what to do.

## Review the relationship before renegotiating the price

A lower rate does little for you if the vendor cannot support your next stage.

This is where I'd slow you down for a minute. What did you need when you signed? What do you need now? An early-stage brand can change a lot in a year. Different channels, different production volumes, different demands on your team. A relationship that made sense at the beginning deserves another look as the business changes.

Look at service errors, responsiveness, reporting quality, capacity, and the work your team absorbs to make the relationship function. For a broker, review account execution. For a warehouse, review inventory accuracy and fulfillment. For a co-manufacturer, review production reliability and the cost of changeovers.

Then ask what leaving would require. Inventory transfers, packaging commitments, tooling access, data exports, and outstanding purchase orders can all affect the transition. Start early enough to make a workable choice.

## Don't assume every renewal clause works the same way

The legal treatment depends on the agreement and applicable law. For example, [New York General Obligations Law § 5-903](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GOB/5-903) sets reminder-notice requirements for certain service, maintenance, and repair contracts. That is a specific rule with a defined scope, not a universal escape from automatic renewal.

Have your attorney assess the actual clause. Operationally, keep tracking the deadline while that review happens.

## Your next action: audit five agreements

Start with the relationships that would be expensive or disruptive to replace. Give each a named owner and a verified notice deadline. Store the signed documents somewhere the backup owner can access.

Review this register monthly. Update it whenever an amendment changes the term, the price, or the notice requirements. And make sure the backup owner knows where it lives. You should be able to take a week away without a vendor decision getting made by default.

You can still decide to renew. You may have a great partner! I just want that to be a decision you made on purpose, with time to work through the terms.
