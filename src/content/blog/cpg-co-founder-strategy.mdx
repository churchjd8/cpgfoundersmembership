I want to tell you about three people who built something extraordinary together.

Eric Ethans had just closed his raw-food restaurant in Encinitas, California. He'd been making cold-pressed juices in the kitchen, and when the restaurant shut down, he started hand-delivering bottles door to door. On a skateboard. In San Diego. Customers loved him. They loved the product more.

Bryan Riblett was his business partner. The formulator. The guy who understood what went in the bottle better than anyone on earth. And when James Brennan started bringing 16-oz dark green bottles into my New York office with that accent of his... "Dude, you've got to try this green juice"... the three of us eventually found our way into a room together.

I was 38. Third kid just born. Just left the corporate track. Reading a business plan that would eventually become Suja Juice.

Three completely different people. Three completely different skill sets. Zero formal co-founder agreement. Zero prior working history. And somehow, improbably, that combination produced $0 to $100 million in six years, faster than KIND, ZICO, Honest Tea, or Annie's at comparable stages.

That's the part people remember. Here's what they forget.

## The Part Nobody Talks About

Most co-founder relationships I've seen up close don't end the way Suja's did.

In my thirty-plus years of building companies, I've watched partnerships dissolve at the $2 million mark because one person wanted to raise capital and one wanted to stay bootstrapped. I've watched 50-50 splits paralyze decision-making for two years while a retailer window closed. I've watched a founding team of three turn into a legal battle that cost more to resolve than the company was worth at the time.

The co-founder relationship is the most leveraged relationship in your business. More than your lead investor. More than your anchor retailer. Get it right and you move faster, see farther, and build bigger than you ever could alone. Get it wrong and you're fighting a two-front war... one outside with the market and one inside with the person who's supposed to be your most important ally.

So let's talk about how to get it right.

## Start With the Three Roles

Before you think about who, figure out what you actually need.

Every successful CPG founding team I've been close to covers three functional roles, whether it's one person, two, or three.

**The Creator.** This is the product person. Eric. The one who understands what goes in the bottle, why it tastes the way it does, what the consumer feels when they open it. In beverage, that's a formulator. In food, it might be a chef or a food scientist. If your founding team doesn't have someone who lives and breathes product, you'll always be guessing about the most important variable in CPG. And remember: "93% of the time, consumers choose on taste above everything else." If nobody on your team is obsessed with the product, that stat is working against you.

**The Builder.** The person who turns a product into a brand. Packaging. Positioning. The story that makes someone reach for your bottle instead of the one sitting six inches away on the shelf. You can outsource some of this to agencies and consultants, but you need someone internally who wakes up in the morning thinking about why a consumer should choose you. That conviction can't be rented.

**The Operator.** The business brain. Someone who can read a P&L without squinting, talk to an investor without sweating, negotiate a co-man contract, and hold the whole financial structure together while the other two are doing what they do best. Someone who understands that "CPG is a 'Penny Profit' business" and treats every margin point like it matters. Because it does.

Most founders who struggle in co-founder relationships are actually struggling because they've doubled up on one of these roles and left another one empty. Two operators and no creator means you're fighting over strategy while the product never gets right. Two creators and no operator means you run out of money six months before you'd have figured it out.

Map the roles before you pick the partner.

## The Equity Conversation Nobody Wants to Have

The single most predictable source of co-founder conflict is the equity conversation that didn't happen early enough.

Have it on day one. Not month six when you've built something real. Not after the first retail win when everybody's emotions are running high. Day one.

Here's the framework I'd use.

Equal splits (50-50 or 33-33-33) feel fair. They can work... if you've been deeply honest about what everyone is actually contributing, if everyone has the same risk tolerance, and if everyone trusts each other's judgment completely. That's a lot of "ifs." In my experience, equal splits work when the contributions are genuinely equal and the decision rights are clearly separated. They fail when they're used as a way to avoid the harder conversation about who's risking more, contributing more, or leading more.

Unequal splits are uncomfortable to propose. And they're the right answer more often than founders think. If one person is leaving a $200K salary and one is keeping their day job for the first year, that's not equal contribution. Price that reality into the structure before resentment prices it for you.

Standard vesting: four years with a one-year cliff. Meaning if someone leaves in month eleven, they walk away with nothing. After month twelve, they've vested 25%. Then monthly vesting until year four. This is non-negotiable, in my view. I've said it a hundred times: "Equity without vesting is a time bomb." I've watched companies get to a great outcome and then have it complicated by a co-founder who left in year two but never got bought out properly because nobody put a vesting schedule on paper.

Don't skip this conversation. "Hope is not a strategy" applies here too.

## What the Agreement Needs to Cover

You need a co-founder agreement. Not someday. Before the first dollar moves.

At minimum it needs to address: equity splits and vesting schedules, who has decision rights on what (product decisions, hiring decisions, capital decisions), what triggers a buyout, how you value the buyout if it happens, what happens if one partner wants to raise capital and the other doesn't, and what happens when you genuinely can't agree.

That last one is the clause founders skip because they can't imagine needing it. That's exactly why they need it. I've seen companies ground to a halt on decisions as basic as whether to pursue a new retailer because two 50-50 owners couldn't resolve a disagreement and had no mechanism to break the tie.

Hire a lawyer who has done CPG co-founder agreements before. Not your general business attorney. Someone who has been in the room when one of these relationships fell apart and knows what the language actually needs to say to protect everyone involved.

The agreement isn't a symbol of distrust. It's a symbol of respect. You're saying: I take this seriously enough to protect both of us.

## The Four Questions Before You Commit

If you're still in the selection phase, here are the four questions I'd want answered before I locked arms with anyone.

**What are you leaving to do this?** The answer tells you their true risk tolerance. A person walking away from a $300K financial services salary has already demonstrated something about how seriously they're taking this. Someone who's contributing "on nights and weekends" while keeping their primary income intact... that's a different level of skin in the game.

**Where do you want this to go in five years?** You'd be amazed how often co-founders have never had this conversation. One person is building to $20M and a lifestyle business. One person is building to $100M and a strategic exit. Those are compatible visions right up until they're not. Usually around year three when the first acquisition offer comes in.

**What decisions do you want to own?** If two people both want to make product decisions, you have a problem. If both want to run the sales relationships, you have a problem. Push for specificity on this before you start. The answer tells you whether your skill sets actually complement each other or just politely overlap.

**How do you behave when things go wrong?** Ask for a specific story. Not a hypothetical answer. A real situation where things went sideways, what they did, and what they'd do differently. "Resumes are rehearsed. Opinions are polished. Stories are revealing." Listen for ownership. Listen for blame. The answer to this question predicts how they'll handle the first real crisis, and in CPG there's always a first real crisis.

## The Warning Signs

If you're already in a co-founder relationship and something feels off, here's what I've seen that tends to precede a real fracture.

One person stops sharing bad news with the other. That's usually fear, which means the communication channel is already compromised.

Decisions are getting made in private instead of together. The CEO checking with an investor before talking to the co-founder. The COO making an operational call without looping in the business lead. Small things that build into a pattern.

The equity conversation hasn't happened because neither person wants to have it. That silence has a shelf life. It always comes out eventually. Always at a worse moment than if you'd just had the conversation early.

If any of those feel familiar... have the conversation now. Not after the next raise. Not after the next milestone. Now. These things don't resolve themselves. They calcify.

## The Bottom Line

I got lucky with Eric and Bryan. I know that. We fell into a complementary dynamic that could have easily never happened. But I've also been deliberate about co-founder structures on every venture since. And the difference between the ones where it worked and the ones where it didn't almost always traces back to the same root: clarity, or lack of it.

The right co-founder won't make your job easier. They'll make what you can build bigger. They'll see the things you can't see, cover the ground you can't cover, and call you out when you're wrong in ways that investors and employees never will.

But the wrong one will take you places you never planned to go.

Most of them are bad places.

"Dream boldly. Plan soberly." That applies to co-founders as much as it applies to anything else in this business. Choose carefully. Structure it properly. Protect the relationship like it's the most important asset in the company.

For most of the years you're building... it is.
