Forty-four fundraising rounds.

That's the number I've completed across thirty-plus years as a founder. Eight companies, $212 million raised, nearly $700 million returned to investors. Five home runs. Three strikeouts. And somewhere around round fifteen or sixteen, I figured out something that changed every raise I did after it.

The investors who wrote the fastest checks in my later rounds... they weren't strangers. They were people who'd been getting my updates for eighteen months. People who'd already watched me handle bad news honestly. Who already knew the business, the team, the vision. They didn't need convincing because I'd already done it — drip by drip, over dozens of emails.

The founders who struggle to close rounds? Almost universally, they're calling investors they haven't spoken to since the last check cleared.

"Enthusiasm attracts. Desperation repels."

That line is true in a room, and it's just as true in an inbox.

---

## The Phone Call You Never Want to Make

Here's a scenario I've seen play out dozens of times. A founder raises a seed round — $750K, eight or ten angels, good terms. Everyone's excited. The cap table is clean. The vision is bold.

Then... silence. Radio quiet for eight months.

The founder is heads-down building. Working seven days a week. Doing what founders do. And the investors? They've got nothing. No updates. No velocity data. No wins, no losses. Just... nothing.

Then month nine, the runway starts looking thin. Cash in the bank is maybe four months out. It's time to raise a bridge.

So the founder fires off an email. Hey everyone — things are going great, we're crushing it, we need $500K to get to our next milestone.

Here's the problem. That email is the first real communication those investors have had in months. They don't know if things are "going great." They have no context for the ask. And that "bridge" request doesn't feel like growth — it feels like a rescue.

You've essentially walked back into someone's life after eight months of silence and asked to borrow money. How do you think that goes?

Compare that to the founder who's been sending monthly updates. Who wrote in month two about a new retail door they won, and in month four about a spoilage issue they caught early and fixed. Who was honest in month six when gross margins came in lighter than projected and explained the plan to close the gap. Who shared the velocity data in month eight showing quarter-over-quarter improvement.

That founder fires off the same bridge email in month nine. And the response time is completely different. Because those investors already know the story. They've been watching the movie. They know the team can execute and handle adversity. The ask feels like a logical next chapter, not a cry for help.

That's the difference between a cold investor and a warm investor. And you create warm investors with consistent, honest communication — not with a great pitch deck.

## Why Most Founders Avoid Investor Updates

I get it. Writing a monthly update when things are going badly feels awful. You're exhausted, the business is hard, and the last thing you want to do is write a summary of everything that went wrong this month and send it to the people whose money you're spending.

So you skip it. And then you skip the next one. And before long, you've built a wall between you and your investors out of a combination of embarrassment and inertia.

This is the trap.

The investors who eventually feel most burned — the ones who become problems when things get hard — are almost always the ones who felt left in the dark. Not because the business failed, but because they found out about it secondhand. They got blindsided.

One of the principles I've operated by for thirty years: I have never successfully sold or financed a company from a position of weakness.

And weakness doesn't just mean bad financials. Weakness means your investors don't trust you. Weakness means they're calling other portfolio founders to ask what's really going on at your company. Weakness means you show up to a raise already on defense.

You build trust the same way you build gross margin. Slowly, consistently, and before you desperately need it.

## What a Great Investor Update Actually Does

A well-written investor update does three things at once.

First, it manages expectations. CPG is a "Penny Profit" business — the pennies matter, and so does the rhythm. When investors have been getting monthly updates, they understand the seasonality, the competitive pressure, the operational reality. They've calibrated their expectations against the real business, not the pitch deck. That means when you have a tough quarter, you're not resetting their entire mental model of the company. You're just updating a story they already know.

Second, it builds confidence through transparency. There's something counterintuitive here: being honest about problems makes investors more confident, not less. When a founder says "here's what went wrong this month and here's what we're doing about it," that's leadership. That's the behavior of a founder who has the company under control. Hiding bad news feels safer in the moment, but it's corrosive. It signals either that you don't understand the business or that you don't trust your investors with the truth. Neither is a good look.

Third — and this one is underrated — it builds your round before you start the round. Capital amplifies whatever foundation you've built. If you've spent eighteen months building a foundation of trust and consistent communication with your existing investor base, they will often re-up before you even open the round to new investors. And existing investor participation is one of the most powerful signals you can send to new investors. It says: the people who know this company best, who've seen it up close, who've watched it hit and miss its milestones — they're writing another check.

That's social proof that no pitch deck slide can replicate.

## The Structure That Actually Works

After 44 rounds and years of sending these updates myself, here's what I've found works:

**Keep it monthly.** Quarterly is too slow. The story moves too fast in early-stage CPG for quarterly to be meaningful. Monthly keeps investors in the cadence of the business.

**Keep it honest.** The most important thing is not to curate only the good stuff. I say this clearly: share wins and challenges in roughly equal proportion. When investors only hear good news, they stop trusting the good news.

**Lead with the headline metric.** What's the most important number right now? Revenue, velocity, gross margin, days of runway — whatever is governing your decisions this month, lead with it. One number. Not fifteen.

**Tell one story.** Something that happened this month that captures where you are. A buyer conversation. A production win. A failure that taught you something. The story grounds the numbers in reality.

**Share the ask.** This is the part founders skip. Every good investor update ends with a specific ask. Not "let us know if you can help" — that's vague and useless. Something like: "We're looking for an introduction to a buyer at Sprouts" or "If you know anyone with deep cold chain experience, I'd take a call." Your investors are a network. Use them.

**Keep it short.** No investor is reading a 2,000-word update. Five paragraphs, ten minutes to read. If it takes longer than that to write, you're trying to explain rather than communicate.

## The Moment That Changed How I Think About This

At Suja, we had an extraordinary group of angels who backed us from the beginning. Before the institutional rounds, before Coca-Cola, before Goldman Sachs. These were people who took a real risk on us.

By the time we did the Coca-Cola deal — which valued the company at $300 million — there was a complication. Some of the terms were structured in a way that would have benefited the most recent institutional investor far more than the angels who'd been there from day one. It was a painful realization.

We fought to find a better structure. We worked with Goldman Sachs to put together an alternative that let those angels sell roughly 40% of their holdings at 3.4x. And they kept the other 60%. Which they eventually saw return closer to 10x.

I think about that outcome sometimes. And I genuinely believe it was possible — in part — because we'd maintained those relationships. Because those investors were still engaged. They were advocates, not just check-writers. When we went back to them to explain the situation and what we were trying to do, they trusted us. Because we'd given them every reason to.

Trust compounds. So does integrity.

You build that trust long before you need it.

## The One Thing You'll Be Tempted to Skip

When things are going well, you'll be tempted to skip the update because you're busy. When things are going badly, you'll be tempted to skip it because it's uncomfortable.

Skip it enough times, and you've built a wall between you and your investors at exactly the moment in the life of the company when you need them most.

Hope is not a strategy. And investor silence is not investor patience. It's investor uncertainty, quietly building.

The monthly update is fifteen minutes of writing. It is not glamorous. It is not what anyone imagines when they picture themselves as a CPG founder.

But it's one of the highest-leverage things you can do between rounds. Because when you show up to raise money from a warm investor base — people who know the story, trust the team, and believe in the execution — you're not selling anymore.

You're just collecting.
