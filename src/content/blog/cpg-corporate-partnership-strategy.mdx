There's a memory I keep coming back to.

Growing up in Western New York, first warm days of summer, I'd spend hours painting the dock at our family lake house with my dad. Hard work. Sweat. The smell of paint and lake water. And at the end of the day, we'd crack an ice-cold Coke.

I had no idea that three decades later, I'd be sitting across a table from the world's largest beverage company, negotiating what would become a $300 million valuation for the company I'd helped build from zero.

There's something almost surreal about that.

I'm not telling you this to be sentimental. I'm telling you because the relationship between Suja and Coca-Cola taught me more about corporate strategic partnerships than any business school class ever could... and most of what I learned came from the hard parts, not the headline.

## The Fantasy vs. The Reality

When founders talk about landing a corporate strategic partner, I hear the same script every time. Distribution leverage. Brand validation. Global reach. Resources. Access.

They're not wrong. All of those things can happen.

What they're rarely ready for is the reality of being "half in and half out." That in-between zone where you're not fully independent and not fully acquired. Where you're simultaneously trying to operate at speed and navigate the approval cycles of a company forty times your size.

Pre-meetings become a thing.

That phrase sounds minor. It's not. It represents the entire weight of operating inside a large-company orbit without the full resources or authority. You're accountable to a partner you don't fully control, and they're accountable to processes you weren't built for.

We closed our deal with Coca-Cola in July 2015 at a $300 million valuation — 4.7x trailing twelve-month net sales. Together with Goldman Sachs Private Equity, $150 million was invested for a 50% stake. It was a two-step deal: Coke would acquire the remainder at a future date, once certain milestones were met.

Sounds clean, right? Here's what they don't put in the press release.

Two-step deals are tricky. You get partial liquidity for your investors. You retain operational autonomy on paper. But ownership and control are split. Teams can't fully integrate. Decision authority becomes unclear. Innovation cycles get longer. Budgeting becomes exhaustive.

"Strategic partnerships don't create great businesses. They amplify the strengths and expose the weaknesses that already exist."

We saw both sides of that.

## The Deal You Cannot Close Without an Internal Champion

I've led 44 fundraising rounds over my career. I've negotiated with investors ranging from angels to major PE firms. The Coca-Cola deal taught me something I'd never fully appreciated before: no deal survives without an internal champion on the acquiring side.

Someone inside the larger organization who is willing to take on reputational risk for you. Who will fight for your deal when you're not in the room. Who genuinely believes in what you've built.

For Suja, that person was Mike Saint John, then President of Minute Maid Juice. Warm, articulate, deeply knowledgeable about the natural foods space. He described Minute Maid as an autonomous entity inside the Coca-Cola universe. That word — autonomous — is what I needed to hear. Not just "partnership." Not "synergies." Autonomy.

He earned our trust. We earned his. And that relationship held when the institutional machinery tried to slow things down.

If you're pursuing a corporate strategic partnership right now, your first question shouldn't be "what's the valuation?" It should be: "Who is my champion inside that organization, and how strong is their internal position?"

No champion. No deal worth doing.

## There Is No Wrong Time to Do the Right Thing

In the final stages of the deal with the world's largest beverage company, we identified a potential quality issue. We pressed pause.

People thought I was out of my mind.

What it earned us was something far more valuable than speed. We earned the respect of Coca-Cola's senior leadership. Integrity under pressure is visible. People remember it.

"There is no wrong time to do the right thing."

That's not just an aphorism. That's a strategy. The credibility you build by doing the hard, right thing in a high-stakes moment compounds for years after the deal is signed.

Earlier in that same deal, I also fought to protect our angel investors from getting squeezed out. The contractual structure would have allowed ACG — a PE firm that had been in the deal less than twelve months — to take the lion's share of the first $90 million, largely bypassing the people who'd spent three years building Suja alongside us.

It was ACG's contractual right. It was my job to fight it.

I found Goldman Sachs to put in $60 million as an alternative. The angels sold roughly 40% of their holdings at a 3.4x return while retaining 60% of their equity. Years later, their total return was roughly 10x their original investment.

"Deals are not just about valuations. They are about fairness and morale."

The people who believed in you when you had nothing deserve to participate in the outcome. If you have leverage, use it for something that matters.

## The Backlash Nobody Warns You About

When you take on a corporate partner — especially one as iconic as Coca-Cola — some of your most loyal customers will feel betrayed. They'll post. They'll write to you. Some will say you sold out.

Seth Goldman had walked this road before us at Honest Tea. His advice, and John Foraker's too: "Don't argue with customers. Invite them to watch."

Tell them to give you a year. Ask them to judge by your actions, not their assumptions. If the ingredients change, if the quality slips, if the mission drifts, they should absolutely hold you accountable. Until then, stay visible and stay honest.

We didn't ask for blind trust. We asked for time and accountability.

That's still the right approach.

## What Strategic Validation Actually Delivers

The fantasy of instant riches and seamless global distribution never fully materialized for us. What did materialize was credibility.

We became a company that other companies, retailers, and investors wanted to believe in. Not because we changed who we were, but because someone powerful had validated what we'd built.

Real things happened. Meaningful things. Aligning our plastic bottle sourcing through Coca-Cola's supply chain significantly improved our gross margins. Access to Nielsen data at preferential rates sharpened our category thinking. Walking into any buyer meeting and saying "our strategic partner is Coca-Cola's Venture and Emerging Brands division" opened doors that would have taken years otherwise.

Just not the doors you imagined when you first read the term sheet.

"Coca-Cola didn't validate Suja because we were perfect. They validated us because we'd spent years building a company worth believing in."

And then three years later, on July 3, 2018, Scott Uzzell called to say Coke would not be moving forward with the acquisition.

I've told that story elsewhere, and I'll tell it again someday soon. The full weight of that call, what it felt like walking downstairs to my family on the Fourth of July, the forty million in secured debt coming due in October... that deserves its own conversation.

But the lesson for today isn't about the failure of that partnership. It's about what made the partnership worth having in the first place, and what I'd do differently if I were advising a founder walking into that same situation now.

## What to Know Before You Sign

A few things I'd tell any CPG founder considering a corporate strategic partnership.

**Find your champion first.** Before you engage the company, identify the person inside who will fight for you when the deal gets complicated. Without them, the deal dies quietly and you never know why.

**Understand the two-step trap.** If the second step isn't contractually required, it may never happen. Get clarity on what actually triggers the full acquisition before you celebrate the partial one. "Half in and half out" creates friction that compounds.

**Protect who built it with you.** When you have leverage in a deal, use it for fairness, not just for yourself. The angels who took a chance on you when you were nobody deserve to participate in the outcome. Fight for them. You'll never regret it.

**Do the right thing at the hard moment.** There is no wrong time to do the right thing, including pausing a deal with the world's largest company to make sure the product is right. Integrity under pressure is visible. People remember it long after the term sheet is signed.

**Know what you're actually getting.** Strategic partnerships bring credibility, validation, and sometimes meaningful operational leverage. They rarely deliver the seamless global distribution machine you imagined. The synergies are real, but they're also limited. Go in with clear eyes.

Strategic partnerships don't create great businesses. They amplify the strengths and expose the weaknesses that already exist.

Make sure what gets amplified is something worth amplifying.
