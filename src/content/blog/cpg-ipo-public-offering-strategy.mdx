May 2026. Suja Life goes public on Nasdaq under the ticker SUJA at a valuation north of $700 million.

Ringing that bell had been a dream of mine for a long time. I asked to be there. Paine Schwartz, the current owners, cited company policy and declined.

So I watched from a screen in San Diego as the team... the current team... raised their hands above their heads in Times Square.

I want to be honest with you about that moment. Because I think it holds a lesson that's more valuable than anything I can teach you about SEC filings or underwriting fees.

Here it is: **"Build the company, but don't be the company. Love the work, but don't be the work."**

If you're building something, and you're building it right, the day will come when it no longer needs you. That's not a failure. That's the point.

The bell is a moment. The work is the point.

Now let me tell you what it actually takes to build a CPG brand worth ringing that bell.

---

## Most CPG Exits Are Not IPOs. Understand That First.

Before we go any further, let's set expectations. The vast majority of CPG exits are M&A transactions, not public offerings. Strategic buyers. PE firms. Brand aggregators. I've been through eight ventures, returned roughly $700 million to investors, and only one of those became a public company... and I wasn't CEO when it happened.

That said, an IPO is a legitimate exit path for the right brand at the right scale. And even if you never go public yourself, understanding what public market investors require will make you a better builder at every stage.

The discipline it demands... the financial rigor, the governance, the reporting infrastructure... that discipline is what separates brands that exit at premium multiples from ones that limp to the finish line.

## The Financial Profile That Gets You There

Public market investors think differently than private investors. They're not just betting on a vision. They're betting on a repeatable, reportable business.

Here's what the profile needs to look like before an IPO conversation is realistic:

**Revenue scale.** You're generally not having a serious IPO conversation below $100 million in revenue. More realistically, $150 million to $250 million. The market rewards brands that have proven the model isn't a fluke.

**Gross margin.** I've said it before and I'll keep saying it: gross margin determines destiny. For a public company, you want to be north of 45%. Suja went through years where margins were in the 28 to 32 percent range. That period was survivable in a private context. It would have been brutal on a public earnings call. By the time Suja hit the public markets, margins had improved substantially. The wellness shots business... 60% gross margin versus the 12% we were generating on outsourced kombucha... is a big reason why.

**EBITDA and profitability trend.** Public investors want to see a path to 15 to 20 percent EBITDA margin. Not necessarily there yet, but trending in the right direction with clear unit economics. The days of "grow at all costs, we'll figure out profitability later" ended. The market doesn't forgive burning cash without a credible profitability model anymore.

**Revenue quality.** How sticky is the revenue? How concentrated is your customer base? A brand doing $200 million with 70 percent of that revenue coming from one retailer has a single-customer concentration problem. Public investors will discount you heavily for that. Distribution breadth, velocity consistency, and repeat purchase rates matter enormously.

**Growth rate.** You need to be growing at a rate that justifies a premium multiple. 15 to 25 percent revenue growth makes a very different story than 5 to 8 percent. Suja grew at roughly 200 percent CAGR in its early years. By the time of the IPO, that had moderated to a more sustainable but still strong trajectory.

## The Governance Infrastructure You Need Years Before You Need It

Here's what most founders don't understand: by the time you're having serious IPO conversations, you needed to have your governance in place two to three years ago.

Public markets require an independent board of directors. Not your friends. Not your angels. Real independents with relevant backgrounds who will hold you accountable. Start building that board early. I've written before about why your advisory board matters as a founder... but the board of directors of a public company is a different level of responsibility entirely.

You need a CFO who has been through a financial audit. Public companies file audited financial statements. If your CFO has never managed that process, you have a problem. The cost of a great finance leader who has been through public company reporting is not a luxury... it's survival infrastructure.

You need internal controls. Documented processes. Sarbanes-Oxley compliance. Systems that can produce accurate financial reporting on a quarterly cadence under public scrutiny. The "we'll clean it up later" approach doesn't work.

I sometimes joke that the "Rule of Twos" applies to IPO readiness too. Whatever you think it costs to get compliant with public company requirements... double it. Whatever timeline you think you need... double that too.

## SPAC vs. Traditional IPO vs. Strategic Exit: Know Your Options

Let me give you a fast overview of the three main paths for a CPG brand looking toward a liquidity event.

**Traditional IPO.** Underwritten by investment banks, filed with the SEC, priced through a roadshow. This is the path Suja Life took. It is the most prestigious, requires the longest runway of preparation (generally 18 to 24 months minimum once you're serious), and is most appropriate for brands with strong institutional investor appetite. The underwriting fees and ongoing compliance costs of being a public company are real. Plan for 7 to 10 percent of IPO proceeds in direct transaction costs, and ongoing public company costs of $3 to $5 million annually.

**SPAC (Special Purpose Acquisition Company).** A faster route to the public markets via merger with a publicly traded shell company. SPACs got a lot of attention in 2020 and 2021, fell out of favor as many SPAC deals underperformed, and have been recovering slowly. For CPG brands, a SPAC can work if the SPAC sponsor has deep relevant industry experience and genuine distribution value-add. But the dilution from warrants and pipe deals can be brutal. Understand the structure before you sign.

**Strategic acquisition.** For most CPG founders, this remains the highest-probability premium exit. A strategic buyer who puts your brand in their distribution network and layers on their cost structure will often pay more per dollar of revenue than public market investors will. The Coca-Cola transaction with Suja... where they paid $150 million for a 30 percent stake in 2015, implying a $300 million valuation at roughly 4.7x trailing revenue... is a example of what's possible when the strategic fit is genuine and both sides see the synergies.

The hard truth? The same financial discipline that positions you for an IPO also positions you for a premium strategic exit. You're building the same company either way.

## Begin With the End in Mind. Not at the End.

The biggest mistake I see founders make is treating exit planning as something that happens near the end. It doesn't. Exit optionality is built into every major decision you make from day one.

Your gross margin trajectory either opens or closes doors. Your cap table structure either invites or complicates potential acquirers. Your customer concentration either creates risk or demonstrates broad durability. The governance you build either signals institutional readiness or signals "work in progress."

I have never successfully sold or financed a company from a position of weakness. The founders who command premium exits are the ones who built something genuinely worth buying, and who walked into those conversations from a position of strength.

Dream boldly. Plan soberly.

The bell in Times Square was a moment. What Suja Life represents... 30,000 retail locations, 50 million bottles a year, a category that didn't exist when Eric Ethans was delivering cold-pressed juice by skateboard in Encinitas... that's the work.

Write the ending into your beginning. The rest takes care of itself.
