Here's a conversation I have about twice a month.

A founder calls. They're somewhere between $2M and $8M in revenue. Things are moving. The team is stretched. They need someone who's done this before — a CFO who can model a fundraise, a CMO who's actually launched a brand at scale, a VP of Sales who won't flinch when a Kroger buyer pushes back hard.

But the payroll math doesn't work. Full-time at the right level means $175,000–$250,000 plus equity. They can't carry that yet.

And the conversation always lands in the same place: *"So we're going to bring in a fractional."*

Sometimes that's exactly right. Sometimes it's the most expensive mistake they'll make this year.

Here's how to tell the difference.

---

## The Case For Fractional (When It Actually Works)

Let me start with what's real. Fractional executives exist for a reason. The gap between what a $3M CPG brand needs and what it can afford to pay is genuinely large. A seasoned CFO who's built investor-ready financial models, managed a complex capital structure, and navigated a beverage P&L isn't sitting around waiting for a $100K salary offer. They're expensive. And early-stage brands can't carry that weight yet.

For certain functions, at certain stages, fractional is genuinely the right call.

**The CFO.** This is the clearest use case. At $1M–$5M in revenue, you need someone who can build investor-ready models, manage your cash flow, and speak fluently to a board. Cash is oxygen in CPG — lose it and you're done. A fractional CFO at 20–40 hours a month can handle all of that. I've seen brands pay $8,000–$15,000 a month for this and get five times the value of what a full-time hire at that same cost would provide. You're getting someone who's navigated this across 10 other companies. That experience compounds fast.

**The CMO at early stage.** If you have a solid marketing manager internally and just need senior strategic oversight — someone to stress-test your positioning, oversee agency relationships, and show up for board-level conversations — a fractional CMO can work. But only if that internal manager is genuinely strong. The fractional CMO is a quarterback, not a full backfield.

**Legal.** General counsel as a fractional resource is almost always the right call for brands under $15M in revenue. Contract review, IP protection, vendor terms, label compliance — this work doesn't require 40 hours a week. Find someone who knows CPG food and bev law specifically, not a generalist who'll charge you $400/hour to learn the industry on your dime.

**Specialized consultants.** A supply chain expert for a specific project — standing up a new co-man relationship, navigating an import issue. A regulatory consultant for FDA compliance. These are project-scoped and clean. That's fractional done right: a defined problem, a defined deliverable, a clear end date.

---

## The Functions Where Fractional Will Hurt You

Here's where founders get into real trouble.

**VP of Sales.** I'll be direct: your sales leadership cannot be fractional. Not at any stage where retail matters.

Retail buyers have 30 to 50 vendors knocking on their door every month. The ones who build real relationships — the ones who get the call when a competitor gets delisted and there's shelf space to take — are the ones who are all in. A fractional VP of Sales is nobody's internal champion at a major retailer. They can't attend the buyer's regional event because they're at another client's board meeting. They can't pick up the phone at 6 a.m. when your DSD distributor has a problem because technically that's not billable before 9.

"Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains." And don't confuse having a fractional sales leader with having a real sales strategy. Those are very different things.

**Head of Operations.** If you're manufacturing at any meaningful scale, your operations leader needs to be fully accountable. Full stop.

At Suja, we once had a transformer fail at 2 a.m. in the middle of a production run. Someone from the team climbed a tree next to the building, jumped onto the roof, and manually reconnected the circuit. Orders shipped on time. Our customers never knew what it took. That kind of ownership doesn't exist in a part-time relationship. When something breaks at 2 a.m., you need someone who is all the way in. Fractional people aren't.

**Retail Execution and Field Leadership.** Your field team — the people building displays, auditing shelf sets, managing demo programs — has to be genuinely invested in the brand. Fractional leadership here creates accountability gaps that show up on shelf at exactly the wrong moments. And in CPG, velocity is your rent payment. You pay it every week or you're gone.

## The Trap Nobody Talks About

Here's the thing about fractional that most founders figure out the hard way: the math often doesn't work.

A senior fractional executive can cost $15,000–$25,000 a month. That's $180,000–$300,000 a year. At the high end of that range, you're in reach of a full-time VP who has been in CPG for 10–15 years. And you'd get their full attention, their full accountability, and their full investment in your outcome.

The fractional model makes sense when you're genuinely not ready for the full-time version — when you need 20 hours of senior judgment per month, not 160. But founders often use "fractional" as a budget-saving mechanism when what they actually need is the whole person.

"Hope is not a strategy." And "we'll figure it out with fractional support" is often just a more expensive version of hope.

The other trap is the vague engagement. A fractional hire without specific deliverables, clear metrics, and accountability checkpoints is just an expensive advisory relationship. I've watched founders pay for a fractional CMO for 18 months and produce... a brand refresh that never shipped and a few decks that never got used.

Define the outcomes before you sign. What does success look like at 90 days? What decisions does this person make vs. advise on? What specific metrics move under their watch?

My rule for any engagement today: 3-month maximum contracts to start, with milestones written into the agreement. After 90 days, you know whether this is working. If you wouldn't commit to it, neither should they.

## How to Hire a Fractional Executive Right

Same scorecard as a full-time hire. Same standards, same rigor. A "fractional" title doesn't lower the bar — in some ways it raises it, because you need someone who can get up to speed fast, contribute immediately, and work within a compressed time budget.

A few things that matter specifically for fractional:

**CPG-specific experience is non-negotiable.** A fractional CFO who has never built a trade spend model doesn't know what they don't know. The language of this industry — deductions, velocity, SPINS, slotting, distributor margin — has to be fluent, not learned on your clock. You cannot afford to fund someone's CPG education.

**Stage-appropriate references.** Not enterprise company references. Not "I helped a $500M brand optimize their go-to-market." Did they actually help a $3M–$8M brand navigate a Series A? Or are you their "smaller client" while their real attention is elsewhere?

**20 hours a month, minimum.** Anything less than that and you won't get real strategic work — you'll get reactive availability. Which is worth almost nothing. The fractional model only works if they're actually present enough to know your business.

And then be honest with yourself about when it's time to convert. The signal is simple: if you're consistently waiting on a decision because your fractional person isn't available, you've already outgrown the arrangement. That lag costs more than you think — in deals not closed, in opportunities not moved on, in the compounding cost of every delayed decision.

"A rushed hire creates more work than an empty seat." True. But the answer to that isn't a fractional hire that pretends to be something it's not.

---

## The Upgrade Moment

Here's a useful rule of thumb. Use fractional from $0 to $5M — primarily for CFO and specialized consulting. Plan the full-time upgrade in your financial model so it doesn't surprise you. At $5M–$8M, your CFO, CMO, and VP Sales should probably all be full-time if you're serious about scale. At $8M+, fractional support is still valuable — but as a supplement to full-time leadership, not a substitute for it.

"The team that launches a brand is rarely the team that scales it." That's true for founders, and it's true for the talent strategy around them. Fractional done right buys you time and expertise while you build toward what you actually need. Fractional done wrong quietly bleeds you — in real dollars, in delayed decisions, and in the opportunity cost of not having the right people fully in the fight.

Use it surgically. Be honest about what you need. And don't let the budget conversation talk you into a cheaper version of the wrong answer.

CPG is a "penny profit" business. Every dollar of misallocated cost matters — including the ones you're spending on talent that isn't set up to win.
