The week Coca-Cola told us they weren't completing the acquisition, I sat in my office and tried to remember the last time I'd actually looked at our gross margin by channel.

Not a quarterly report. Not a board presentation. Our actual weekly number.

I couldn't remember.

That's the kind of thing you don't realize until it's too late. We'd grown from zero to $100 million in six years. We'd been named one of the fastest-growing companies in America. We had over 350 employees, relationships at Whole Foods, Target, Costco, and Walmart. And somewhere in all of that velocity, I had traded the discipline of looking at the business every week for the performance of presenting the business every quarter.

Big mistake.

By the time Todd Fisher, our CFO, and I were living inside weekly cash projections trying to figure out if Suja would survive, I understood something I should have understood years earlier. **The data that saves a company is never the data you review after a crisis. It's the data you review every Monday morning before one starts.**

I call it the Monday Morning Dashboard. Seven numbers. Every week. No exceptions.

It doesn't require a data analytics team or a sophisticated BI platform. It requires discipline. Which, in my experience, is exactly the thing founders are most resistant to when things are going well... and most desperate for when they aren't.

---

## 1. Cash Balance and Runway (In Days, Not Months)

Not "we have eighteen months of runway." That's board meeting language. What you need on Monday morning is this: how much cash is in the bank today, and at your current burn rate, how many days does that last?

Days forces specificity. Months enables comfortable fiction.

At Suja's worst, we were operating with less than $100,000 in the bank some weeks. That's a fact our monthly reports would have softened. Weekly visibility would have forced earlier decisions. When cash is tight, know it on Monday morning. Don't discover it Thursday when payroll runs.

The moment you slip below 120 days of runway, your priorities need to reset. Not because that's when you should panic, but because that's when you should be starting your next raise.

## 2. Gross Margin by Channel (Updated Weekly)

Most CPG founders check gross margin quarterly. Some monthly. Very few weekly. That's backwards.

Your gross margin is not a fixed number. It shifts with product mix, channel mix, promotional activity, and ingredient costs. A week where you're running a deep discount at Costco while your co-man is running at reduced efficiency is a materially different business than your "average" gross margin would suggest.

At Suja, we didn't understand this well enough in the early years. Our blended company-wide gross margin was hovering around 28 percent, and we felt okay about it because we were watching the blended number. When we finally broke it down by product line, we found our outsourced kombucha was running at about 12 percent gross margin. Twelve. And it was sitting inside a blended average that looked tolerable.

We eventually shifted away from kombucha and leaned into our internally-produced wellness shots, which were running around 60 percent gross margin. That single product mix shift moved company-wide gross margin from 32 percent to 40 percent. Eight points. From one decision.

Gross margin determines destiny. I mean that literally. If you're not watching it weekly by channel, you're flying blind in a business where the difference between 30 percent and 40 percent determines whether you get to stay on the field.

## 3. Velocity by Key Account (vs. Your Internal Minimum)

Velocity is units sold per store per week. And if you're in 400 Whole Foods doors, what's your average velocity today versus last week versus the minimum threshold you need to stay on shelf?

You have roughly nine months to prove you belong in the set. Once you're below the 30th percentile in velocity for your category, you're on borrowed time. Below the 20th percentile, something is seriously wrong.

The problem is, most founders know their distribution number. They don't know their velocity number. They celebrate getting into 400 stores and forget that the battle for that shelf space renews every single week.

"Don't confuse distribution gains with velocity gains." A product in 1,000 stores at below-minimum velocity isn't an asset. It's a countdown timer.

If velocity drops two weeks in a row at a key account, you need to know on Monday morning. Not at the next quarterly reset conversation.

## 4. Repeat Purchase Rate (Weekly Approximation)

This one's harder to track in real time because consumer data lags. But you should be approximating it weekly using whatever data you have access to... DTC data, retailer loyalty programs, consumer panel reports.

The target we always gave ourselves at Suja: 40 to 50 percent repeat rate. Below 20 percent means you don't have proof of concept yet, regardless of how many new trial users you're generating. Below 10 percent on DTC means you need to be talking to your consumers right now, because the product isn't delivering on its promise.

"You can market your way into trial, but you cannot market your way into loyalty."

Repeat rate tells you whether your product is actually working. No amount of distribution or marketing will save a product with a 15 percent repeat rate. You'll just burn through more shelf space and capital doing it.

## 5. Open Deductions and Chargebacks (Total Outstanding)

This is the silent margin drain nobody talks about until it gets out of hand.

Retailers deduct money from your invoices for a hundred different reasons. Damaged product, short shipments, promotional compliance issues, pricing disputes. Most of them are legitimate. Some of them aren't. And if you're not tracking the total outstanding balance every week and actively managing the process, they compound quietly until you've got a six-figure hole in your receivables that you never even contested.

I've seen small brands lose 5 to 8 percent of net revenue to unresolved deductions because nobody owned the process. CPG is a "penny profit" business. Five points of uncontested deductions is not a rounding error. It's the difference between breaking even and bleeding.

Track the total outstanding balance. Track what's being contested. Track what's resolved. Every week. Assign someone to own it. This number doesn't manage itself.

## 6. Top 5 Pipeline Opportunities (Next 90 Days)

Every founder should have a running list of the five accounts or distribution opportunities they're actively pursuing for the next quarter, with an honest probability assessment and expected close date.

Not a wish list. Not a brainstorm. Real conversations happening, buyers engaged, samples sent, POs requested.

This metric tells you whether you have a real pipeline or just hope. And hope is not a strategy.

The reason this belongs on the Monday Morning Dashboard is simple: if you don't review it weekly, near-term opportunities fall through the cracks while you're managing everything else. The Whole Foods buyer you had a great conversation with at Expo West doesn't hear from you for six weeks and books a meeting with someone else. The regional Kroger contact who was interested in your fall reset doesn't get your category data because you forgot to follow up.

Active pipeline management is how you compound distribution. Passive management is how you stagnate.

## 7. Burn Rate vs. Budget (This Week's Actual)

Not the monthly P&L. Not the trailing twelve-month summary. This week's actual operating spend versus what you budgeted to spend.

This is the number that creates weekly operational discipline. If you're 15 percent over budget this week, you know before it compounds into a monthly miss, a quarterly gap, and a board conversation you're not ready for.

This number forces a simple question every Monday: what did we spend that wasn't in the plan, and was it worth it?

At Suja in 2018, after Coca-Cola walked away, we cut marketing by more than $4 million and operating expenses by more than $3 million. Something surprising happened. Revenue grew about 10 percent in 2019. Constraint created clarity. When you know exactly where every dollar is going every week, you make better decisions about which dollars actually matter.

## The Monday Ritual

Seven numbers. Thirty minutes. Every Monday before 9am.

Not in a board presentation format. Not cleaned up for investors. Just you, the numbers, and an honest conversation with yourself about whether the business is moving in the right direction.

A simple one-page spreadsheet does the job. This week versus last week versus the same week last year. No fancy visualization required. The goal isn't sophisticated insight. The goal is visibility.

Here's what I've learned across eight ventures and 44 fundraising rounds: the things that kill CPG companies don't usually announce themselves loudly. They arrive quietly, week after week, in the numbers nobody was watching.

"Hope is not a forecasting model. I've always believed luck is found at the intersection of preparation and opportunity. If you're not operationally ready, opportunity doesn't wait long."

Knowing your seven numbers every Monday morning is how you stay ready.

On July 3rd, 2018, I hung up the phone after Coke said no. We had $40 million in secured debt coming due in October. Gross margins below 32 percent. Some weeks less than $100,000 in the bank. It wasn't one bad week that got us there. It was years of watching the quarterly narrative instead of the weekly reality.

The Monday Morning Dashboard won't save every deal or prevent every crisis. But it will make sure you see what's coming... early enough to do something about it.

Build the dashboard. Run it every Monday. The numbers will tell you everything you need to know, if you're willing to look.
