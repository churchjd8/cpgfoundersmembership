## Chapter 10: Scaling Quickly Without Losing Control

### The 2 a.m. transformer story (best "embrace chaos" anecdote in the book)
Suja's facility was buzzing at 2:00 a.m. filling growing orders when **the power went out.** The transformer was on the roof; the only person with a key was the landlord, asleep at home. So the production crew **climbed a tree next to the building, jumped from a branch onto the roof, and reconnected the circuit at the transformer** without waking him. Orders shipped on time. *"Our customers never knew what it took to get the product to them on time."*

> "The team knew they wouldn't get in trouble for trying, even if their plan hadn't ended so well."

### Embrace chaos
> "Whenever I spot a dog with its head sticking out of a moving car window, I am reminded of the early days at Suja. I was that dog, wide-eyed, and holding on for dear life."
> "If you're fortunate enough to grab hold of the tiger's tail, be prepared for a bit of a shit show."

**A normal day looks like:** marketing is going to print on new labels but ops still has an ingredient problem · the only way to get product to the customer is air freight, which destroys the margin on the delivery · you just found out your top mechanic and maintenance tech are moonlighting at a competitor.

> "Scaling isn't just growth with more zeros attached... Decisions that used to be reversible become permanent."
> "In the early days, hustle can compensate for inefficiency. When scaling, it can't."

### Decentralize for efficiency
The CEO's role shifts from **"ready, fire, aim"** to leading others and overseeing planning. Push decisions to the person nearest the decision. *"If information has to cycle up two or three different levels, communication can easily get lost."*

### Choose good over perfect
**The Expo East couple:** Jeff met a couple who started a juice company around the same time as Suja. Careful, meticulous, extremely risk-averse. Years later they were stuck at a fraction of Suja's scale. *"Their desire for perfection kept them from moving fast enough to seize opportunities."*

Suja went **from startup to nearly $70 million in three years** — *"not because every process or product was ideal, but because we focused on what mattered most, which was keeping the customer experience strong instead of trying to chase perfection."*

Voltaire: *"Perfect is the enemy of good."* Jeff's addition: *"But in startups, it's survival."*

> "When good people don't feel trusted by their leader to try things or suggest ideas, they move into fear. And where there is fear, there is no creativity."

On the Steve Jobs defense that perfectionists reach for: *"Who really wants to leave a path of interpersonal destruction behind them? Driving away talented people and hiring 'yes people' in their places quickly kills both culture and momentum."*

### Speed and agility as strategy
> "We operated like proverbial ducks in water: calm and collected above the water but paddling furiously beneath."

**Costco rotations.** The relationship with their buyer was collaborative enough that they could tell very early in a new rotational launch whether it would succeed. If initial results missed a velocity threshold, they'd **immediately begin an inventory wind-down and work with the buyer to build the replacement.** *"We earned trust by being the first to let them know when we needed to replace an offer."* (Chart in book: X = failed rotation, ★ = became an everyday item.)

**Trader Joe's celery juice, 2019.** Celery juice became a fast-moving trend after a boost on *The Rachael Ray Show*. TJ's typically preferred private label, but that would have taken far longer through their bureaucracy, so they did a third-party-branded project instead. **From first call to product on shelves nationwide: under six weeks.**

## Chapter 11: Building an Engine That Lasts *(TOC title: "Scale Through Systems")*

> "Many founders think massive growth solves problems. But growth magnifies them."

**Suja scaled across seven manufacturing locations in five years.** *"From the outside, our growth probably looked like pure speed. But speed wasn't the story. Discipline was."*

### Three strategic decisions that shaped everything: manufacturing, shelf life, supply chain

**1. Choose what to own — manufacturing.**
Suja processed **more than 1.5 million pounds of fruits and vegetables every week**, much of it harvested less than **twenty-four hours** earlier, and depended on **HPP**. Most co-manufacturers excel at shelf-stable products; very few are built for highly perishable ingredients moving at that speed.
> "Sometimes the most expensive decision in the short term becomes the least expensive decision in the long term."

**2. Solve the constraint that limits growth — shelf life.**
Launched in **thirty-five Whole Foods stores in 2012 with a 24-day shelf life.** Today those products average **roughly one hundred days**, sold in **more than 30,000 retail locations**, with **more than 50 million bottles produced each year.**
> "Shelf life determines far more than how long a product stays fresh. It influences where you can distribute, how much inventory you can safely carry, how far your products can travel, and ultimately how large your territory can become."
> "Every industry has a limiting factor like this... What constraint will eventually limit your growth if you don't solve it now?"

**3. Design the supply chain around your mission.**
Through **Greg Peyser** and **Russell Jack**, **Jack Family Farms** shared the vision of making organic produce more accessible — and **eventually became part of Suja.** That simplified the supply chain, lowered costs, and improved confidence in quality and supply.

**The democratization decision.** The original flagship sold for **nearly $10 for a 16-oz bottle** — fine at Whole Foods, incompatible with the mission. They created a **12-oz product at $3.99**, which immediately compressed margins: fewer ounces, dramatically lower price, still premium organic ingredients.
> "Our mission wasn't to build an exclusive premium brand. It was to make healthier nutrition accessible to far more people."

### Let scaling work for you — the margin arc
- For a while gross margins **hovered around 28 percent.**
- As the 12-oz product gained traction and volume increased, fixed costs spread across far higher volume.
- **Share of the cold-pressed juice category grew from roughly 5% to more than 45%.**
- **By the time Jeff left Suja, gross margins had climbed to nearly 50 percent.**

> **"Revenue is exciting, but gross margin is transformational."**

**Jeff's standing advice to consumable-product founders:** build a plan that gets gross margins to **~40% by end of year two** and **close to 50% by years three or four.** *"Higher margins reduce your dependence on outside capital, create more options, and allow growth to fund more of itself."*

> "Founders often obsess over top-line growth, but I advise people to pay just as much attention to the engine underneath it."

## Chapter 12: Winning the Shelf *(TOC title: "The Battle of the Shelf")*

### "What's a cat man?"
After a grueling battle to gain doors, Jeff said to Suja's head of sales **Nicky**: *"Wow, these past months were incredibly stressful. I can't wait until a couple of years from now when we achieve full distribution throughout the country and have nothing to worry about!"*

Nicky, aggressively: *"Two years from now, we'll be fighting tooth and nail to maintain our shelf space. We'll need the right cat man to win."*

Jeff: *"What's a cat man?"* — which triggered relentless teasing from his experienced team. (A **category manager** reads syndicated data and builds the case for a brand to maintain and expand its footprint.)

> "Imagine being a champion climber, clawing your way up a mountain, only to realize that reaching the summit was merely half the battle; staying there was a whole new and equally formidable challenge."
> "When you're the proverbial king of the hill... you transition from the hunter to the hunted."

**Retail vs. online, stated cleanly:** retailers already have shoppers coming to them, but you must get **the gatekeeper** to decide you deserve shelf. Amazon and your own site don't have that gatekeeper — but consumers aren't already coming to you, so you must advertise to move them there.

### The power of syndicated data
> "To a data enthusiast like me, opening a new Nielsen or Circana (formerly IRI) report felt like a kid walking into Willy Wonka's chocolate factory."

**Dwight Richmond**, early Whole Foods executive and natural-foods pioneer, explained that Whole Foods didn't expand cold-pressed juice because it was fashionable — **they expanded it because the data consistently showed consumers were embracing the category and that multiple brands could grow total category sales rather than cannibalize one another.**

> "Great founders do more than tell a great story. They tell stories supported by the data."
> "The best buyers aren't persuaded by charisma alone... What earns their confidence is objective evidence that your product will grow the category, not simply take sales from another brand."

**What syndicated data lets you analyze:** products (SKU → line → brand) · markets (retailer, channel, geography) · time frames (4 / 13 / 26 / 52 weeks — comparing near-in to long periods reveals trend direction) · facts and measures (dollars, units, promotions, velocity) · attributes (claims, sizes, package types).

**Cost reality:** large brands spend **millions annually**; mid-sized companies **hundreds of thousands** on subscriptions and specialized talent.

**Workarounds for startups without a Nielsen budget:** tap free or shared data resources and retailer summary reports · leverage specialty channel performance as proof when pitching larger retailers · gather insights organically via pilot launches, in-store demos, direct consumer feedback · **monitor category reset schedules by noting shelf-tag print dates**, talking to buyers, and watching planogram changes.

### The metrics that actually move buyers
Velocity (Units per Store per Week) · ACV Distribution · Number of Stores Selling · Volume per TDP and % ACV Max · Dollars per Point of Distribution · Percent Sold on Promotion · Panel-Based Shopper Insights (repeat rates, buyer penetration, switching).

> "Over time, I realized that data wasn't simply answering questions, but it was helping me ask better ones."

Jeff promoted **Jason Polinsky to category manager reporting directly to him** — *"one of the most valuable team members that I had when it came to scaling during my tenure at Suja."*

### Building trust with buyers
**Proactive transparency:** if a SKU wasn't performing, Suja raised it **themselves** rather than waiting for the buyer. *"Buyers appreciated that we were paying attention and thinking like partners, not just protecting our own shelf space."*

**Play their idea first.** When buyers had ideas that felt "out there," Suja often tried it their way. If it worked, great. If it didn't, it became much easier to revisit Suja's original proposal. *"Either way, working with buyers' ideas often bought us time on the shelf."*

**The clock:** once you're on shelf you have **roughly nine months to prove you belong**, less in highly competitive categories.

**The Rowdy Energy / Albertsons story:** they paid **nearly $1 million in one-time slotting to Albertsons.** Because velocity was significantly below hurdle levels, **they were discontinued after just eight months.** (Not the norm — normally you pay a single-case cost per flavor per door and get at least a year.)

### You're competing with the entire set
Not just Blueprint Cleanse or Evolution Fresh — **every product in the same planogram managed by that buyer.**

**Percentile vs. the retailer's median velocity:**
| Your position | What it means |
|---|---|
| 80%+ | You reign supreme; can potentially expand off-cycle without waiting a year |
| 60–80% | Likely able to increase SKU count at the next reset |
| 50% | Doing okay, but you may have other priorities now |
| 30–50% | You need a solid plan or you won't make the next reset |
| Below 20% | Something is seriously amiss; you probably won't last a full cycle |

## Chapter 13: The Art of the Pivot *(TOC title: "Innovation and the Art of the Pivot")*

### The board dinner confrontation
At a pre-board dinner, a family-style table of about fourteen people, a Suja board member asked:
> "Jeff, don't you see that what you think is disruptive innovation is just causing unnecessary churn?"

Jeff, calm but firm: *"We would prefer to have a single hero product like Red Bull or Coca-Cola instead of continuously innovating."*

The pushback he heard: *"Why are we innovating so much?" "We don't have the cash to properly market all these SKUs."* And those concerns weren't wrong — **they didn't have a hero SKU representing 30%+ of sales.** Instead: a cluster of solid performers and nearly twice as many SKUs that together sold about what one popular SKU did.

**The hero SKU rule:** *"If you don't have at least one SKU consistently outperforming the rest by 30% or more, you don't have a hero SKU yet, and you're leaving growth on the table."* A hero single-serve SKU often generates **an additional 100%+ in revenue through secondary formats** (larger formats, multipacks, adjacents).

### Market timing
Steve Jobs, Stanford: *"You can't connect the dots looking forward."*

**Early years: three national competitors** — Evolution Fresh, Blueprint Cleanse, Suja.
**Six or seven years later:** at an annual Whole Foods review, a buyer told them **she had 62 cold-pressed juice brands requesting meetings.** She couldn't possibly see them all.

### Build around your advantage
Owning manufacturing let Suja turn orders quickly without third parties, strengthen customer service, build responsive buyer/supplier relationships, and **innovate faster than competitors.** At first it was *"a bit of a boat anchor around our necks as it was significantly underutilized."*

> **"Coca-Cola's Stage-Gate process often required 16 to 18 months. We could move from concept to shelf in six weeks."**

Partnering with retailers on innovation increased enthusiasm, strengthened internal advocacy, deepened relationships, and let Suja offer **short-term exclusives.**

### Innovation creates a portfolio
> **"In Suja's first seven years alone, we launched 275 SKUs, averaging one new SKU every nine days. We also aggressively cleaned house, discontinuing all but 55."**
> **"Only one of our original top-10 SKUs remained in the top 10 a decade later."**

**Awards and recognition (2015 unless noted):**
- Whole Foods **Supplier of the Year** (2015)
- INC. 5000 **Fastest Growing Food and Beverage Company in America** (2015)
- INC. 5000 **13th Fastest Growing Company in America** (2015)
- Forbes **2nd Most Promising Business in America** (2015 & 2016)
- Prevention Magazine's **Top 100 Cleanest Packaged Goods**

**Famous pivots he cites:** Kutol wall-cleaning compound → **Play-Doh** ($8B brand) · Netflix DVD → streaming (once offered to sell to Blockbuster for $50M; now ~$240B) · WhatsApp status app → messaging ($19B to Facebook) · Burbn → **Instagram** · Western Union telegraph → money transfer → consumer finance → fully digital payments (1851–2026).

### Plan B before you need it
> "When launching a new product line, I almost always develop an alternative line in parallel. I call it Plan B, a plan quietly percolating in the wings. At least half the time, Plan A doesn't materialize the way you expect."

Plan B preserves shelf space, maintains retailer confidence, and buys time for awareness, trial, and repeat.

> **"Founders don't innovate because they know what's going to work. They innovate because they don't."**

## Chapter 14: Validation Through Strategic Partnerships (Coca-Cola, Part 1)

> "Partnerships aren't transactions. They're systems. They have incentives, power dynamics, cultural mismatches, and hidden tradeoffs that only become visible over time. If you don't understand those forces going in, you're negotiating blind."

### The personal frame
Jeff's fondest memories: the first warm days of summer, **painting the dock at the family lake house in Western New York with his father**, then swimming and boating all season. *"Those days were marked by hard work, time with my dad, and an ice-cold Coke at the end of it all."* Later he came to understand HFCS, excessive sugar, chemical preservatives, GMO ingredients — and the diet-soda myth.

### Why Coke made strategic sense
Coca-Cola was actively exploring newer categories targeting millennials, Gen Z, and Gen X. Their better-for-you history: **Minute Maid, Odwalla (90s), Honest Tea (early 2000s), ZICO Coconut Water, later LA Libations.**

**Where Suja was:** **$110 million raised**, **built and outgrown five manufacturing facilities**, **$100 million in revenue in six years.** They had proof of concept and distribution. They needed fresh capital and a strategic partner who could scale them without compromising what made Suja different.

**Coke's interest was multifaceted:** high repeat purchase rate signaling real loyalty · potential synergy with their struggling **Odwalla** brand · belief in the team.

**Relationships built long before the deal:** Jeff spoke at **VEB's annual team meeting** (Venture & Emerging Brands — formed in the early 2000s to identify and invest in beverage trends, operating like a disciplined PE firm). He negotiated with **Matthew Mitchell**, a VEB co-founder, and **Scott Uzzell**, its president. *"I even joked that if Pepsi had been the buyer, I would have had to hide my lifelong preference for Coca-Cola."*

### The two-step structure
Because Coke wasn't planning to acquire more than 50% at the outset, it was a **two-step deal.**

**Upside:** easier to close · partial liquidity for investors · founders keep operational autonomy longer.
**Downside:** ownership and control are split · teams and processes can't fully integrate · decision authority becomes unclear · **if the second step isn't contractually required, it may never happen** — leaving the company permanently between independence and acquisition.
> "Being half in and half out creates friction that founders need to anticipate."

**The deal, July 2015: Coca-Cola valued Suja at $300 million — a 4.7x multiple on trailing twelve-month net sales. Together with Goldman Sachs Private Equity and Coca-Cola, $150 million was invested for a 50 percent stake.**

### An internal champion changes everything
> "No deal survives without an internal champion on the acquiring side. There must be someone inside the larger organization who is willing to take on reputational risk... If no one is willing to carry that risk, more than likely, the investment will quietly die."

For Suja that was **Mike Saint John, then President of Minute Maid Juice** — warm, articulate, deeply knowledgeable. He described Minute Maid as an **autonomous entity inside the Coca-Cola universe**, which resonated immediately. *"Even a fraction of Coca-Cola's resources, paired with autonomy, would be transformative."*

### The moment of truth
They asked a leader in Coca-Cola's quality department a simple existential question: **was HPP a viable technology Coke would embrace and implement?** Suja's entire product depended on it.
> "If that answer had gone the other way, I'm certain our deal with Coca-Cola would have collapsed on the spot. Years of work hung in the balance based on a single opinion. This is why I say that deals don't hinge on logic alone; they often hinge on conviction."

### Doing the right thing at the hardest time (two stories)

**1. Pausing the deal over quality.** In the final stages of the deal with the world's largest beverage company, they identified a potential quality issue. **Jeff pressed pause.**
> **"There is no wrong time to do the right thing."**
> "Slowing a deal of that magnitude takes nerve. What it earned us, though, was something far more valuable than speed. We earned the respect of Coca-Cola's senior leadership. Integrity under pressure is visible. People remember it."

**2. Fighting for the angels.** Earlier financing gave certain investors a preference to take the first money out in a full or partial sale. Coca-Cola was committed to **$90 million** — but the vast majority would have gone to **ACG**, the private equity investor who had been in the deal **less than 12 months**, largely bypassing the people who had spent years building Suja. As CEO and co-founder Jeff found that unacceptable.

It was ACG's right to take the majority of the first slug — *"but it was also my right to fight to find another equity firm to secure the other $60 million."* After difficult negotiations they secured **Goldman Sachs' $60 million**, which allowed the angel investors who had been in for **three long years** to **sell roughly 40% of their holdings at an average 3.4x return while retaining 60% of their equity.**
> "Deals are not just about valuations. They are about fairness and morale."
> **Later, the angels got a second bite of the apple, and their total return was roughly 10x their investment.**

### Life inside a corporate giant
Innovation cycles got longer. Budgeting became exhaustive. **"Pre-meetings became a thing."**

**Real synergies:** aligning plastic bottle sourcing with Coca-Cola **significantly improved gross margins**; access to **Nielsen data at preferential rates.**

**Unmet expectations:** couldn't integrate Suja into Coke's food service accounts due to internal politics and non-fully-owned status. In an early meeting with **Publix**, Suja was **overshadowed by a large Coke delegation**, limiting direct engagement. *"This is the reality of partial acquisitions."*

### Backlash — "you sold out"
Some of Suja's most loyal customers accused them of selling out.

**Seth Goldman** (Honest Tea co-founder) had walked the road first: before selling to Coke, he made clear **his team would retain control over their product formula.** That stuck. *"Our mission at Suja was to democratize organic juice. That mission depended on product integrity and affordability. If we lost either, the deal wasn't worth doing."*

**John Foraker** (then former President of General Mills' natural sector) and Seth gave the same advice:
> **"Don't argue with customers. Invite them to watch."**
> "Tell them to give you a year. Ask them to judge you by your actions, not by their assumptions. If the ingredients change, if the quality slips, if the service suffers, they should let you know. Until then, stay visible and stay accountable."

> "We asked for time and accountability instead of blind trust."

### Credibility as the hidden return
> "The fantasy of instant riches and seamless global distribution never fully materialized. What did materialize was credibility."
> "We became a darling of the CPG world, not because we changed who we were, but because someone powerful validated what we had built."
> "Coca-Cola didn't validate Suja because we were perfect. They validated us because we'd spent years building a company worth believing in. **Strategic partnerships don't create great businesses. They amplify the strengths and expose the weaknesses that already exist.**"

## Chapter 15: Collaborative Retail Relationships

Opens on his father: *"He wasn't successful because he was the smartest person in the room. He was successful because he surrounded himself with people who were smarter than he was."*

> "If the consumer is the holy grail, retailers hold the keys to the kingdom. Nearly half of grocery purchases in the United States flow through a handful of major players."

**Suja built relationships with Walmart, Costco, Kroger, Target, and Whole Foods in a short period of time** — rare. Jeff and **Linda hosted homemade, Thanksgiving-style dinners at their home** with key retailers. **One memorable Target dinner featured a loaf of Challah bread designed with the Coca-Cola and Suja logos, made by Jeff's mother-in-law, Rachel.**

### Play to win together
Nicky's sales team embodied it: if a SKU underperformed, they didn't blame the buyer or hope — they **brought solutions** (swap in an innovation, adjust pack size or price architecture). *"The goal was simple: help the retailer win."*

Named team wins: **Lauren Soward** managed Target and Walmart while absorbing Trader Joe's after Jessa's departure without adding headcount. **Jody** navigated complex growth with Kroger and Publix through difficult internal seasons. **Gretchen** reinvigorated Whole Foods and helped launch Elevated Nutrients after hard lessons from products that missed thresholds.

> "Retailers want suppliers who make their category better. If you can do that consistently, you earn trust. And trust compounds."

### Sequence matters — build distribution intentionally
General order that worked: **Whole Foods → Target → Costco → Kroger → then Walmart.**

**Why Walmart last:** their Everyday Low Price model creates pricing tension across channels — products often sit **roughly fifty cents below traditional grocery pricing daily.** Go too early and you compress value perception and create friction with retailers who monitor price matching.
> "Retail strategy is not just about doors. It is about order."

### Every retailer wins differently

**Whole Foods — more than a P&L.** A seasoned CEO told Jeff early: *"Do not evaluate Whole Foods purely by traditional profit and loss metrics. It is also a marketing platform."* He was right.

After launching in the SoCal division, Suja expanded into **Arizona, Colorado, Northern California — then national within months.** *"Those were some of the most stressful weeks of my career. After all, when we launched we only had a twenty-two-day shelf life."*

**Less than six months after launch** they were working with the WFM grocery team to build the cold-pressed set at a more accessible price point → **the 12-oz Elements line at $3.99 alongside the 16-oz premium juices at $9.99.**
> "Most boards would not approve developing a new product line when you have less than one percent market share. But together with Whole Foods, we saw the category opportunity and leaned in."

Countless hours in **Austin** refining samples with buyer feedback → **Supplier of the Year out of tens of thousands of vendors.**
> "The lesson is not to chase awards. It is this: if you treat a retailer like a long-term partner and invest real time into building the category with them, extraordinary outcomes can follow."

**Costco — know the game before you play.** Their buyers understand your COGS in detail and collaborate aggressively to lower delivered cost because **volume is the engine.**
> **"While most grocery stores might sell $15 per store per week for a product, Costco expects roughly $1,000 per club per week."**

They operate in **regional divisions that individually rival national accounts elsewhere**, and their **13-week rotation model** creates a treasure-hunt dynamic. *"For us, that rotation model was a gift. It allowed us to test innovation without committing to a full-year reset. If it worked, we scaled. If it did not, we learned without burning the relationship."*

**Target — programs, visibility, and human relationships.** In **2015** Suja was invited into **Target's Made to Matter** program, which highlights emerging health and wellness brands — end caps, visibility, access to category leadership.
> "Every major retailer has programs like this. They may not call it Made to Matter, but they exist. Founders should actively seek them out."

**The oversized bed photo.** After getting to know the Target team, Suja invited them to visit the manufacturing facility — but instead of a plant tour and conference rooms, they encouraged them to **stay overnight and join a home-cooked meal made by Linda.** After cocktails and laughs, Jeff gave a tour of the house; because one room has an oversized bed, someone suggested they all pile in for a quick photo. *"It was harmless fun (and yes, my wife was right there with us)."*
> "It's hard to build real trust through Zoom calls and formal meetings alone. But when you share meals, stories, and genuine laughter, people start to see each other as partners, not just counterparts on opposite sides of a deal."

**Years later, pitching a new protein soda line, Jeff reconnected with the same buyer from those early days.** The trust opened the door.

**Walmart — collaboration under pressure.** *"Probably the most intense — and at times the most humbling — of all."*

They **intentionally delayed entering for several years**, then launched in roughly **half their stores.** It did not go smoothly: **sub-75-day shelf life** and premium pricing from all-natural ingredients meant **velocities came in lower than expected.** Over the next **four years** they churned through iterations — replacing slower SKUs, testing new ones, adjusting formats. Some worked. Many didn't.

> "Walmart didn't give up on us. And we didn't give up on Walmart. That mutual persistence built something valuable: familiarity."

**The smoothie trade.** Suja was pushing to expand distribution of their 2-oz wellness shots; Walmart hesitated and instead wanted a **Walmart-exclusive plant-based smoothie.** Suja's production team hated the idea — slow, complex — and they didn't believe it would work long-term. **They agreed anyway**, running a limited, operationally inefficient batch at unattractive economics **in exchange for expanding distribution of higher-performing SKUs.**
> "It was a calculated trade. We endured the buyer's passion project to unlock growth in the broader account. That decision paid off. Today, Walmart remains one of Suja's largest customers."
> "The lesson wasn't about winning every negotiation. It was about understanding leverage, thinking long-term, and making selective concessions to grow the whole relationship."

### Strategic navigation between retailers — the barcode move
Before launching in Walmart, **a senior Target buyer gave them valuable advice: don't use identical barcodes or pack sizes across retailers**, because Target would price-match Walmart's EDLP. Identical barcodes would have forced permanent retail compression.

**So Suja deliberately launched a 10.5-oz bottle at Walmart and a 12-oz bottle at Target.** More complexity, more coordination, more cost — but it preserved pricing integrity and let them scale Walmart alongside other retailers without sacrificing long-term profitability.

### The broker advantage
Suja's early Whole Foods launch wouldn't have been possible without **Bill Wieland and Presence Marketing.** Bill was selective about the brands he represented; his belief gave Suja credibility and opened doors. **At one Christmas party, after a few cocktails, Bill somehow left with Suja's Nutcracker decoration.** *"The memory of it still makes me laugh."*
> "When trust goes both ways, they become extensions of your team."

### What retailers taught me
Help the retailer grow the category, not just your brand · learn how each retailer defines success · play the long game · protect the broader retail ecosystem · keep your mission front and center · support every conversation with both data and enthusiasm · stay flexible as markets evolve.

> **"Retailers rarely become long-term partners because of one great product. They become long-term partners because they trust the people behind it."**

## Chapter 16: Coca-Cola's Fateful Decision

### The call
**July 3, 2018.** House full of family preparing for the Fourth. **Scott Uzzell**, President of Coca-Cola's Venture & Emerging Brands, called.

> "My heart pounded when I heard his voice. Then came the verdict: Coke would not be moving forward with the acquisition."
> **"When I hung up the phone and walked downstairs, I lost my composure in front of my boys and started weeping. I wasn't thinking about awards or growth percentages at that moment. I was wondering, 'How did I let us get to this place?'"**

**Six weeks earlier**, Coke's management and HR teams had visited to discuss what integration would look like and what it would be like to be part of the organization. *"People were genuinely excited at the prospect of that. We fully believed we were preparing for our next chapter together."*

**Why they walked:** they wanted higher gross margins; they believed Suja was still early; **leadership had changed since the original 2015 deal, and new executives are rarely eager to inherit decisions they didn't originally sponsor.**

Suja had **grown nearly 18% in the prior twelve months and over 50% since Coca-Cola's initial investment.**

**The morning after:** *"I woke up to the sound of fireworks and the annual parade going on outside. It was one of those perfect summer mornings with blue skies, about eighty-two degrees, not a cloud in sight. And yet, I felt sick to my stomach. For a split second, I wondered if it had been a nightmare."*

### The harsh reality — the math
- **On track to lose close to $10 million on about $100 million in revenue.**
- **Nearly $40 million in secured debt coming due in October.**
- **Burning more than $10 million a year.**
- **Gross margins below 32 percent.**
- Strategic partner just walked away.
- **Todd Fisher, CFO**, and Jeff lived inside weekly cash projections. *"At times, we were operating with less than $100,000 in the bank."*

**Coke and Goldman Sachs, their primary lenders, extended the maturity and provided an $11 million bridge loan** — buying six months to stabilize.

**The comparison that stung:** Suja went zero to $100M in six years — **faster than KIND, ZICO, Honest Tea, and Annie's at comparable stages.** *"Those companies took ten to fifteen years to reach sustainable profitability. They grew more slowly and burned less cash. They learned the lessons gradually that we had learned all at once."*
> "The speed at which we grew had masked some problems... Once Coke walked away, there were no more places to hide."

**Board dynamics changed.** After the legal and financial agreements with Coke and Goldman, governance shifted. *"For the first time in my career, I found myself operating without that same sense of collective confidence."*
> "Running a company through a crisis is hard enough. Doing it while questioning whether you still have full board backing is something else entirely."

### From disruption to discipline — the turnaround plan
- Cut marketing by **more than $4 million**
- Eliminated low-margin outsourced products
- Reduced inefficient manual assemblies
- Shifted toward higher-margin shots
- Launched a **$4 million raw-material cost program**
- Reduced operating expenses by **more than $3 million**

> "Nothing was sacred. We rebuilt the business from the inside out."
> **"Then we realized something surprising. After cutting marketing in half, revenue grew by about 10 percent in 2019. That taught me that strong brands with loyal customers don't always need massive spending to grow."**

**Honest note on financial engineering:** *"I learned during this period how easily income statements can be shaped. With enough cost-cutting and product-mix optimization, you can 'engineer' EBITDA profitability. We'd done that, but the real work was making sure it was sustainable."*

Focus areas: cash flow management · margin discipline · data-driven decision-making · SKU-level profitability · inventory turns · channel performance. *"Data became our compass. We used it to identify where money was leaking, where growth was real, and where we were fooling ourselves."*

### Why big-brand/small-brand deals are hard
> "Large companies are like large ships, and turning the ship requires immense time and force."

**The freshness paradox:** Suja's strength was that *"the juice you buy off the shelf could have literally been in the ground just one week ago."* Consumers loved it. But **harvesting weekly can scare a company the size of Coca-Cola** — a freeze or uncontrollable heat could render the produce non-viable. It hadn't happened, but it could. *"I suspect that this factor was among the concerns that impacted Coca-Cola's decision."*

**Size matters.** The president of Coca-Cola's North America division affirmed their growth and their ability to reach **~$120M in annual revenue** — but a final acquisition would require being **above $200M.**
> "I understood, yet in that moment, I felt like the goal posts had been moved on us."

### The margin lesson (the book's central financial argument)
> **"Our gross margins were below 30 percent, which is not sustainable for a scaled consumer brand."**
> "If you are building a business in CPG, you need a clear path to at least 40 percent gross margin by the end of year two. Fifty percent is even better because it gives you breathing room."
> "Margin is not just an accounting metric; it is strategic leverage. Margin funds marketing and innovation. It absorbs shocks and reduces dependence on outside capital."

**Over eight years they raised $115 million.** *"With stronger margins earlier, the amount of funding we needed could have been dramatically lower... When your product does not generate enough gross profit dollars to fund your ambition, investors must fill the gap. And eventually, those investors start asking harder questions."*

**The irony:** much of the margin depression came from owning manufacturing — *"and years later, as the business fills out the capacity, the profitability tends to soar because you're able to absorb your overhead and avoid the expense of co-packers and their required margins."*

### Growth is not enough
> "For a period, the market rewarded growth at almost any cost."
> "Capital markets change. Investor risk tolerance shifts. What is acceptable in one fundraising cycle becomes unacceptable in another."

**Eleven fundraising rounds in seven years**, each bringing higher expectations. *"Later, especially unexpected rounds, create fatigue and compounded skepticism."*

### The summer everything tightened
The category was softening. **Retailers had over-proliferated the refrigerated set** — *"when too many brands crowd into limited shelf space, the result is a promotional arms race. Consumers get trained to buy only when something is discounted, and category dollars shrink instead of expanding."* Cold-pressed juice was suddenly being described as **"peaked."**

> **"I have never successfully sold or financed a company from a position of weakness, and this place where we found ourselves was especially vulnerable."**

That Fourth of July, Jeff and Linda did what they've done many times: stepped back and got perspective.
> "Once again, I was reminded that the real problems are the ones money cannot fix."

### Survival mode
Objective changed immediately from top-line growth to **cash survival.** Deep budget cuts. **Greg Rose, CMO**, managed and communicated the marketing reductions with professionalism. **Bella Tumini, Marketing Manager**, executed with fewer resources. **Jason Polinski, Category Management**, sharpened mix and profitability.

> **"Even during this period of intense cost control, our sales increased by 9 percent. Constraint had created clarity and worked to our advantage."**
> "Founders often hesitate to cut aggressively because it feels like retreat. However, we discovered that disciplined cost control sharpened our focus and improved execution."

### Innovation as a lever
Since **2014** they'd been developing **2-oz wellness shots.** Head of sales **Nicky Vidaurreta** had pitched them to buyers repeatedly over the years. **In the summer of 2018 she secured a critical win by swapping Suja's kombucha shelf space at Costco into the wellness shots line** — improving velocity and margin mix at a pivotal moment.

> "If fewer than half your SKUs consistently meet retailer velocity requirements, you are wise to continue refining your portfolio. **Innovation is not churn if it improves turns and strengthens economics.**"

They also used **targeted, time-bound exclusives** — often 3–6 months, sometimes by channel rather than nationally — to secure distribution and carry additional SKUs along with the innovation.

### Operational discipline
**Bottom-up budgeting** replaced aspirational forecasting, similar to larger CPG companies. Predictability improved dramatically.
> **"Hope is not a forecasting model. I have always believed luck is found at the intersection of preparation and opportunity. If you are not operationally ready, opportunity does not wait long."**

**By April and May of 2019, for the first time in company history, they saw a credible path to annual profitability** — after eight years in the industry.

### The down round
**Eight months after the Coke news, they recapitalized through a painful down round — a "cram down."** Valuation significantly reduced; governance provisions tightened.
> "For the first time in my career, I felt the loss of full board confidence."
> **"Boards support strategy while performance supports confidence. When performance weakens, control increases. This is not personal, but it is structural. Founders are wise to understand that dynamic before they experience it."**

## Chapter 17: A Pivot in Action (the wellness-shot pivot that saved Suja)

### The fortune teller
**2014.** Linda visited a fortune teller named **Tara** — usually about the kids or personal insights. This time Tara predicted that **the product that would ultimately create the most value for the company would be wellness shots.** At the time it made no sense: they hadn't launched any shots; Bryan had only just begun experimenting with a few early formulations. *"Still, the comment stuck with me."*

### Building the category
Bryan focused on formulations while Nicky pitched retailers. An early concern was **placement** — if retailers treated shots as an afterthought and kept moving them, customers wouldn't find them.

Competitors **VIVE** and **KOR** launched their versions first. *"That actually helped us. We could watch how the category was forming and see how intense the sensory experience needed to be for consumers to believe the products were effective."*

**Whole Foods** eventually agreed after several attempts — but **placed them in health and beauty**, a completely different category from the juices.

**Costco** was already selling a competitor's shot in Southern California — sweeter, more mainstream, performing well. When Nicky pitched, **the buyer proposed a head-to-head test between the two shots — with a catch: he also wanted Suja to develop a plant-based milk for his region.** None of them had experience producing plant-based milk. **They said yes anyway.**

**Pea protein** has a notoriously harsh, *"stiff, cardboard-like"* flavor. The plant milk struggled.

**But the shots took off.** At Costco they quickly hit **~$2,000 per week per club.** If that held across a broader rollout it was a **$15 million annual opportunity** — and **the shots replaced kombucha on that shelf, significantly improving margins.**

Simultaneously they expanded with **Trader Joe's**, where they already produced private-label cold-pressed juices; private-label wellness shots were a natural extension.

**They entered third behind VIVE and KOR — and within a year captured more than 30 percent market share.**

### The Kombucha Lesson
> "Just because you can produce a great product doesn't automatically mean you should."

Technically their kombucha was strong — **they had solved one of the category's biggest challenges by maintaining consistent alcohol levels.** But the market didn't care. **Consumers associated Suja with cold-pressed juice, not fermented tea.**

**Shots were different: essentially a more concentrated extension of cold-pressed juice, a category where Suja already had credibility.**
> "Expansion strengthens a brand when it builds on what you already do well, while random expansion can weaken it."

### How wellness shots quietly saved Suja (the numbers)
- Gross margins had hovered around **32 percent** for years. They kept pushing for growth assuming scale would fix it. **It didn't.**
- **Kombucha was outsourced: ~12 percent gross margin.**
- **Wellness shots, produced internally: ~60 percent gross margin.**
- **Stopping kombucha and leaning into shots moved company-wide gross margin from ~32% to ~40%** — largely from that one shift.

> **"You can cut expenses all day long, but you usually can't cut your way to greatness."**
> **"Gross margin is everything."**
> "Sometimes the difference between surviving and thriving is replacing the wrong product with the right one."

### Why the category worked
> "If someone wants the benefits of ginger or turmeric, the traditional option is to buy the root, wash it, juice it, and drink it. But most people aren't going to do that. Drinking a two-ounce shot of concentrated juice is just easier."

**The inversion of the mantra:** the juice line followed *health without the punishment.* **Shots worked differently — consumers *expected* them to be more intense, and that intensity signaled the ingredients were doing something. "They ventured closer to health with a little punishment. And consumers swarmed."**

Consumers didn't care about ashwagandha, lion's mane, or ginseng. **They cared about the outcome.** *"So instead of explaining ingredients, we focused on communicating benefits."*

### What product shifts taught them
> "Small shifts in product mix can have massive consequences for a business."
> "Bring new consumers into the brand, not just ask existing customers to follow you somewhere unfamiliar. When we launched kombucha, our customers didn't follow us. But wellness shots were built on the foundation of cold-pressed juice, a product already trusted."

> **"As it turns out, the fortune teller was right."**
