## Chapter 18: The Moment of Truth

### A quiet search for a buyer
By early 2019 they had stabilized and began preparing for a potential sale. On paper they were improving — shots were still early, margins improving but not fully proven.

**They hosted management presentations with more than fifteen potential buyers. None made an offer.**
> "That didn't surprise me. We were still a work in progress. Sometimes when you're doing the right things, you just have to keep going."

### The turnaround
- **2018: lost about $10 million on an EBITDA basis.**
- **2019: generated positive $3 million.**
- Gross margins improved materially; revenue streams diversified so they were no longer dependent on a single product or channel.

> "Diversification gave us resilience. We didn't suddenly become smarter; we just became more disciplined."
> **"Being 'left at the altar' forced honesty. It forced us to confront weaknesses we had been rationalizing and forced alignment between capital, operations, and strategy. And in the long run, it made us stronger."**

### Stepping aside
**By May 2019** Jeff had brought the company back to a strong position. *"I would never have left without reaching profitability. But I was exhausted. Eight years of eighty-hour weeks had taken their toll."*

> "Stepping down as CEO was the hardest professional decision of my career. I was walking away from something I had built with my own hands. But I also knew the company needed a different leader for its next phase."
> "My instincts were shifting back toward growth. The board's priorities were stability and debt reduction. Both were valid, they just weren't aligned."

**Bob DeBrode joined in August 2019.** Jeff **officially left in January 2020.**

### Two years later
**In 2020, Suja was sold to Paine Schwartz for approximately $300 million.**
**Today the company generates more than $350 million in revenue with roughly 10 percent EBITDA margins.**

> "Coca-Cola's decision felt devastating at the time. In hindsight, it was a gift. It forced discipline. It forced rigor. It forced maturity."
> **"Sometimes leadership doesn't look heroic. Sometimes it looks like spreadsheets, uncomfortable cuts, hard conversations, and patience. That's what resilience looked like for us."**

## Chapter 19: Personal Reflections on Leading a Startup — The Good, the Bad, and the Ugly

**The most exposed writing in the book.** Frame: ABC's *Wide World of Sports* — *"the thrill of victory and the agony of defeat."*

**Opening note on his kids:** two of his kids started their own consumer businesses. **Both ultimately failed and had to close.** *"As a parent who is used to being in charge, it was incredibly painful to watch my kids struggle and stress through a business. Yet the skills they gained are worth more than the most expensive MBA on the planet."* He told them: **"You've been given a gift you'll have for the rest of your life."**

### The Good
- The rush of seeing your product on shelves for the first time; a consumer posting about loving something you created.
- *"You get to be your own Michelangelo, painting the culture of your company and building your own version of a Sistine Chapel."*
- **"Suja years":** *"At Suja, we gained about seven years of traditional business experience in a single year."*
- *"One of my greatest joys is speaking with early-stage founders. I sometimes walk into a room with nothing prepared except one invitation: Ask me anything."*
- *"The 'Babu' insight I share isn't because I'm exceptionally smart, but because startups force you to learn quickly."*
- **Jobs created:** nearly **350 employees** at the height during his tenure — *"well over a thousand family members were supported by those jobs."*
- **The Presence Marketing story (most emotionally effective "good" moment):** during one of the fundraising rounds, **Bill Weiland created an incentive program letting his Presence employees invest a portion of their retirement allocations in Suja.** Jeff was nervous about it at first. **After the company sold, a mid-level Presence employee came up to him at a trade show and thanked him profusely — the money she made on her Suja investment put both of her kids through college.**
- Relationships forged *"in what feels like a wartime environment... That kind of experience ties people together for life."*
- *"I often joke that I'm one degree of separation from almost anyone in the CPG world, but in truth, that isn't necessarily an exaggeration."*
- **Financial upside:** *"Entrepreneurship can allow you to leap multiple socioeconomic levels in a single career. Growing up in a middle-class family in the Midwest, I expected to do well in life, but I never imagined generating millions of dollars in personal equity over three decades."*

### The Bad
- **"Over the past thirty years, I estimate that nearly half of my time has been spent raising money."**
- **"Of the 30 years I've spent building startups, probably only five of those years were spent actually making money."** Most startups lose money for three to four years.
- Retail is unforgiving: relentless outreach, constant rejection, slow and expensive approval cycles, costly slotting fees.
- Operational surprises: cost increases, supply-chain disruptions, packaging errors, recalls, poor hires. *"My million-dollar kombucha costing error was one such example."*
- **The two brothers:** *"I am friends with two brothers who have both invested in my ventures over the years. One has only invested in my winning companies, while the other has only invested in the losing ones. One of the reasons I wrote this book is to help founders avoid some of the quicksand traps I've encountered — the kind that impacted one of those brothers far differently than the other."*
- **The retirement regret:** *"One financial lesson I wish I had followed earlier in my career is this: after every successful exit, I should have set aside at least 10% of my after-tax proceeds in a retirement account I couldn't touch. Startup careers rarely include traditional retirement programs. As I approach retirement age, I do occasionally worry about long-term financial security."*

### The Ugly
- *"I know founders who became so consumed by their companies that they ended up divorced and splitting time with their kids."*
- **On Linda:** *"While I may be the one making decisions about the business, Linda shares the risks with far less control or visibility. As we get older, every new venture is essentially a bet on portions of our joint retirement."*
- *"I have missed dinners, had to cut vacations short, and couldn't attend more parent-teacher meetings than I wish... the quiet guilt that comes from not always being present for my children crept in often."*
- **Generational echo:** *"My dad traveled constantly for work, and I ended up much closer to my mom as a result. As I get older, I sometimes wonder whether my own kids will feel the same distance from me."* And: **"My parents ended a 25-year marriage in part because my father wanted to pursue a risky venture later in life, while my mother wanted stability."**
- **3 a.m.:** *"There are nights when you wake up at 3:00 a.m., staring into the dark, worried about payroll, investors, or whether the company will survive."*
- **The physical toll:** *"Think about how U.S. presidents look when they enter office versus four or eight years later. They always look dramatically older. The same effect can happen to founders."*
- **The rehab admission (the most exposed passage in the book):**
  > "Ironically, while running a 'better-for-you' beverage company, I neglected my own health. After working eighty-hour weeks for nearly eight years at Suja, I was completely burned out. I was drinking far too much alcohol. **In fact, after leaving the company, I spent a month in rehab to reset my life and health.**
  > Linda and my family told me they believed that if I hadn't stepped away from Suja when I did, I might not still be here today."
  > "Had I managed the balance better, I might still be the CEO today, and preparing to take the company public. **That lost opportunity still sits somewhere in the back of my mind.**"
- *"With hindsight, I strongly encourage founders to put their own oxygen masks on first... I admit I still need to do better about heeding my own advice here."*
- **The energy drink over-investment:** *"When my energy drink company was failing, I invested several additional chunks of cash trying to keep it alive. Before I knew it, I had invested nearly a million dollars in the company's final year. Optimism is part of being an entrepreneur, but I should have been more disciplined."*
- **Identity risk:** *"If the company fails, your sense of self can unravel. If it succeeds, ego inflation can become a risk."*

### The verdict
> "Human nature tends to forget the bad and remember the good. Most founders I know say they would do it all again."
> "Watching something move from a napkin sketch to a product on store shelves is a rewarding experience unlike anything else I've ever known."
> **"Even now, at 64, I'm still searching for that once-in-a-lifetime opportunity to build something so meaningful that one day my grandchildren might say their grandfather created something that truly made a difference."**
> "I'm a startup junkie. And if I had the chance to do it all over again, I absolutely would — just hopefully a little wiser the next time."

## Chapter 20: Babu's Field Guide for Startup Entrepreneurs (15 rules)

> "I've built companies that scaled. I've built companies that didn't. The lessons below were paid for in equity, dilution, sleepless nights, and more than a few expensive mistakes."

1. **If It's Not Great, Nothing Else Matters.** In consumer products, taste is king. In software, usability. In services, experience. *"You cannot out-market a mediocre product."*
2. **Be Obsessed With the Customer, Not the Idea.** *"Founders fall in love with ideas. Winning companies fall in love with customers."* Know who they really are, what they actually value, what problem they're paying to solve, what alternatives they're comparing you against.
3. **Make Sure the Math Works.** *"Hope is not a strategy. Revenue without margin is ego."* Does scale improve the economics, or just magnify the losses? *"If the unit economics don't work, you don't have a growth problem. You have a business model problem."*
4. **CPG Distribution Follows Relationships.** *"People do business with founders they trust. Trust accelerates distribution."*
5. **Use Innovation as Leverage, Not Decoration.** Should open doors, unlock bigger contracts, create switching costs, differentiate. *"If your innovation doesn't move the needle, it's expensive noise."*
6. **Earn Your Spot Every Day.** *"Shelf space. App store ranking. Enterprise contracts. Investor confidence. All of it is rented."* Until you see real traction, assume you're on probation. *"Stay paranoid in a healthy way."*
7. **Build a Team That Creates Productive Tension.** Operators who've done it, builders who break things, realists who protect the downside, optimists who see around corners. *"If everyone thinks the same, you're blind. If no one aligns, you're stuck. The goal isn't comfort. It is performance."*
8. **Maintain a Bias for Action.** *"Speed compounds. So does indecision. Make the call. Adjust as you go."*
9. **Build Community Before You Need It.** *"You cannot buy authentic loyalty. You earn it through value and access. Community creates nonlinear growth. Paid ads alone rarely do."*
10. **Own the Customer Relationship.** *"If you don't own your customer data and relationship, you're building on rented land."* Invest early in direct channels, recurring revenue, customer insight.
11. **Attention Is Fragmented. Adapt.** *"If you don't naturally live in the new channels, hire someone who does. Founders who cling to old playbooks get passed."*
12. **Partnerships Only Work If They're Real.** *"If the relationship is cosmetic, the market will know. Authenticity scales. Optics don't."*
13. **Persistence Is Powerful. Discipline Is More Powerful.** Stay in the fight when customers love the product, the economics can work, and the problem is execution not demand. *"But don't confuse grit with denial. Sometimes, the most disciplined move a founder can make is to force reality early rather than finance hope."*
14. **Don't Let Enthusiasm Write Checks Your Business Model Can't Cash.** There's always someone better capitalized, better connected, bigger. *"Emotions and ego build bloated companies. Humility builds adaptable ones."*
15. **Build Your Own Playbook.** *"Founders who win in the long term aren't the ones who never get knocked down. They're the ones who evolve faster than the environment around them."*

## Chapter 21: Fatal Flaws and Recoverable Mistakes

> "Over the years, I started keeping a private list I call 'fatal flaws.' It's not something you'll find on Google. It's a collection of hard-earned lessons, many of which were paid for in cash, stress, lawsuits, dilution, and near-misses."
> "Some mistakes slow you down. Some cost you money. Some end companies. The difference matters."

### LEGAL

**1. Contracts lasting longer than 1 year.** *"Multi-year agreements can trap you in bad economics long after they stop making sense."* Prevent: don't sign >12 months when possible · track renewal dates centrally · calendar alerts 90 days before termination windows · negotiate opt-out language upfront · **run all contracts through a large language model for concerns.**

**2. Contracts that auto-renew (evergreen).** *"If a supplier is trying to invoke this, even if it's in the agreement, it's an unethical way to do business. These vendors often don't remind you in hopes that you will forget."* Most evergreen contracts are signed when you're rushed, understaffed, inexperienced, or generally optimistic. *"They don't hurt you right away. They hurt you later, when you've likely forgotten about it and need flexibility most."* **Jeff today: "3-month maximum contracts are all I sign these days."** Also: centralize contract authority (one person — CFO/CEO/COO) and keep a shared contract calendar reviewed monthly.

**3. Personal Guarantees.** *"One of the most common ways founders quietly put themselves in long-term financial danger."* **Personal guarantees survive bankruptcy, business shutdowns, and restructuring.**
> **"You will be tempted, but don't do it! I've skirted it for 40 years, and you can too!"**
Prevent: prioritize equity over early debt (*"Dilution is usually cheaper than risking your personal balance sheet"*) · strengthen fundamentals first · use asset-backed financing · use **PO financing** (20%+/yr sounds expensive but is rarely outstanding more than ~3 months — requires discipline) · negotiate caps, time limits, automatic removal after milestones · bring in credible institutional investors to lower perceived risk · maintain strict personal/business separation · **"A demand for a full personal guarantee is often a warning sign"** · use founder-focused advisors · create leverage before borrowing.

**4. Trademark and Brand Name Conflicts.** *"Your brand name is not just a creative expression. It's a financial asset."* Note the retail trap: with a large number of total points of distribution, **a name change may be treated as a new SKU** — new slotting, new shelf tags, an execution nightmare the retailer controls, not you. Prevent: comprehensive searches before launch · experienced IP counsel early · **federal registration, not just state** · avoid descriptive names or near-misses · think globally · check adjacent categories.

**5. Inaccurate or Non-Compliant Labels.** *"Yesterday's ambulance-chasing law firm has been replaced by today's class action litigators who look for slight non-compliances with your label and wait until you are large enough that their settlement return is acceptable to them."*

**6. Document substantiation.** *"A surprising number of founding partners never sign your formation documents."* Finalize equity agreements early; document vesting and roles; review annually.

### LEADERSHIP

**7. Founder Over-Dilution.** *"Early on, equity feels cheap. Advisors, consultants, and friends all get small pieces that add up and become expensive later."*
> **"If a company sells for $200 million, and the founder only owns 4% of the company, that's $8M before taxes, after ten years of work."**
> "Ownership means influence. When your stake shrinks, so does your voice. You can 'win' financially and still lose control and upside."

| Healthy dilution | Dangerous dilution |
|---|---|
| Raising at increasing valuations | Giving away more than 5–10% to the management team too early |
| Not diluting existing shareholders more than 10–15% per round | No clear equity strategy |
| Giving equity to people who truly add value | Messy cap table |
| **Maintaining 50%+ at exit (founder or founding team)** | Single-digit ownership too soon |

**8. Bad Partnerships or Wrong Early Hires.** *"A misaligned co-founder or an executive who doesn't act consistently with the company's culture or vision can do more damage than a failed product launch."* Before forming a partnership, talk through worst-case scenarios: what if one of you wants out · what if one underperforms · how equity vests · which decisions require unanimity.
> **"Always use vesting. Equity without vesting is a time bomb."**
> "Hire for the stage you're in, not just because of someone's resume."

**9. Damage to Relationships with Family and Friends in Business.** *"I've seen siblings stop speaking over equity misunderstandings. I've seen friendships fracture because one person expected sweat equity to convert into control. I've seen parents invest money they couldn't afford to lose."*
> **"Relationships are worth more than equity. Protect them accordingly."**
> **"Never take money from someone who can't emotionally and financially afford to lose it."**
> "Don't give someone a leadership role because you trust them personally. Only give them responsibility because they're qualified."

**10. Bad Advice.** *"Success has a thousand authors, and failure is an orphan."*
> "Some advice is priceless, but some is downright dangerous. The hard part is that they often sound identical or come from people we genuinely care about."
> "Early-stage founders are especially vulnerable because uncertainty creates insecurity. And insecurity looks for certainty, even if it's coming from an unreliable source."
Filters: triangulate with two other proven people · consider the source (*"They may have been at a massively successful company, but were they truly in the room?"*) · get a neutral second opinion when advice feels aggressive or misaligned · learn to detect **artificial urgency**.
> "Everyone giving advice has incentives. Investors want growth. Consultants want engagement. Friends want to feel helpful. Your job is to filter noise from the signal."

### FINANCE

**11. Running out of cash.** *"Cash flow kills more startups than competition."* Maintain **12–18 months runway** · monitor burn weekly · **begin your next raise when you have at least four months of runway remaining.**

**12. Charging business expenses to your personal credit card.** *"Several times over the last 20 years, I personally have gotten stung with unreimbursed business expenses or credit cards with no business ability to repay."* Turn in expenses promptly, especially when cash is plentiful.

### SALES
**13. Outsourcing control of your sales efforts.** Maintain internal leadership; work with brokers; hire month-to-month fractional sales members if needed.

### MARKETING
**14. Hiring the wrong Head of Marketing.** *"Misaligned marketing can stall growth and take years to potentially unwind. It can also cost you a fundraising round."* **"I love hiring temporarily unemployed marketers and try them for three months."**

### OPERATIONS & SUPPLY CHAIN

**15. Not owning your own formulas.** *"Here's a painful one: you think it's your formula… until you find out it isn't."* Some flavor houses and co-packers quietly retain ownership. **"A VC or strategic will never buy you if you don't own your formulas."** A flavor house often *does* own the flavor — that's okay — but you should have a path to secure it via volume or buyout, **and they should never own the formula.**

**16. Product Recalls.** *"A recall during the first few years or during a fundraising round can be catastrophic."* Invest in QA · audit suppliers · maintain lot traceability · experienced FDA counsel · know your HACCP plan.

**17. Ignoring regulatory and securities law.** Violations halt fundraising and trigger penalties.

### The Founder Quarterly Audit (Costly But Correctable)
Run every quarter / 90 days. Six sections:

- **Legal & Governance:** confidentiality & invention assignment agreements signed by all employees and contractors · equity grants board-approved and documented · **83(b) elections filed within 30 days of issuance** · option grants comply with current valuation requirements · no legacy contracts with hidden guarantees or off-balance-sheet obligations · clean, current cap table.
- **Financial Hygiene:** model reflects real distributor deductions and promo accruals · top 3–5 profitability drivers identified · distribution and freight intentionally classified (COGS vs. OpEx) · payment terms reviewed with top suppliers in past 6 months · P&L by account reviewed before accepting new retail placements · gross margin tracked monthly against target · cash runway clearly understood.
- **Sales & Channel Strategy:** core SKUs show consistent velocity before expanding distribution · launches justified by data, not enthusiasm · channel sequencing aligns with positioning · promotional calendar strategic, not reactive · slotting fees negotiated and capped deliberately · **after year two or three, review weak-margin products and cull** · no major account added without contribution margin analysis.
- **Operations & Supply Chain:** co-man certifications verified and current · **co-man facility visited within the past 12 months** · ingredient and packaging costs itemized in contracts · inventory aligned with forecast · capacity commitments realistic · **retailer delivery performance tracked and above 95%** · supplier pricing benchmarked within past 12 months.
- **Founder Discipline:** founder not operating as a bottleneck · outside expertise engaged where gaps exist · core revenue drivers remain the focus · expansion supported by data · emotional decisions separated from operational decisions.
- **Team:** true performance issues assessed · cultural disconnects assessed · outside expertise engaged.

**Three final questions:** Where are we leaking margin? Where are we overextended? What can we fix now before it compounds?

## Chapter 22: The Universal Principles For Any Startup (10)

1. **Start With Truth, Not Excitement.** *"Eventually, every business is audited by reality. Customers don't reward passion. They reward value."*
2. **The Market Is the Judge.** *"You don't get to declare your product a success… the market does. The market doesn't care how hard you work. It simply responds to whether what you built matters."*
3. **Momentum Changes Everything.** *"When a business has momentum, problems feel manageable... When momentum stalls, even small issues feel existential. Forward motion creates options. Options create resilience."*
4. **Cash Is Oxygen.** *"You can survive a flawed product, a poor hire, or a strategic mistake. You cannot survive running out of cash. Growth without discipline is just speed. And speed without a runway is dangerous."*
5. **Gross Margin Creates Freedom.** *"Your economics are not a back-office detail. They determine how much strategic freedom you have when the pressure rises."*
6. **Culture Multiplies Outcomes.** *"I've never seen a strategy outperform the quality of the team executing it."*
7. **Growth Magnifies What's Already There.** *"Weak systems don't survive growth. Thin margins don't stretch. Cultural issues only get louder as the stakes rise. Growth doesn't create strength. It reveals whether you ever truly had it in the first place."*
8. **Timing Often Matters More Than Being First.** *"You don't have to be first to win, you simply have to be ready."*
9. **Adaptation Is Not Failure.** *"The mission can stay steady while the path to accomplishing it adapts... That flexibility does not signal weakness or inexperience, but wisdom."*
10. **It Takes Longer Than You Think.** *"If the business becomes your entire identity, every delay or setback will feel personal and permanent."*

**The closing synthesis:**
> "When I strip away the complexity, the capital structures, the negotiations, and the headlines, entrepreneurship is not mystical. **It is simply disciplined execution through uncertainty.**"
> "Rather than rushing a product to market to keep your preferred timeline, focus on **learning faster than your competitors.**"
> "Entrepreneurship isn't just creating, it's protecting cash, protecting culture, and protecting momentum long enough for something durable to take root."
> **"So listen to the market without letting it define your identity."**

## Chapter 23: The Journey & The Destination

### Mt. Rainier
**More than 30 years ago**, flying into Seattle late one night on business, a bright full moon illuminated the **14,411-foot summit of Mt. Rainier.** *"The sight of the mountain's shimmering silhouette through the airplane window captivated me. It was so magnificent, I couldn't help but stare! Right then and there, I promised myself that I would stand on that summit one day."*

**Less than a year later, he climbed it.** That first climb began a thirty-year mountaineering life that took him to **all seven continents**, **six of the Seven Summits**, **five summits reached.**

**As he writes the chapter he is en route from San Diego to Kathmandu** for Everest — *"The locals refer to it as 'Chomolungma' or 'the Goddess Mother of the World.'"*

### The Amazon piranhas
On another flight, from São Paulo to the U.S., he flew over the Amazon and saw the brackish convergence where saltwater meets fresh — *"often referred to as 'salt and pepper' or 'oil in water.'"* Staring at the river below, he decided **he would one day catch piranhas there. In a sturdy boat, of course.**

**In less than a year he was standing on the banks of the Amazon catching piranhas in the dark.** *"The detail I hadn't factored in was the monkey on my back. And I'm not talking about a figurative monkey. But at least he seemed friendly."*

### The argument
> "I could never stand on a summit without the long, challenging journey that would inevitably test my endurance, my strength, and my resilience. And as breathtaking as every summit has been, the steps it took to get there shaped me and built my stamina."
> "I wouldn't dream of fishing in piranha-infested waters or climbing to breathtaking summits without guides, proper tools, careful planning, and contingency plans."
> "Learning to 'raise my hand' and risk failure to avoid mediocrity is a decision I'll keep making again and again."
> "And in many ways, the role of 'Babu' that my kids gave me so many years ago, is the one I've tried to play for you throughout this book. **Someone who walks ahead, pointing out the cliffs, the switchbacks, and the places where the view is worth the climb.**"
> **"You don't wait until you feel ready to start the climb."**
> **"You don't have to be perfect to be prepared."**
> **"Both in business and in life, the summit may be the goal, but the real transformation happens on the climb up."**
> "So raise your hand. Take the step. Start the climb. And maybe one day, many years from now, you'll look back on the mountains you've climbed — and smile when someone calls you Babu."

## Epilogue: Ringing the Bell (written last — the emotional payoff of the book)

> "I finished writing this book in early 2026, expecting it to end where Chapter 23 ends — on a mountain, with an invitation to start the climb. Then, just as the final manuscript was heading to the publisher, **the company I co-founded fourteen years ago in the ice closet of my partner's nightclub rang the opening bell at Nasdaq.**"

**On a May morning in 2026, Suja Life went public under the ticker SUJA at a valuation north of $700 million.**

> **"Ringing that bell had been a lifelong dream of mine. I wasn't there to ring it."**
> "Paine-Schwartz, the current owners of the company, cited 'policy' in declining my request to attend. I won't pretend that didn't sting. Thirty years into this work, the entrepreneurial journey still finds new ways to teach you something. Some lessons just don't come with a warning label."
> "So let me tell you what I felt instead, sitting in San Diego, watching from a screen as the team I once led raised their hands above their heads in Times Square. **Mostly, I felt grateful.**"

### How we got here
> "Fourteen years ago, a small group of us had a wild idea: take a dark green juice that barely had a three-day shelf life and turn it into something the world would embrace. **We almost lost it three times.**"

Eleven fundraising rounds in seven years. Coke declining its option, leaving $40 million in debt to repay in six months. *"There were Mondays when we sat down with less than $100,000 in the bank, modeling cash flow line by line because that was all we had left to model."*

**Eighteen months before that call from Coke, almost as if by instinct, they had launched the two-ounce wellness shots** — ~60% gross margin vs. the ~12% on outsourced kombucha. *"At the time, the shots felt like an experiment. In hindsight, they were part of what made today possible."*

> **"The decision that saves you is almost never the one you made on the day you needed saving. It's usually one you made eighteen months earlier, on a Tuesday, when nobody was watching."**

### Who got us here
> "There is no bellringing in New York City without the **400 families** who built Suja with us."
> "And there's an even longer list of OGs no longer with the company whose fingerprints are all over this moment. **The ones who took the leap when Suja was three guys, an idea, and a juice that turned brown after three days.** Your efforts were never lost on me. I will forever be in your corner, cheering Suja on."
> "For me, though, the moments I find myself returning to most often aren't the headlines. They're quieter than that. **The drive home along the Pacific as the sun went down. Pulling into the driveway and being met not by a CEO's title, but by a wife who knew me before any of this and four kids who only ever called me Daddy.**"
> "Those were the moments I'd ask myself, half out loud: **How did I get this lucky?** ... I'd usually settle on some combination of luck, timing, grit, grace, and people who believed in me before I'd earned it."

### What I want you to take from this
> "If you've made it this far in the book, you've already done the hard thing. You've sat with someone else's wins and losses and let them inform your own thinking. Most people don't."

**First: the bell, if it ever rings for you, is not the point.**
> "The bell is a moment. **The work is the point. The people you build with are the point. The decisions you make on Tuesdays when nobody is watching are the point.** If you do those things well for long enough, sometimes a bell rings. Sometimes it doesn't. **Both outcomes are honorable if the work was honest.**"

**Second: even at the summit, the climb keeps teaching you.**
> "Not being invited to ring my own company's bell taught me something I needed to learn at sixty-four that I couldn't have learned at thirty-four. **Build the company, but don't be the company. Love the work, but don't be the work.**"
> "The version of you that exists outside the title is the one your family signed up for — and the one you'll need when the title goes away, whether you hand it over or it gets handed away."
> "At sixty-five, I'm still a startup junkie, and the climb is still the thing I love most."
> **"When those bells ring — and they will — the OGs are absolutely getting a front-row seat."**
> "And if you're reading this from your own ice closet, your own first sketch on a napkin, your own moment of staring into the abyss with $100,000 in the bank and a payroll due Friday — **start the climb. I'll be cheering for you too.**"

Signed: *"With immense gratitude, Jeff aka 'Babu' — San Diego, California — August 2026"*

## Appendix A: A Founder's Field Guide to Essential Retail Metrics

Framing: *"When I first entered retail, I hadn't yet learned the language of retail. Terms were tossed around like ACV, velocity, panel data, and dollars per point of distribution as if everyone should know what they meant."*

### Retail Dashboard
| Metric | Definition | Jeff's Take |
|---|---|---|
| **Units per Store per Week (USW)** | Avg units each store sells weekly | **"Shelf space is rented every week. Velocity is your rent payment."** |
| **ACV Distribution** | % of total retail sales represented by stores carrying your product | "Distribution gets you into the game. It doesn't guarantee you'll stay there. **Never celebrate new distribution until it's producing strong velocity.**" |
| **Dollars per Point of Distribution** | Revenue per new point of distribution | **"Adding stores isn't the goal. Adding productive stores is."** |
| **Gross Margin** | Revenue minus COGS | **"Revenue gets the headlines. Gross margin gives you options."** |
| **Percent of Time Sold on Promotion** | % of time your brand is on promotion | "I used to think promoting less than your competitors was a good thing... I didn't understand that by allowing this I was giving my competitors an unfair velocity advantage and essentially tying one hand behind my back." |
| **Percent Sold on Promotion** | % of sales generated while discounted | **"Promotions should create trial, not dependency."** |

### Understanding Your Customer
- **Consumer Panel Data** — *"While store data tells you what happened, panel data tells you why. And why they'll come back."*
- **Household Penetration** — *"You can't build repeat customers until you've earned first-time customers."*
- **Buy Rate** — *"Great brands don't just attract customers once. They become part of people's routines."*
- **Repeat Rate** — **"Your first sale belongs to your marketing. Your second sale belongs to your product."**
- **Purchase Frequency** — *"The more often customers return, the less you'll have to spend convincing new ones to try."*

### Understanding Distribution
- **Numeric Distribution** — how many stores carry you. *"More stores create more opportunity — but only if the product performs once it gets there."*
- **Weighted Distribution** — how important those stores are. *"Winning the right retailers is often more important than winning the most retailers."*
- **Total Distribution Points (TDP)** — shelf presence. *"Shelf space is valuable real estate."*

### Pricing & Promotion
- **Average Selling Price (ASP)** — *"Don't confuse higher sales with a healthier business."*
- **Price Elasticity** — *"Before lowering your price, ask whether you're solving a pricing problem or masking a value problem."*
- **Incremental Sales** — **"Retailers don't reward replacement. They reward growth."**
- **Promotion Lift** — *"If your sales disappear the moment a promotion ends, you've built a discount strategy instead of a brand."*

### Category Management
- **Category Reset** — *"Treat every reset as if you're pitching your business all over again."*
- **Planogram** — **"Sometimes moving six inches on a shelf can have a bigger impact than spending thousands on advertising."**
- **SKU Rationalization** — *"Sometimes your strongest strategy is simplifying your assortment so every SKU earns its place."*
- **Cannibalization** — *"Innovation should expand your business, not simply shift sales from one of your own products to another."*
- **Incrementality** — *"Buyers don't reward products that shuffle dollars around the shelf."*
