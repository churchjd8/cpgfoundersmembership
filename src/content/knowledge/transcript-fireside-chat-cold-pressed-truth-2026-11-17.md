**[15:46] Joshua Church (reading Omar's question):** What is the distribution secret when it comes to CPG? Is it spend? Who has the bigger purse? You often see new products on the shelves and you sit there shocked that they got on the shelves. What's the secret?

**[15:46] Jeff Church:** The secret, as one smart CPG person told me who's co-moderating this, is that it's not about getting on the shelf, it's about getting off the shelf. And I grossly underestimated that. It's actually a lot easier to get on the shelf. Once you're on the shelf, you're a target for people, and you have to have great velocity to stay on the shelf. Velocity and repeat rate are probably 2 of the most important things in CPG. How quickly is your product turning? People make the mistake all the time of getting distribution. Revenue by distribution, equity funds, investors, they don't value that, because that doesn't mean you're going to be able to retain it and stay on the shelf. It's velocity. The number of turns that you're doing per week or per month or per year for that SKU is whether or not you can hold that territory.

**[15:47] Joshua Church (reading Mike Gerber's question):** What company throughout your career has been your favorite and which was the most challenging?

**[15:48] Jeff Church:** The most challenging was a company called Universal Building Products, which was a construction-based company in Chicago that I bought. It was a big company, a $250 million business. And within 10 days of acquiring it, there was an explosion in the plant and a fatality, and I had to spend about 6 months in Chicago without coming back. It was at the peak of construction spending in 2006. It was like rearranging deck chairs on the Titanic a little bit after that. It was an awful situation. And that wasn't even a food product, it was a construction product. So when I got into Nika Water, which was our first CPG brand, and then Suja, I knew I was going to be a saint about food safety, because it's the last time I'm ever going to be in a situation like that. So that's the most challenging.

**[15:49] Jeff Church:** One of the ones that I really loved is a business called Lynx Professional Grills, which was a really great business, a small business doing about $10 million in revenue. It was outdoor barbecues like a Viking, and this was in 2006, when everyone wanted to build outdoor kitchens. I bought it. It was kind of teetering on bankruptcy. I was able to restructure it. These were big, heavy outdoor stainless steel grills, and I took all the guts to China and I kept all the outer shell in the US, and did a lot of innovation. We were the first brand to bring the Ruth's Chris sear zone that you see when you go to restaurants like that into the grill. We brought in these little recessed lights to shine down on the knobs. We brought in the indoor range technology that is the push and turn versus having to hit the flint thing to get the thing to ignite. And I just loved it because it was just so creative. That was my first consumer experience.

I call it cul-de-sac innovation. With product, my 4-year-old grandson is my best taste tester because he doesn't have any filter and he's not going to worry about hurting my feelings. He's going to tell me exactly what he thinks. With the construction products, all the stuff's up in the ceiling where you can't see or touch or feel it. People really aren't motivated by it. But with CPG and consumer, people love talking to you about the product. Lynx, I bought for $10 million and sold two and a half, three years later for about $35 million, and just had a great experience with it.

**[15:50] Joshua Church (reading Bob's question):** How do you reinvent a brand to sustain growth?

**[15:51] Jeff Church:** The top 10 SKUs for most any business today weren't even in the top 10 SKUs when that founder started that business. That's true for Suja. It's true for most brands I know. People, especially millennials and Gen Zers, want that innovation. They don't want repetitiveness. They want to see change. They want to see flavor innovation. They want to see different platforms. I probably get accused of churning a little bit, meaning doing too much innovation, and some of it's probably warranted, but most of it's not. Because in CPG, until you're turning velocity-wise at least at the mid-level point for that retailer, and you've got the gross margin that you need to sustain your business, you're not safe.

**[15:52] Jeff Church:** So I'm always having a plan B and a plan C. That can drive people crazy because it's work and it's effort. But you don't know what's going to hit. I think one of the greatest skills for a founder is the ability to pivot and to know when to pivot. Because if you pivot too much and when it's not needed, you get churn, and churn isn't good. But if you pivot at the right time... had we not pivoted at Suja to shots, there would be no Suja going public or anything like that. But to get to shots, we went through probiotic waters, we went through vinegars, we went through kombucha, and then we got there.

**[15:53] Jeff Church:** Maybe if we were smarter, we could have gone from A to Z, but you just can't. Because you just don't know what's going to work. So I think it's the ability to pivot, and to recognize that whatever you're doing today isn't going to be what you're doing tomorrow, and to instill that into your team. Especially when you're growing so fast, people don't like to change. But you have to educate your team that what you're doing today, if we're successful, is not at all what you're going to be doing a year from now, because we're going to be growing. And that's the best thing about it, because with growth comes structure, more people, and for the people that come in earlier, it gives them an opportunity to lead and step up.

**[15:53] Joshua Church (reading Bella Tumini's question):** You talk a lot about the joy of working with your family. Can you share which member of your family has been your favorite to work with?

**[15:54] Jeff Church:** I'm going to have to say Bella Tumini. Bella is my niece who has been with me, other than defecting for a little while, since she graduated from college. Family done well is great. Family done bad is awful. It's just about having clear expectations, I think, with family. One of my favorite stories, Joshua, you know this well, is watching Monday Night Football one night and we were short-staffed at Suja. I said, Joshua, I need to take you and Nina. I think you guys were probably 15 or 16. I need a couple of bodies in here to work the cold presses. And you're like, "ah, Dad, I'm watching the football game." I'm like, yeah, you'll be back by the 4th quarter. He didn't come back by the 4th quarter. He came in at 6 a.m., completely covered in beet juice. And that's when he said, "Dad, I don't think your hobbies and your stuff are the things I want to do." And now he's gone on to do all the exact same things I've done. So be careful what you wish for.

**[15:56] Joshua Church (reading Nicola's question):** Having launched and scaled multiple CPG brands, how do you decide which channels to prioritize, and what signals tell you it's time to double down, change course, or walk away?

**[15:56] Jeff Church:** Traditionally in CPG, most founders are not coming up with Coke or Pepsi type products. They're coming up with better-for-you type products. There's some functionality, some need state it's addressing for the consumer, whether that's energy, whether that's liver, whatever it is. You can't just launch a Coke product because it's saturated and you won't be able to compete. So most new founders are launching things in the natural food channel. There's a traditional process where you launch at a Whole Foods or Sprouts or something like that.

**[15:57] Jeff Church:** And then from there, when you get a couple retailers where you're turning at the right velocity levels... in order for you to be able to raise institutional capital, private equity, VC money, you have to be able to demonstrate that you've got good velocity in Middle America in grocery. So after the natural channel, you go to this series of H-E-B in Texas, Publix in the Southeast, Harris Teeter in the Southeast, Wegmans in the Northeast, Meijer in Michigan. You go to some cross-section of that, and if you can demonstrate that you're doing median velocity in those stores, then investment capital comes, and then you go more into grocery. But you don't go to the Piggly Wigglys and places like that because you're not going to get the velocity numbers and the really critical data.

When we launched Proda, we launched it with one retailer. That's the thesis of James Richardson's book that I mentioned earlier, Ramping Your Brand. And I made this mistake before, where I went into 20,000 doors, including 14,000 7-Elevens, because I kind of gamified the system on a 100-store test. They're like, "wow, these velocities are insane. We want to push you out everywhere." Okay, great. They put us out everywhere and they're like, "Jeff, what happened? You're turning like 70% less than what you were turning in the test." You just don't have the marketing budget to be able to get the product off the shelf.

**[15:58] Jeff Church:** So when we started Proda in April, it's a protein soda, amazing, try it out, it's at Sprouts right now but we'll be going much broader in April, we said we want to partner with one retailer who's going to lean in with us and who we can lean in with. So we gave them a year exclusive, which some people would say is stupid, but they leaned in heavy with us and gave us both ambient placement and cold placement. In CPG it's your Nielsen numbers, your syndicated data. When you go through the cash register and you give that loyalty card to the cashier, that data gets captured and then it gets sold back to brands, and the brands use that as a competitive weapon. We wanted to have the fastest turning protein soda in the country.

**[15:59] Jeff Church:** And we got that. We'll stay in Sprouts and continue to build there and lean in, because we both love each other. But we will also go into other areas because we need to build the business.

A lot of these retailers are taking brands earlier now than they used to. A Costco and a Walmart, for example, typically wouldn't come online until much later, but they're both making concerted efforts to bring in these earlier stage brands because they want to get that sizzle. They know that's what brings in the consumers. So today that's changing a bit. If you aren't on top of your game and you go into a Walmart... they've done a lot better over the years, one of my dear friends is one of the heads there, they're bringing early-stage brands on, but it used to be a disaster. In fact, we're taking a product in there next year, it looks like in April or so. It's about knowing the route to market and how you're going to get there. Like, you don't go to Sam's before you go to Costco, or Costco won't take you. There are some rules you need to learn through the process. And if it's a product that's not for natural but a convenience store product, then you go convenience store. But most of the time with convenience stores, because it's so much of a smaller footprint, their velocities have to be so much greater to hold the shelf. You've got to be turning much faster, and you don't have enough consumers buying your product in the beginning.

But because of that, I earned a massive amount of credibility, and my team earned a massive amount of credibility with Coca-Cola. We only have our good deeds in life. I look myself in the mirror and I feel pretty darn good. I've done some stupid things too, but I feel pretty darn good about that.
