## Chapter 1: The Founder's DNA

### The Coca-Cola call (the book's cold open)
- **5:00 p.m., July 3, 2018.** Six years after Suja started as a small juice cleanse experiment.
- Coca-Cola had taken a **30% minority stake in 2015** in the then three-year-old company.
- Suja was the fastest-growing CPG brand in the country, on "most promising company" lists, growing **20%/yr for the three years after Coke's investment**.
- 300+ employees waiting. Investors watching.
- Key line: *"But I would discover that big moments are rarely about celebration; they're about exposure."*
- *"You could see the thick haze of stress on our faces as we waited for the news, making us look and feel 20 years older than we really were."*

### Three Seconds That Changed My Life (the spine of the whole book)
- Starting tight end, **Lakewood Rangers** varsity (Cleveland, OH). No wins junior *or* senior year.
- 9th game senior year: caught three passes including a TD — better than usual for him.
- 4th quarter, near the opponent's goal line, down by less than a touchdown, seconds left. QB rolled out; primary receiver covered. **Jeff was wide open in the end zone and did not raise his hand.** QB got sacked. They lost again.
- The reason was fear — of dropping it, of letting the team down, of being blamed.
- He replayed those three seconds for **twenty years**.
- **Age 38.** Third child Jacob just born. Choice between corporate security (Wall Street track) and an unknown startup. 2:00 a.m., flipping channels, he lands on an interview with retired CEOs asked what they'd change. Every one of them said the same thing: they wished they'd been bolder, taken more chances, chosen fulfillment over safety.
- *"At 38 years of age, I was now far more afraid of mediocrity than of failure."*
- Asked himself: "What's the worst that can happen if I fail?" Answer: experience, lateral thinking, deeper business understanding, and a well-paying C-suite job. "That really wasn't a huge downside."
- **He raised his hand.** Mom and sister urged him on. Linda — Moroccan immigrant — "whose risk tolerance makes Richard Branson seem risk averse."
- *"Courage doesn't eliminate fear. Sometimes it simply refuses to let fear make the final decision."*
- Timeline note: the football moment was **forty-seven years ago** relative to the writing.

### Track record
- **Eight businesses started or acquired** across the 27 years after taking the plunge. Currently building the **ninth: Proda, a rapidly growing protein soda.** Six businesses came before Suja.
- **$212 million raised. Nearly $700 million returned** to angels, family offices, and PE firms. **Median return 3.3x.** Five home runs, three strikeouts.
- *"I often wake up in the middle of the night with deep remorse and second thoughts about the three companies and the investment capital lost."*
- *"Learning from failure is a brutal, yet unavoidable reality for successful entrepreneurs. You can only succeed if you're willing to fail."*

### What makes a founder
- *"Great companies rarely begin with the best ideas. They begin with the right founder."*
- *"Successful founders aren't born, they're made."* Not IQ, wealth, fame, or personality type.
- *"Great founders are not fearless. They simply reach a point where the fear of standing still outweighs the fear of moving forward."*
- *"Over time, courageous choices compound into something that looks like instinct but is actually earned experience and muscle memory. That is what I mean when I refer to founder DNA."*
- **Six key traits (all learnable):** intellectual curiosity, resilience, bias for action, financial discipline, integrity, the ability to learn.
- Framing shift: not *"Do I have what it takes?"* but *"Am I ready for all that being a founder will require of me?"*

### Formative background
- Grew up in **Cleveland, Ohio**. "My first operating system was loaded and hardwired in the Midwest."
- Dad was **CFO of an international industrial company**; loved working summer jobs in his dad's plant — "to this day love the smell of cutting oil."
- Dad and his partner (the CEO) did a **leveraged buyout in the 1980s: $250k each to acquire a $100 million company.**
- Middle-class family. Preferred sports over academics. Parents valued character: *"They were prouder of me for being the person who would befriend the new kid at school ... than they would have been if I made straight A's."*
- Halfway through high school realized he had to apply sports-level discipline to academics.
- **The language of business:** started with a college accounting class → grueling internship doing mind-numbing audits → fluency in cash flow, CAC, margins, fundraising, ops, hiring, execution. *"Every compelling business idea must translate to numbers. And numbers never lie."*

### The closing turn of Ch. 1
> "While waiting for Coca-Cola's decision, I thought they were evaluating our products, our financials, and our market position. But in reality, they were evaluating something much deeper. Every hire we'd made. Every culture decision. Every operational discipline. Every mistake we'd learned from. Every difficult conversation we'd chosen not to avoid. In other words, they weren't just evaluating Suja. They were evaluating the founder and the team behind it."

> "Success isn't built on avoiding failure. It's built on learning from it faster than everyone else."

## Chapter 2: Pre-Launch Preparation

**Core posture:** *"Dream boldly. Plan soberly."* Most founders are glass-half-full; the healthiest keep their heads in the clouds and feet planted in logic.

### The readiness questions (ask, then hand to someone who'll tell you the truth)
Industry fluency · Will passion survive setbacks (early excitement usually fades at 12–18 months as cash dwindles) · Capacity for the work (5–10 balls at once) · Alignment with life circumstances · Tolerance for ambiguity · Support system (spouse/partner alignment is essential) · Honest read on strengths and weaknesses (*"Believing you can do it all isn't confidence. It's a liability."*) · Financial risk management · Comfort raising capital · Personal investment.

### Hard numbers stated here
- Most CPG startups need **$200,000–$350,000 of meaningful early capital** to launch, then future rounds of millions over years.
- Every founder should make **at least a small personal financial investment** — skin in the game is a signal to other investors. Don't wipe out your savings.

### Taste
- **Consumers choose taste above everything else 93% of the time.** Jeff finds only about half the products he tastes acceptable.
- **Repeat Rate** — % of consumers who try and repurchase within 12 months — *"is probably the most important metric in all of CPG."* Amazon/DTC gives you a good read after ~3 months. **Below 10% → survey consumers and dig in.**

### Timing / differentiation
- *"You don't need to be first. In fact I really like being second or third because you can learn what #1 and #2 did right and wrong. What you don't want to be, however, is sixty-first."*
- *"Products are copyable. Brands are not."* If the answer to "what makes us hard to copy" is "nothing," pause.
- *"While true differentiators are disruptive, small shifting of the playing field or getting the jump balls to fall your way are the same small shifts that make Vegas print money."*
- Fads spike and collapse; durable categories build steadily and reward discipline.
- Also: the market must be large enough that **someone will want to buy this business in five years** and still have room to grow it.

### Preparation increases endurance
*"Entrepreneurship is a marathon, not a hundred-yard sprint."* Founders tell him they're burned out at 90 days. He's been at it 30 years.

**Tools:** Online Entrepreneurial Readiness Test (50 questions), askBabu.ai, The Founders Club (300+ founders on WhatsApp).

## Chapter 3: Pre-launch Roadmap with Timeline and Budget

### The HACCP story
Launching Suja, a Whole Foods buyer asked if they had a HACCP plan. Jeff: *"What's a HACCP plan?"* He assumed it was a one-page compliance form. It's Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points — a structured FDA-recognized food safety system. Within a week he was in an all-day food safety course learning how many ways a beverage company can unintentionally harm people. *"Ignorance is expensive in any business, but especially in the CPG industry."*

### The realities
- **Timeline:** assume **9–12 months** to launch from scratch. "Blow and go" risks overspending, missed details, and limping to the starting line exhausted.
- *"The first five years of your life as a founder will feel like running a marathon made up of repeated 100-yard sprints."*
- **Budget:** most first-time CPG brands cost **$150,000–$350,000** to get on shelf; realistic average **~$250,000**.
- **The Rule of Twos:** everything takes twice as long and costs twice as much as you expect.

### The 8-phase Pre-Launch Roadmap (assumes $250K, Jan 1 start → Dec 31 launch)
| Phase | Focus | Budget | Milestone |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Market, white space & incrementality | $10,000 | Clear articulation of your USP |
| 2 | Company foundation and the basics | $10,000 | Legal structure + partner alignment before major spend |
| 3 | Product formulation & research | $25,000 | Finalized specs meeting regulatory + consumer standards |
| 4 | Branding, positioning & packaging | $40,000 | Trademark-cleared name, production-ready packaging files |
| 5 | Co-manufacturing & 3PL search | $25,000 | Signed co-man agreement + 3PL before production |
| 6 | Go-to-market preparation | $40,000 | Clear channel strategy + retailer outreach plan |
| 7 | Investor model & fundraising | $20,000 | ≥25% of target raise soft-committed before full outreach |
| 8 | Preparing to launch | $80,000 | First production run complete, initial shipments scheduled |

**Sharp operational notes buried in the phases:**
- Retailers only want products that **grow their category** (incrementality), not replace what already sells.
- C-Corp structured for **QSBS** can create future tax advantages.
- *"Make sure that the product tastes amazing — this is the one thing that most founders only get about 70% right."*
- **Own your formula.** If you use a flavor house and don't pay them directly, they'll likely own the *flavor* — but they should never own the *formula*.
- If your co-man also sources ingredients, demand a **complete cost breakdown including their margin %** — otherwise you can never optimize.
- Use a lawyer who has drafted a CPG co-man contract before.
- Getting your **Amazon page** right materially changes revenue; hire someone who's done it multiple times.
- Financial model must be **bottom-up over five years**, not a revenue goal backed into. Deck: **20–25 slides.**

### The human side
*"If your identity becomes fully tied to the business, your mood and happiness will rise and fall based on daily sales reports."* Founders become controlling when stressed, even over team members with more experience. *"No startup is worth losing yourself or those you love."*

## Chapter 4: The M.A.P. Framework (Market · Audience · Positioning)

> "Most startup failures happen before the first dollar is ever invested. Founders fall in love with ideas that should never become businesses."

Thirty-year-old framework. Especially critical in CPG "where shelf space is finite, velocity is measured weekly, and competition is relentless."

### M = Market
Jeff's own confession: early on, asked what market Suja was in, he'd have said *"the juice business."* Half-right, and *"half-right can be dangerous."* The real answer: **a premium organic wellness juice playing in the fast-growing cold-pressed segment of the broader beverage industry.** Before that clarity they risked underpricing, pitching the wrong retailers, and benchmarking against commoditized mass juices with higher velocity and lower price.

**The Russian nesting dolls:**
- **Industry (TAM)** — e.g. Beverages / Food & Beverage. US non-alcoholic beverage ≈ **$161B**.
- **Category** — e.g. Non-Alcoholic Beverages.
- **Segment (SAM)** — e.g. Juices.
- **Niche (SOM)** — e.g. organic cold-pressed juice wellness shots.

**Five things to research at each level:** market definition · previous year's market size · five-year CAGR · % of the larger market · key competitors.

**Reference numbers used:** cold-pressed juice sub-segment ≈ **$1.5B**; cold-pressed ≈ **10% of the broader juice segment**; functional beverages growing **7–9% annually** vs. broader beverage ~5%. Niche competitors named: Vive Organic, KOR Shots.

**Triangulation:** when you lack citable sources, ask several knowledgeable people the same question. Wide variance = low reliability; convergence = solid read. Tools: Google, ChatGPT Deep Research, brokers, Statista, askBabu.ai.

**Jeff's ideal segment to enter:**
- It's new but consumers already understand it (cold-pressed juice → shots)
- Retailers see growth and are expanding availability (small footprint, high sales dollars)
- The leaders have made some mistakes (limited initial offering)
- There's room to improve something meaningful (additional benefit states)

> "If you're too early, you burn cash educating the market. If you're too late, you fight for scraps."
> "You don't need to own the ocean. You just need to own a well-defined pool."

### A = Audience
> "'Health-conscious consumers' is not an audience. It's a placeholder."

**Suja's bullseye:** *"A health-conscious millennial woman, 25–40, living in a metropolitan area, who shops at Whole Foods or Trader Joe's, does yoga or CrossFit, and is willing to spend on wellness. She's looking for convenient ways to boost her health because she's busy with her career."*

**Five steps:** paint the ideal customer · identify the job to be done (Clayton Christensen, *Competing Against Luck*) · understand emotions and decision drivers · understand the decision-making process · use real customer insights.

- **Badge value:** "being seen as a healthy person, the kind who carries a green juice into a meeting as a lifestyle statement." Seeing that behavior around your brand is a good sign.
- Suja's marketing tapped positivity ("drink plants, feel great") plus a bit of FOMO.
- Shelf decisions happen in seconds — packaging and first impressions carry huge weight. **Sampling** was the key conversion lever: *"Will I like the taste enough to pay $4–$5?"*

**"The truth of the business is at the shelf."** — Jody Cnossen. Jeff initially wasn't sure. *"Over the years I've realized that he's 100% right. That is where your ultimate consumer is. And they are likely less than a couple of miles away from you right this second."*

**Real demo quotes they heard doing wellness-shot samplings:** "Is there sugar in this?" / "Oh, it's organic? Great!" / "$3 for a tiny shot?!" / "What does it do exactly?"

**Free insight source:** competitor Amazon reviews. "Tastes awful," "cap leaks in my gym bag" = your opportunity.

**Suja's actual audience profile:** health enthusiasts and lifestyle consumers, mostly (not exclusively) women in their 20s–30s, educated, employed, willing to invest in health; follow wellness influencers; experiment with vegan/paleo; want immunity, detox, energy; had tried or knew about juice cleanses. **Pain point: wanting genuine nutrition but lacking time to juice at home.** Skeptical of big corporate juice brands with sugar and hidden ingredients — so Suja positioned as an authentic, small-batch-feeling alternative and worked to keep the farmer's-market ethos while scaling.

> **"Health without the punishment"** — the internal mantra. Not a marketing slogan at first; a reflection of behavior they were seeing.

> "You can market your way into trial, but you cannot market your way into loyalty."

**Define your audience tightly enough that you know:** where they shop · what they do with free time · what else they buy · why they choose you · what would make them leave.

### P = Positioning
> "What makes our solution the 'only' or 'best' option for our customers' exact needs?"
> "If you can't capture it in a single line, your messaging isn't sharp enough."

**Suja's USP:** **HPP (High Pressure Processing)** — truly fresh (not heat pasteurized, which kills nutrients) *and* safe with a commercially viable shelf life — combined with **USDA organic**. At the time most juices weren't organic; those that were organic or raw had a three-day shelf life unless frozen.

**Claimed positioning:** *"fresh-juice-bar-quality in a bottle, available affordably at your grocery store."* Never on the label; it was the concept. Differentiated them from Naked Juice (natural but heat pasteurized, not organic) and from the local juice shop (super fresh but not convenient daily).

**The formula:** *"We're the only [X] that [Y] for [Z]."*
Example: *"Suja is the only juice brand that delivers raw, organic nutrition with a realistic shelf life, for health-conscious consumers on the go."*

**Suja's one-liner:** *"the ultra-premium organic juice that makes healthy living easier."* Internal joke version: *"a Whole Foods juice bar in a bottle."*

**Four resonance tests:** The Stranger Test · The Ideal Customer's Reaction · The Memorability Test (ask them to repeat it back later) · Testing Validation (Pickfu.com — ~100 demo-matched respondents for ~$100; discount close results, trust lopsided ones).

Signal they were landing it: *"So it's like the fresh juices you'd get at a spa or health store, but ready-made?"* Signal they weren't: *"Is it like Naked Juice?"*

> "Clarity trumps cleverness, but if you can have both, great."
> "Positioning is the bridge between brand and economics. Consistent clarity directly impacts velocity, and velocity earns you shelf space, which ultimately builds the real value of any CPG enterprise."

**MAP Scorecard:** 9 questions, 1–5 each, 45 max. 0–10 Foggy · 11–25 Getting There · 26–40 Sharp · 41–45 Crystal Clear. Plus a plug-and-play ChatGPT/askBabu "Instant MAP Analysis" prompt.

## Chapter 5: Stage-Gate Process

> "Gates filter weak ideas. Stages grow the surviving company."

Adapted from the corporate Stage-Gate process big CPG companies use to kill weak ideas early. In a startup it protects something more valuable than capital: **time, energy, focus.**

> "Passion, while super important to have, can also be one of the most dangerous things a founder brings to a business if it isn't paired with discipline."

**The five stages every idea must survive:**
- **Stage 0 — Conviction and Advantage.** Is there a real problem? Do we have a real advantage in solving it?
- **Stage 1 — Minimum Viable Brand.** Can this product actually exist as a business?
- **Stage 2 — Proof of Demand.** Will people buy it *again*?
- **Stage 3 — Scale Readiness.** Can the business handle meaningful growth?
- **Stage 4 — Growth Inflection.** What is the most effective lever to scale?

**The four gate decisions:** Go · Hold · Recycle · Kill.

> "That last option is the hardest for founders... But in reality, a timely kill is often a strategic win."
> "This process is not about how much you love an idea. It's about surviving a gauntlet designed to kill it."
> "It replaces blind momentum with deliberate progress."

## Chapter 6: Life Stages of a Brand (the framework that built the $100M brand)

> "Startups are a bit like children... The key characteristics for success in one stage can cause fatal errors in another."

### Stage 1: Product Creation and Launch

**The origin scene — "lightning in a bottle."** Benjamin Franklin, June 10, 1752, kite in a thunderstorm.

**James Brennan** — restaurant entrepreneur and prior deal partner, a Gladwell "connector" from *The Tipping Point* — kept bringing 16-oz glass bottles of dark green juice into Jeff's office in his distinct New York accent: *"Dude, you've got to try this green juice; I promise it will change your life."*

Jeff's pre-Suja definition of health: daily milk, Raisin Bran, finishing every meal, regular exercise, staying under 225 pounds. *"I've always preferred sugar over protein, so the notion of consuming dark green juice was akin to an exotic and scary adventure."*

He relented. **Three ingredients — apple, kale, lemon — and it tasted amazing.** That juice became Suja's MVP, later named **Green Supreme**.

To Linda: *"Honey, if I, with my unrefined Midwestern palate, can love a product with kale in it, then anyone on the planet can!"*

**The creators:** **Eric Ethans** and **Bryan Riblett** — longtime friends who had developed ten cold-pressed juices running one of the early raw food restaurants in **Encinitas, California**, under the company **Elevated Nutrients**. When the restaurant closed, Eric hand-bottled the juices and **delivered them door-to-door on his skateboard.** Customers loved him; they loved the product more. **Anne Lawless** (who later founded the beauty brand Lawless) had been brought in for organization and forward motion. Bryan had culinary training. What they lacked was scalable management infrastructure — early twenties, long on talent, short on operating experience.

> "A great product does not equal a great business."
> "Opportunity often exists on the edge of chaos."
> "That's Stage 1 in its purest form: creation before professional execution."

**Stage 1 is about:** living in creative chaos · scrappy execution · finding the MVP · testing whether anyone actually cares · being willing to strap the entire team on your back and take the hill.

**Suja's MVP had to be:** a cold-pressed juice that tasted incredible · healthy with no added sugar · something Jeff would personally reorder and others already bought regularly · a business that works in the heartland, not just the coasts.

**What was missing: commercial viability.** The original model was a three-day juice cleanse with a **five-day shelf life.**

> "The MVP must include more than product appeal. You'll need production, logistics, safety, and shelf life to make it profitable and scalable."

**Buzz beats marketing at Stage 1.** *"Marketing and Public Relations at Stage 1 is largely a waste. It's actually buzz, not marketing, that drives Stage 1."* Of Jeff's three failures, **one failed because he didn't have enough connectors like James to create early buzz.**

**The cautionary tale:** they ran **billboards in Manhattan while they had only four Whole Foods stores in the entire city.** *"We created awareness with nowhere to convert it."*

**The connector chain that made Suja possible:**
- Prior venture **Nika Water** taught him bottles, labels, co-packers, regional buying teams — and introduced him to **Jessica Pratt**, a natural foods broker aligned with Whole Foods. (Nika didn't succeed financially but was the education.)
- **May 2012**, after a dinner at **Jimbo's Natural Foods**, Jeff mentioned he was working on a cold-pressed juice brand. Jessica and her husband made green juice at home daily — she asked if he had any idea how hard it is to make it taste good without adding sugar. *"Her reaction told me something important: what Eric had created was rare."*
- Jessica introduced **Diane Snyder** and **Chris Giuliani** inside Whole Foods. At the time **Blueprint Cleanse** was the only similar brand on shelf. Chris believed the segment was about to expand and saw Suja **growing the category, not replacing a brand.**
- *"Neither Diane nor Chris ever received financial benefit from Suja. They helped because they believed in the product and they are foragers at heart."*
- Brokers earn ~**5% of sales** and are worth it.

**Branding vs. marketing — the naming story.** Working with design agency **Bex Brands**, they got approval from Whole Foods for **50 Southern California stores** — and still had no brand name. The front-runner was **Hebe Juice** (Greek goddess of youth), but "Hebe" is a derogatory term in some circles and Jeff was deeply uncomfortable. While they wrestled with it, **Eric went to Whole Foods for lunch, sat next to a young woman, asked her name — "Suja" — and she told him it meant "a long and beautiful life" in ancient Hindu.** He came to Jeff right after lunch. *"Sometimes, Stage 1 decisions are messy. Sometimes they're serendipitous. In our case, they were both."*

**Stage 1 must answer three questions:** Does anyone care? Will they buy it? Will they buy it again?

Jeff spent **2–3 hours every day studying the Whole Foods portal data by store.** *"It wasn't enough to get into stores. The product had to move."*

**Common Stage 1 mistakes:** expanding door count before proof of concept · mistaking early enthusiasm for durable demand · ignoring unit economics.

**Milestone: September 2012** — six months after tasting that first green juice — Suja launched in 50 Whole Foods stores.

### Stage 2: Proof of Concept

> "Stage 2 is where optimism and reality collide."
> "Stage 1 proves you can launch. Stage 2 proves you deserve to grow."

**The signal they had something:** the team started **hoarding bottles in the office refrigerator and writing their initials on the caps** so no one else would drink them. *"We joked that there must be something addictive in every bottle. That joke led to one of the most important insights of my career: health without the punishment is not only possible, but marketable."*

**The bell curve insight:** one end is pure indulgence, the other prioritizes function over taste. *"The real opportunity is in the middle, where we all want to be a bit healthier, but we don't want to be bludgeoned by a bad taste."* That **middle 85%** wants to "move to the right" but won't sacrifice taste.

**Repeat rate thresholds:** *"Most brands fail because repeat purchases never exceed 20%. We targeted 40–50% repeat rates. If less than 20% of first-time consumers don't rebuy within 12 months, you don't have proof of concept. You simply have churn."*

**Rowdy Energy as cautionary tale:** expanded widely and quickly, spread limited marketing dollars too thin, couldn't meet velocity thresholds to hold shelf.

**The HPP breakthrough.** Refrigerated shipping + short shelf life crushed the margin on the 3-day cleanse model. They needed **60+ days** for national distribution; best effort got them to **24 days** — "long enough to reach the Rockies." Then one day at **Jimbo's**, Jeff noticed an **Evolution Fresh** bottle mentioning **High Pressure Processing**. HPP uses pressure instead of heat to eliminate microorganisms while preserving taste and nutrition. It **extended shelf life beyond sixty days** and added cost. *"It made nationwide distribution possible."*

**"Buy local" in Southern California was a strategic advantage, not luck.**

### My $1M Lesson (the spoilage story)
Because shelf life was short during market testing, distributors were hesitant to order aggressively. They offered to lean in **if Suja took on the spoilage risk.** Suja trusted them and agreed. **Ninety days later: over $1 million in spoilage deductions.** Revenue was rising, cash was bleeding, and they had to run an **unplanned $1M fundraising round.**

Lessons: *Proof of concept is fragile. Gross margins matter. Contracts matter. Trust must be documented.*
> "Stage 2 reality: the cost of mistakes multiplies. Growth without discipline is dangerous."

**Attrition stat:** of every 5,000 brands that launch, roughly **7% make it to Phase 2** — nearly 4,700 left behind.

**Stage 2 premature-acceleration temptations:** adding SKUs before a hero product is proven · expanding geography before ops are tight · expanding distribution ahead of velocity thresholds · hiring ahead of revenue · prioritizing distribution over velocity.

**What matters in Stage 2:** retail velocity (USW) · repeat rate · margin discipline · operational reliability.
**Goal of Stage 2:** customers return, retailers profit, margins hold, operations scale.

### Stage 3: The Pain of Growth

> "The skills that built the company are no longer the skills that grow it."
> "Founders shift from doing to designing."
> "And this is where you find out if you actually love building, or if you just love creating and launching."
> "This is where scaling stops being romantic and starts being real."

**Organizational evolution isn't optional.** Early OG team members either specialized, stepped into leadership, or moved on. *"The team that launches a brand is rarely the team that scales it."* Blending scrappy builders with experienced operators guarantees friction: *"Builders move fast. Operators want structure. Both are right."*

> "One misaligned hire. One unclear reporting construct. One leadership decision you delay because it's uncomfortable. That's all it takes to slow momentum."

**Systems over story — syndicated data.** Retailers share raw POS data (every checkout beep) with syndicators (**NIQ/Nielsen, Circana/IRI, SPINS**) who clean, categorize, aggregate, and sell it back. **Target the 50th percentile velocity at minimum.**

> "You're no longer pitching passion. You're presenting competence, performance, reliability, and credibility."
> "If your SKU turns five units per store per week and the competitor turns two, now you have leverage. If you're both turning at the same rate, you don't."
> "Data doesn't replace storytelling. It sharpens it."

**Repeat rate reveals the truth.** *"You can spend aggressively on customer acquisition, but if customers don't come back, you're lighting money on fire."*
> "This is where enthusiasm meets math. And math always wins."

**Stage 3 pitfalls:** rising revenue with tightening cash flow · repeat rates masked by marketing spend · premature senior hires without structural support · blurred accountability · missed velocity targets · category commoditization through over-promotion · supply chain strain · builder/operator cultural friction · decision fatigue · expanding distribution before core SKUs are defensible.

> "Stage 3 often feels like a false summit. Growth reveals weaknesses you hoped would fix themselves. Instead, they compound."
> "Discipline over hustle. Precision over passion. Specialists over generalists."
> "A lot of brands look impressive in Stage 2. Stage 3 is where real companies are built."

### Stage 4: Scaling to Win

> "Stage 4 is where a business is no longer fragile, but it's also no longer flexible."
> "Good decisions compound. Bad decisions compound faster."
> "You are no longer trying to prove you belong on the shelf. You are trying to win the shelf."

**Graduation rate: roughly 1 in 5,000 brands.** (Many are acquired before completing Phase 4.)

**Defend the core while expanding the edge.** Every scalable brand needs a **hero SKU** driving velocity, recognition, and repeat. *"Not every idea deserves oxygen. Not every trend deserves a launch."*

**Operational discipline becomes the strategy:** supply chain determines margin · manufacturing determines scalability · shelf life determines reach. HPP went from a Stage 2 breakthrough to a **Stage 4 competitive weapon.**

**Stage 4 economics benchmarks:**
- Top-line **$100M–$200M**; gross margin **≥45%**; ~$30M of operating expense coverage supports brand-building.
- For a **$200–250M exit: 15–20% EBITDA margin** plus **doubling revenue every 18–24 months** supports aggressive **4.0x exit multiples.**
- Many large multinationals won't acquire until revenue exceeds **$200M**. Getting there puts you in billion-dollar-exit conversations.
- **The trap:** if you don't do a deal at that level, you can become *"too large for anyone to acquire you."* **Vita Coco** hit exactly this from 2010–2020 and went public instead.

**Stage 4 pitfalls:** overexpansion diluting focus · innovation distracting from the hero SKU · margin compression masked by top-line growth · operational complexity slowing decisions · cultural drift toward corporate · dependence on one partner or channel · board misalignment on risk · scaling distribution faster than brand equity · losing the entrepreneurial edge.

> "The market does not reward past performance. It rewards current execution."
> "If Stage 3 builds durability, Stage 4 builds leverage."
> "Stage 4 is where the business becomes bigger than the founder, which is both empowering and humbling."

## Chapter 7: Fundraising

**The scene:** on the couch watching football with his oldest son, Josh, who asked how many fundraising rounds Jeff had led. He'd never counted. It took **45 minutes to reconstruct from memory** — and the number surprised him.

**The numbers:** **44 fundraising rounds** over 30+ years. **$200M+ raised** from friends and family, angels, PE, and VC. **At Suja alone: eleven rounds in seven years**, twice in several of those years.

> "One of the biggest reasons I joke that every year at Suja felt like seven normal working years is largely because of fundraising."
> "In CPG, growth consumes capital before it produces it."

**Suja's early CAGR was roughly 200%.**

### The tell — winning vs. struggling rounds
**Winning rounds have:** a credible lead angel or fund willing to anchor · strong or near-profitable performance · a category with visible growth and velocity · multiple believable expansion paths · momentum that makes investors feel competitive · tailwinds.
**Struggling rounds lack:** a committed lead · a clear investment narrative · consistent operating performance · enough runway to run the process calmly · and face headwinds.

> "Most founders don't fail because their idea is bad. They fail because they run out of cash, time, leverage, or credibility mid-raise."

### The manufacturing tradeoff: speed vs. dilution
Suja **built and owned its own HPP manufacturing** instead of using co-packers. Of the **$110M raised in the first seven years, roughly $60M went into manufacturing equipment and facilities**; the other **$50M** funded brand growth, operating losses, and team.

> "Had we outsourced manufacturing, we likely could have built the brand with closer to $50 million total. That difference would have preserved roughly twenty percent more ownership across the board."

But: **there were no co-packers for cold-pressed juice at the time**, and their growth would have exceeded regional HPP tolling availability. They were forced into it — and got speed, flexibility, and control in exchange.

> "Every capital decision permanently locks in consequences around speed, control, and dilution. Founders need to understand those tradeoffs before they sign the term sheet, not after."

### The psychology of asking
> "I am not naturally comfortable asking for money. Rejection never gets easy. But I've learned that you don't get what you don't ask for."
> "Investors don't want to feel pressured. They want to feel like they discovered something."
> "Enthusiasm attracts. Desperation repels."

### Capital doesn't freeze competition
When Coca-Cola invested, people assumed Suja was protected. **They weren't.** **Campbell's and Pepsi both immediately entered cold-pressed juice** — built internal brands and aggressively bought shelf. Simultaneously, a **$15 million manufacturing overrun** on the 200,000-sq-ft Oceanside, CA plant **prevented deploying $10 million of marketing funds** set aside from the Coke investment, during a critical trial-and-repeat window.

> "Capital does not freeze the competitive landscape. In many cases, it accelerates it. Raising money buys you time and options, but not immunity."

### Jeff's 8-Step Fundraising Process (most raises took 3–4 months)
1. **Raise for at least an 18-month runway.** (His career average has been closer to 12–15.) Keep **average round dilution 15–20%.**
2. **Assess investor fit realistically.** Desire ≠ capacity. Be careful with friends and family — *"I learned this the hard way, and actually paid loved ones back their investment when I had a failed business."*
3. **Soft-circle at least 25% of the round before you launch.** Without it you cobble together dozens of small checks and multiply time, complexity, and distraction.
4. **Build a clean deck and disciplined model.** Under 25 slides; bottom-up five-year model; **lead with the deck, not the model.**
5. **Choose the right entity and security.** C-Corp for venture-scale; **QSBS** benefits; understand common vs. preferred vs. convertible notes vs. SAFEs. *"In most early-stage situations, pre-money SAFEs offer the cleanest path forward."*
6. **Set valuation with realism, not ego.** Pre-revenue **$1M–$5M pre-money**; early retail brands **$4M–$10M**; emerging CPG with **$3–20M revenue** and strong growth trade at **3–4x trailing twelve-month revenue.** Overreaching invites a down round.
7. **Execute disciplined outreach.** Build the funnel from school, work, and industry networks. When someone commits, ask for intros — **"clustering."** Make sure every serious investor has tried the product.
8. **Close cleanly.** Build a real data room. *"Most early-stage companies skip this step — which makes doing it well a real differentiator."*

> "Chaos in your raise signals chaos in your business."
> "Fundraising is rarely about convincing investors how brilliant you or your product is. It's about showing them you're prepared, disciplined, and capable of building something that lasts."

### What today's institutional investors expect
$3–5M+ trailing 12-month net revenue · gross margins above 40% with a path to 50% · shelf velocity above the median · a category with meaningful size · a credible path to break-even · a strong team with prior CPG success.

> "The days of funding pure story without fundamentals are largely behind us."
> "Gross margins create freedom. Runway creates leverage. Lead investors create momentum."
> "Fundraising is not proof that you're winning. It's table stakes to stay in the game."
> "Capital amplifies whatever foundation you've built."

## Chapter 8: The X-Factor of Culture and People

> "Culture determines whether those advantages hold together when the pressure hits."
> "As a founder, you are the chief culture architect. If the environment becomes toxic or misaligned, you don't get to blame the market for culture. You have to look in the mirror."

**By design or by default.** Starting Suja was a blank canvas: **quality first, no job too small at all levels of leadership, protect the customer, work hard and support one another.** By contrast, **Rowdy Energy** — an established company with a dispersed, virtual team — made building a cohesive health-and-wellness culture far harder. *"If you don't deliberately design your culture, one will emerge by default that might not be the one you want."*

**Where Suja's culture actually came from:** countless hours at **Kinko's** printing retail pitch decks; **hauling heavy refrigerated juice cases down darkly lit city streets before the hustle of the day begins**; long days and longer nights. *"That camaraderie, born from shared struggle, is culture."*

**Jeff's personal roots:** the golden rule, plus **Tikkun Olam** — *"he who saves one life, saves the world"* — the responsibility to actively participate in making the world better.

### Four key ingredients of a winning culture
1. **Empowerment with Accountability.** *"Micromanagement is fear disguised as leadership."*
2. **Flexibility Without Excuses.** *"Adjust the path, not the principles."*
3. **Desire to Win.** Competitive, not cutthroat. *"Winning becomes collective, not personal."*
4. **Significance.** *"At Suja, we weren't just selling juice. We believed we were expanding access to organic products and healthier choices."*

Even as CEO, Jeff routinely fielded customer feedback and handed out samples at demos. *"The philosophy that nobody is above certain jobs starts at the top."*

### Hiring is culture creation
> Mom's line: **"Show me your friends, and I'll show you who you are."** In business: *"Show me your team, and I'll show you what your company is about."*

**Three hiring mistakes Jeff made repeatedly:**
1. **Chasing star resumes over culture fit.** Hired a Fortune 500 executive with a flawless resume who couldn't adapt to a scrappy environment. *"Today, I would choose a values-aligned builder over a mismatched superstar every time."*
2. **Hiring in a hurry.** Rushed a hire before an important meeting, ignored his instincts, skipped an assessment step. *"A rushed hire creates more work than an empty seat."*
3. **Waiting too long to act.** A senior hire delivered results but damaged culture; Jeff told himself performance outweighed behavior. It didn't.

**Interview scorecards** — written *before* interviewing anyone. Two categories: **Technical Competence** ("table stakes" — what they must do on day one, in practice not theory) and **Cultural/Character Traits** (how they behave under pressure, ownership, operating with limited resources, mission alignment).
> "A scorecard is simply an answer key for what 'great' looks like in that role. It forces you to get clear before you get impressed."

**Ask for stories, not opinions.** *"Resumes are rehearsed. Opinions are polished. Stories are revealing."*
- "Tell me about a time you had to improvise with limited resources."
- "Describe the best culture you've worked in. What made it work?"
- "When did you receive tough feedback, and what did you do next?"

> "A+ hires tend to speak in specifics. They own their mistakes, credit their teams, and don't dodge accountability."

**Win A+ talent with ownership.** *"Equity is not just compensation. It is cultural reinforcement."* When someone thinks like an owner, they protect the margin, care about customer experience, and take fewer shortcuts.

### Cul-de-sac Management
Picture a cul-de-sac: input flows in from every direction — sales, ops, marketing, finance. **In the center, one person is accountable for the final decision.**
> "This is not consensus management. It is informed decision-making."
> "I realized how much smarter *we* are than *I* am."

Push it as far down the org chart as possible. Broad input, clear ownership. Prevents paralysis without top-down edicts or endless debate.

### Culture Win / Culture Misfire
**Win:** they flagged a production issue caused by a supplier error. They could have quietly shipped the batch and protected short-term revenue. Instead they **halted distribution and absorbed the loss.** Weeks later a competitor faced a public recall — and retailers remembered how Suja had handled its own. *"Trust compounds. So does integrity."*

**Misfire:** a senior executive who looked exceptional on paper disrupted the collaborative spirit; used to layers of support and hierarchy. Morale dipped, good people disengaged, Jeff hesitated hoping it would resolve itself. It didn't. *"When we finally made a change, the organization breathed again."*

### Take the work seriously, but not yourself
**The beet juice story.** Suja's first delivery to a Whole Foods distribution center: Jeff maneuvering a tiny 28-footer among a line of **53-foot semis** driven by far more experienced people. Then he tried to unload pallets with a forklift he had no business operating, **sliced into a pallet, and watched beet juice spill everywhere.**
> "I had to laugh at my complete failure to look like a credible CEO that day, but that scenario did more for our culture than any inspiring speech ever could have."
> "When the CEO can make a fool of himself and laugh about it, the rest of the team is more likely to try things without fear of making a mistake."
> "Humility builds trust. Humor preserves stamina."

### Closing lines
> "If you tolerate ego, it spreads. If you reward ownership, that spreads too. If you protect integrity when it costs you something, your team will remember."
> "Culture compounds just like capital. The difference is that it compounds quietly."
> "You can fix a marketing plan. You can renegotiate terms. You can reformulate a product. Culture is harder to repair once it fractures."
> "Anyone can copy your product, but no one can copy your people and how you bring them together. That is the ultimate X-factor."

**Culture Audit — ten questions**, including the gut check: **"Would I want to work for me/us?"**

## Chapter 9: Ten Startup Misconceptions That Can Crack Your Foundation

1. **"Most startups fail in their first year."** Not accurate — only about **21.5%** fail that quickly. About two-thirds fail over ten years, and much can be done in that window.
2. **"I don't have the personality to be a founder."** Less about personality, more about learnable behaviors, strategies, and tactics.
3. **"We're growing fast, we must be doing great!"** Founders confuse **distribution growth with durability.** More stores looks impressive; real traction is **each store selling more per week.**
4. **"Without prior CPG startup experience, I shouldn't be the primary driver of a CPG brand."** Hire against the startup's immediate needs, which evolve.
5. **"I need the vision and intelligence of Bill Gates or Steve Jobs."** *"Chutzpah, or the courage to take risks, often plays a far more significant role than raw intellect."*
6. **"Avoid getting family, friends, and colleagues to invest."** *"If you take this approach, you may never get out of the starting blocks."* Respect their decision either way — but offering them the opportunity is a must.
7. **"I must be first in my market to be disruptive."** Being third or fourth lets you learn from predecessors. *"You just don't want to be 61st."* **One reason Rowdy Energy failed was entering roughly 100th in the energy drink market.**
8. **"My personal life must be perfectly in order before starting."** There may never be a perfect time. *"Manufacturing deposits, packaging minimums, distributor terms — the burn rate doesn't wait for you to feel ready."*
9. **"Industry mentors and experienced CPG professionals provide equally valuable advice."** *"There is a lot of bad advice out there, and much of it comes from people who claim to be CPG experts."* Understand their specific roles and the context of their wins and losses.
10. **"If they've held the title at a larger company, they'll do great here."** An emerging brand is at a different life stage at a fraction of the size.
