The retrieval layer for content. Every entry is a **usable unit** — a story with a scene, a quote with a punch, or a number you can put in a headline. Sourced from *The Cold-Pressed Truth* (8.2.26 manuscript). Chapter refs in brackets.

Use with `jeff_voice_profile.md` (repo root) for voice. Deeper context lives in `cpt-part1/2/3-*.md`; book/jacket/cover facts live in `cpt-00-book-facts.md`.

## TIER 1 — The Signature Stories
Load-bearing. These carry a keynote, a book cover concept, a launch campaign.

### 1. Three Seconds in the End Zone [Ch. 1]
Wide open in the end zone, senior year, team hasn't won a game in two seasons, seconds left. He doesn't raise his hand. Fear of dropping it, of letting the team down, of being blamed. QB gets sacked. They lose. **He replays it for twenty years.** At 38, third child just born, he's at 2 a.m. flipping channels and lands on retired CEOs being asked what they'd change. Every one of them says: bolder, more chances, fulfillment over safety. He raises his hand and leaves the safe track.
**Payoff line:** *"At 38 years of age, I was now far more afraid of mediocrity than of failure."*
**Second payoff:** *"Courage doesn't eliminate fear. Sometimes it simply refuses to let fear make the final decision."*
**Use for:** origin, keynote open, book intro, any "why now" argument, LinkedIn's highest-performing post shape.

### 2. The July 3rd Phone Call [Ch. 1, 16]
5:00 p.m., July 3, 2018. House full of family prepping for the Fourth. Scott Uzzell of Coca-Cola VEB calls to say Coke is not acquiring the rest of Suja. **Jeff hangs up, walks downstairs, and weeps in front of his sons.** Not thinking about awards or growth rates — thinking *"How did I let us get to this place?"* Next morning: fireworks, a parade, blue skies, eighty-two degrees, and he's sick to his stomach. For a split second he wonders whether it was a nightmare.
**Behind it:** $40M secured debt due in October. $10M+/yr burn. Gross margins under 32%. Weeks with less than $100,000 in the bank.
**Use for:** the book's cold open, any "growth ≠ survival" argument, resilience content.

### 3. Ringing the Bell Without Him [Epilogue]
May 2026. Suja Life goes public on Nasdaq under ticker SUJA at a valuation **north of $700 million.** Ringing the bell had been a lifelong dream. **He asked to attend. Paine Schwartz, the current owners, cited "policy" and declined.** He watched from a screen in San Diego as the team he once led raised their hands above their heads in Times Square.
**And he writes about gratitude instead of grievance.**
**Payoff line:** *"Build the company, but don't be the company. Love the work, but don't be the work."*
**Second payoff:** *"The bell is a moment. The work is the point... Both outcomes are honorable if the work was honest."*
**Use for:** the closing argument of everything. Highest-ceiling story in the whole bank.

### 4. The Tuesday Decision [Epilogue]
Eighteen months before Coke walked, almost as if by instinct, they launched two-ounce wellness shots — ~60% gross margin vs. ~12% on outsourced kombucha. At the time it felt like an experiment. It was the thing that saved the company.
> **"The decision that saves you is almost never the one you made on the day you needed saving. It's usually one you made eighteen months earlier, on a Tuesday, when nobody was watching."**
**Use for:** the single most quotable line in the manuscript. Blurb, cover band, ad copy, email subject.

### 5. Babu on Kilimanjaro [Preface, Ch. 23]
Climbing Kilimanjaro with his kids. Around day three, oxygen thin and legs burning, the local guides start calling him **Babu** — Swahili for grandpa — partly for his slow pace, partly because he kept offering unsolicited advice on the trail. The name stuck. His grandkids call him Babu now.
> *"Somewhere between the mountain and the boardroom, I realized that's exactly the role I've always played: the experienced guy walking alongside you, pointing out what lies ahead."*
> *"Someone who walks ahead, pointing out the cliffs, the switchbacks, and the places where the view is worth the climb."*
**Use for:** the brand persona itself. askBabu.ai. Author positioning.

### 6. The Rehab Month [Ch. 19]
Running a "better-for-you" beverage company while neglecting his own health. Eighty-hour weeks for nearly eight years. Completely burned out and drinking far too much. **After leaving Suja, he spent a month in rehab to reset his life and health.** Linda and his family told him they believed that if he hadn't stepped away when he did, he might not still be here.
> *"Had I managed the balance better, I might still be the CEO today, and preparing to take the company public. That lost opportunity still sits somewhere in the back of my mind."*
**Use for:** the credibility anchor for everything anti-hustle-porn. Handle with care — this is the most exposed thing in the book and should never be used as a hook without the substance behind it.

### 7. Pausing a Coca-Cola Deal to Do the Right Thing [Ch. 14]
In the **final stages** of the deal with the world's largest beverage company, they identify a potential quality issue. **He presses pause.**
> **"There is no wrong time to do the right thing."**
> *"Slowing a deal of that magnitude takes nerve. What it earned us was something far more valuable than speed. We earned the respect of Coca-Cola's senior leadership. Integrity under pressure is visible. People remember it."*
**Use for:** integrity content, values-under-pressure, culture posts. Rare in that it's a *values* story with a *deal* stake.

### 8. Fighting for the Angels [Ch. 14]
Earlier financing gave certain investors first-money-out preference. Coke's $90M would have largely gone to **ACG** — a PE investor in the deal **less than 12 months** — bypassing the angels who'd built the company over three years. It was ACG's contractual right. Jeff fought anyway, found **Goldman Sachs to put in $60 million**, and the angels sold ~40% of their holdings at **3.4x while retaining 60%.**
**They eventually returned roughly 10x.**
> *"Deals are not just about valuations. They are about fairness and morale."*
**Use for:** founder integrity, investor-relations content, the "who you protect when you have leverage" theme.

## TIER 2 — The Working Stories
Great for a post, a newsletter section, a talk anecdote.

### The Skateboard and the Ice Closet [Ch. 6, Epilogue]
Eric Ethans and Bryan Riblett developed ten cold-pressed juices running an early raw-food restaurant in Encinitas. When it closed, **Eric hand-bottled the juices and delivered them door-to-door on his skateboard.** Customers loved him. They loved the product more. The company was founded **in the ice closet of his partner's nightclub.** *(Note: the ice closet appears only in the Epilogue — see the pre-print flag in `cpt-00-book-facts.md`.)*

### "Dude, you've got to try this green juice" [Ch. 6]
James Brennan, a Gladwell "connector," kept bringing 16-oz bottles of dark green juice into Jeff's office in his New York accent. Jeff's definition of health at the time: daily milk, Raisin Bran, finishing every meal, staying under 225 pounds. *"I've always preferred sugar over protein, so the notion of consuming dark green juice was akin to an exotic and scary adventure."* He relented to end the pestering. **Three ingredients — apple, kale, lemon — and it tasted amazing.** That became Green Supreme.
**To Linda that night:** *"Honey, if I, with my unrefined Midwestern palate, can love a product with kale in it, then anyone on the planet can!"*

### How Suja Got Its Name [Ch. 6]
Down to the wire with 50 Whole Foods stores approved and no brand name. The front-runner was **Hebe Juice** (Greek goddess of youth) — but "Hebe" is a slur in some circles and Jeff was deeply uncomfortable. **While they wrestled with it, Eric went to Whole Foods for lunch, sat next to a young woman, asked her name — "Suja" — and she told him it meant "a long and beautiful life" in ancient Hindu.** He came to Jeff straight from lunch.
> *"Sometimes, Stage 1 decisions are messy. Sometimes they're serendipitous. In our case, they were both."*

### The Beet Juice Forklift [Ch. 8]
Suja's first delivery to a Whole Foods DC. Jeff maneuvering a tiny 28-footer among a line of 53-foot semis driven by people who knew what they were doing. Then he tries to unload pallets with a forklift he has no business operating, **slices into a pallet, and watches beet juice spill everywhere.**
> *"That scenario did more for our culture than any inspiring speech ever could have."*
> *"When the CEO can make a fool of himself and laugh about it, the rest of the team is more likely to try things without fear of making a mistake."*

### The 2 a.m. Transformer [Ch. 10]
Power goes out mid-production run at 2 a.m. Transformer's on the roof; only the landlord has a key and he's asleep at home. **The production crew climbs a tree next to the building, jumps from a branch onto the roof, and reconnects the circuit.** Orders ship on time. *"Our customers never knew what it took."*
> *"The team knew they wouldn't get in trouble for trying, even if their plan hadn't ended so well."*

### "What's a cat man?" [Ch. 12]
After a brutal fight for shelf space, Jeff tells head of sales Nicky: *"I can't wait until a couple of years from now when we achieve full distribution and have nothing to worry about!"* Nicky, aggressively: *"Two years from now, we'll be fighting tooth and nail to maintain our shelf space. We'll need the right cat man to win."* Jeff: **"What's a cat man?"** — which triggered relentless teasing from his experienced team.
> *"When you're the proverbial king of the hill, you transition from the hunter to the hunted."*

### "What's a HACCP plan?" [Ch. 3]
A Whole Foods buyer asks if they have a HACCP plan. Jeff stares like he's speaking another language and assumes it's a one-page form. Within a week he's in an all-day food safety course learning how many ways a beverage company can unintentionally hurt people.
> *"Ignorance is expensive in any business, but especially in the CPG industry."*

### The $1 Million Spoilage Bill [Ch. 6]
Short shelf life made distributors hesitant to order. They offered to lean in **if Suja took the spoilage risk.** Suja trusted them and agreed. **Ninety days later: over $1 million in spoilage deductions** — and an unplanned $1M emergency raise.
> *"Proof of concept is fragile. Gross margins matter. Contracts matter. Trust must be documented."*

### Billboards in Manhattan with Four Stores [Ch. 6]
They ran billboards in Manhattan while they had **four Whole Foods stores in the entire city.**
> *"We created awareness with nowhere to convert it."*

### The Fortune Teller [Ch. 17]
2014. Linda visits a fortune teller named Tara — usually about the kids. This time Tara says **the product that will ultimately create the most value for the company is wellness shots.** They hadn't launched any; Bryan had barely started experimenting. Five years later the shots are what save the company.
> *"As it turns out, the fortune teller was right."*

### 62 Brands Wanted a Meeting [Ch. 13]
Early on there were three national cold-pressed competitors: Evolution Fresh, Blueprint Cleanse, Suja. **Six or seven years later, a Whole Foods buyer told them she had 62 cold-pressed juice brands requesting meetings.** She couldn't possibly see them all.

### The Oversized Bed Photo [Ch. 15]
Instead of a plant tour and conference rooms, Suja invited the Target team to **stay overnight and eat a home-cooked meal by Linda.** After cocktails and laughs, Jeff gave a house tour; because one room has an oversized bed, someone suggested they all pile in for a photo. *"It was harmless fun (and yes, my wife was right there with us)."* Years later, pitching a protein soda line, he reconnected with the same buyer — the trust opened the door.
> *"It's hard to build real trust through Zoom calls and formal meetings alone."*

### Bill Wieland and the Nutcracker [Ch. 15]
Suja's Whole Foods launch wouldn't have happened without Bill Wieland at Presence Marketing. **At one Christmas party, after a few cocktails, Bill somehow left with Suja's Nutcracker decoration.** *"The memory of it still makes me laugh."*

### The Presence Employee's Kids [Ch. 19]
Bill Weiland created a program letting Presence employees invest a portion of their retirement allocations in Suja. Jeff was nervous about it. **After the sale, a mid-level Presence employee found him at a trade show and thanked him profusely — the money she made put both of her kids through college.**

### The Walmart Smoothie Trade [Ch. 15]
Suja wanted expanded distribution for their 2-oz shots. Walmart hesitated and instead wanted a **Walmart-exclusive plant-based smoothie.** Production hated it — slow, complex, and they didn't believe in it. **They ran it anyway, at unattractive economics, in exchange for expanding the higher-performing SKUs.** Today Walmart is one of Suja's largest customers.
> *"The lesson wasn't about winning every negotiation. It was about understanding leverage, thinking long-term, and making selective concessions to grow the whole relationship."*

### The Costco Head-to-Head (with a Catch) [Ch. 17]
Nicky pitches the wellness shot; the Costco buyer proposes a head-to-head test against the incumbent — **but also wants Suja to develop a plant-based milk for his region.** Nobody at Suja had ever made plant-based milk. **They said yes anyway.** The milk struggled (pea protein tastes like *"stiff, cardboard-like"* flavor). **The shots hit ~$2,000 per club per week** — a $15M annual opportunity — and replaced kombucha on that shelf, improving margins.

### Six Weeks to Celery Juice [Ch. 10, 13]
2019. Celery juice spikes after a boost on *The Rachael Ray Show*. Trader Joe's reaches out. TJ's normally prefers private label but that would take too long, so they do a third-party-branded project. **First call to nationwide shelves: under six weeks.** *(Context: Coca-Cola's Stage-Gate process required 16–18 months.)*

### The Expo East Perfectionists [Ch. 10]
A couple who started a juice company around the same time as Suja. Careful, meticulous, extremely risk-averse. **Years later they were stuck at a fraction of Suja's scale.**
> *"Perfect is the enemy of good" (Voltaire) — "But in startups, it's survival."*

### The Two Brothers [Ch. 19]
Two brothers have both invested in Jeff's ventures over the years. **One has only invested in the winners. The other has only invested in the losers.**
> *"One of the reasons I wrote this book is to help founders avoid some of the quicksand traps I've encountered — the kind that impacted one of those brothers far differently than the other."*

### Bottles With Initials on the Caps [Ch. 6]
The proof-of-concept signal: the team started **hoarding bottles in the office fridge and writing their initials on the caps** so nobody else would drink them. *"We joked that there must be something addictive in every bottle."* That joke produced the mantra: **health without the punishment.**

### The Rainier Promise [Ch. 23]
Thirty-plus years ago, flying into Seattle at night, a full moon lit the 14,411-ft summit of Mt. Rainier. He promised himself he'd stand on it. **Less than a year later, he did.** That first climb started a thirty-year mountaineering life across all seven continents.

### The Amazon Piranhas [Ch. 23]
Flying from São Paulo, he saw the brackish convergence where the Amazon's saltwater meets fresh and decided he'd catch piranhas there. **In less than a year he was on the banks catching piranhas in the dark.** *"The detail I hadn't factored in was the monkey on my back. And I'm not talking about a figurative monkey. But at least he seemed friendly."*

### The Dock and the Ice-Cold Coke [Ch. 14]
The first warm days of summer, painting the dock at the family lake house in Western New York with his father, then swimming and boating all season. *"Those days were marked by hard work, time with my dad, and an ice-cold Coke at the end of it all."* — the setup for the Coca-Cola chapters.

### Kinko's and Dark City Streets [Ch. 8]
Where Suja's culture actually came from: countless hours at Kinko's printing retail pitch decks, and **hauling heavy refrigerated juice cases down darkly lit city streets before the hustle of the day begins.**
> *"That camaraderie, born from shared struggle, is culture."*
