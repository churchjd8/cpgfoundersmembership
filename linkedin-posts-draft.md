## Post 1: The Personal Guarantee

He was about to sign the deal. Big co-manufacturing agreement... better pricing, more capacity, the kind of thing that unlocks your next phase of growth. He'd been working toward it for months.

Then he called me.

I asked one question. "Is there a personal guarantee in there?"

Silence. He hadn't even looked.

I told him to pull it up. Page 14, buried in the boilerplate. There it was. A full personal guarantee. If the business went under, they weren't coming for the company's assets. They were coming for his house. His savings. Everything his family had.

He walked away from the deal. I was very happy he did.

I see this all the time. Founders are so focused on getting the deal done that they skip right past the one clause that could destroy them personally. And the vendors and landlords will always tell you it's "standard." It's not standard. It's just what they prefer.

I've built 8 companies over nearly 40 years. Raised over $200 million. Signed more contracts than I can count. And I have never... not once... signed a personal guarantee. I've skirted it for 40 years and you can too.

Push back. Offer a larger security deposit. Offer prepayment for the first few months. Offer a shorter contract. There is always another way. And if a vendor won't budge on that? There is always another vendor.

Your family's financial security is not a negotiating chip. Period.

## Post 2: The $200K Pile of Inventory

She was so proud of what she'd negotiated. I didn't want to ruin her excitement.

Her co-manufacturer gave her a 15% discount for ordering 50,000 units instead of 10,000. She walked me through the math on our call, just beaming about it. Nearly $40,000 in savings. For a brand her size that felt like a huge win. And I get it, I really do.

But I had to ask. "What happens if you need to change the formula?"

She paused. "Why would I change the formula?"

Two months later she changed the formula. New recipe, better taste profile, the kind of improvement that should have been a breakthrough for her.

But now she's sitting on 38,000 units of the old version in a warehouse. Expiration dates creeping closer every day. Retailers didn't want the old formula once word got out about the new one. She tried discounting, tried online liquidation... ended up donating most of it.

That $40,000 in "savings" became almost $200,000 in waste.

And look, this is one of the most common mistakes I see with early-stage founders. You're not Coca-Cola (not yet anyway). Your formula is going to change. Your packaging is going to change. Your SKU mix is going to change. Ordering six months of inventory when your product is still evolving is like buying a house in a city you haven't decided to live in yet.

My rule is simple: 60 to 90 days of inventory. Pay the higher per-unit cost. I know it's painful. But at this stage flexibility is worth more than margin. Every single time.

## Post 3: Revenue Was Up 200%. We Were in Trouble.

*[Photo suggestion: Suja on shelf at a major retailer]*

Revenue was up 200%. We were in trouble.

The board was thrilled. The team was celebrating. From the outside, Suja looked like we were on a rocket ship. Everything was pointing up and to the right.

But I couldn't sleep. Because I had done the math that nobody else was doing.

We went from 500 doors to 2,000 doors that quarter. Revenue exploded, sure. But when I looked at our velocity per store per week... it had actually dropped 15%.

We weren't selling more. We were just in more places. Same demand, four times the shelf space. That's not growth. That's just... expensive warehousing with better optics.

And here's the thing about that trap. It's so seductive because the revenue number looks incredible. Your board is happy, your investors are happy, everyone's patting you on the back. But your per-door performance is quietly dying underneath all that noise. And eventually the retailers see it too. When they do, the resets come fast (and they are not kind about it).

From that point on I became obsessed with one metric: revenue per store per week. If that number wasn't growing or at least holding steady as we expanded, we stopped expanding. Fixed the demand side first. Then grew again.

That discipline is a big part of why we got to $100 million in 5 years instead of flaming out at $30 million across 3,000 doors.

If you're scaling right now, ask yourself honestly... is your growth coming from more doors or more pull? Because the answer to that question changes everything.

## Post 4: Your Board Is Not Your Friend

*[Photo suggestion: Jeff in a boardroom or at a Suja company event]*

Your board is not your friend.

I learned this the hard way, sitting across the table from someone I deeply respected at Suja. He suggested we consider selling. Early. Before I believed we were anywhere close to done.

The return would have been "good enough." For them.

But I had spent 3 years sleeping four hours a night building something I believed could be transformational. I could see the path to $100 million. I could feel it. And this board member, who had written a check 18 months earlier, was ready to take a 3x return and move on to the next thing.

And look... I understand it from their side. That's their job. Your board's job is to maximize shareholder value. Your job is to build a great company. Those two things align perfectly... until they don't. And when they diverge, it gets uncomfortable fast.

They are not your friends. They are not your mentors (even if some of them feel like mentors). They are your fiduciary partners. Understanding that difference might be the most important leadership lesson I've ever learned.

Here's how I navigated it. I communicated proactively. I built real relationships with every board member individually so I understood what their ideal outcome looked like. And I made sure I always maintained enough influence... through performance, through voting rights, through sheer force of conviction... to protect the vision when it mattered most.

Suja eventually sold to Coca-Cola. The outcome was dramatically better than that "good enough" offer. But it only happened because I understood the dynamics at play and was prepared for that conversation before it ever came up.

If you have a board right now, or you're about to take on investors... understand this dynamic now. Not after it's too late.

## Post 5: We Didn't Invent Cold-Pressed Juice

*[Photo suggestion: Early Suja product or Suja in production]*

We didn't invent cold-pressed juice.

By the time we launched Suja, there were already cold-pressed brands in every major market. Juice Press in New York, Pressed Juicery in LA, local brands popping up in every health-conscious city in America. The idea wasn't new.

We weren't first. But here's the thing... we also weren't the 51st.

We hit the market at the exact moment when consumer demand for cold-pressed juice was exploding but almost nobody could deliver it at scale with real shelf life and national distribution. That gap between what consumers wanted and what was actually available on shelves? That was our entire business. That gap was everything.

And timing in CPG... it's not luck. Or at least it's not just luck. It's obsessive attention to where consumer behavior is heading, not where it is today. It's understanding that a trend you see at your local farmers market takes 18 to 24 months to show up on a conventional grocery shelf. And it's having the operational readiness to actually move when that window opens, because (and I cannot stress this enough) it does not stay open long.

I talk to founders all the time who are worried they're "too late" to a category. And my answer is almost always the same. You don't have to be first. You have to be the best at the moment the market is ready for scale. First mover advantage in CPG is wildly overrated. Timing advantage is everything.

So the question isn't whether someone else is already doing what you're doing. The question is whether the market has caught up to the idea yet. If there's still a gap between consumer demand and what's actually available on shelves... you might be right on time.
